Overview

Depression Chicago's guts were in worse shape, and stranger, than its skyline. The best primary find is a 1934 University of Chicago study (Max R. White, Water Supply Organization in the Chicago Region, full text on the Internet Archive) that answers the owner's question about "loss of water" directly: the city drank unfiltered, unmetered lake water that was over the federal turbidity limit a third of the time; Chicagoans spent $5 million a year on bottled water; a 1930 referendum for filtration carried and the council ignored it; on a summer evening in 1931 a test found zero water pressure in Elmwood Park; suburbs resold Chicago's water at five times the price (the county poorhouse bought it back at four times); and in the winter of 1932–33 Indiana coke-plant phenol, trapped under the ice, drifted to the intake cribs and ate the chlorine — Indiana's 1927 pollution law expressly exempted Lake Michigan. The second primary find is the city's own 1943 subway booklet: the Loop's subsoil was up to 58 per cent water, streetcars ran on plank decking over the open station pits, and the stations were colour-coded blue, red, green, brown. Wire reports recover a forgotten disaster: eleven men killed by "mysterious" poison smoke in a Sanitary District sewer tunnel 35 ft under the Southwest Side (13–14 April 1931), fifteen saved by a bulkhead. [UNVERIFIED — V_R20] The decade also demolished old Chicago — the first skyscraper (1931) for what proved the last one for twenty years, the Masonic Temple (1939) because of the subway, H. H. Holmes's "Castle" (1938), the Everleigh Club (1933), the Great Northern (1940) — often for one-storey "taxpayers". The subway left sealed voids (the half-dug Dearborn tube, the closed LaSalle Street tunnel, the Post Office's hole for a road not yet built). Around the ring: the Van Sweringens buying back their own empire for $3 million, Foshay's bounced cheque to Sousa, the Fisher Building's sealed flag room, 137 dead on the Welland Canal, and Toledo refusing to name a bridge for President Harding (a clean BEND: Harding was never President in the setting). Power-outage events specific to the 1930s could not be confirmed — Chronicling America rate-limited mid-run (see Leads).

Part 1 — Digging under the Loop: the subways and what they cost

The subway groundbreaking and the engineer who died on the train home — 17–19 Dec 1938 (deepens R09's State Street subway entry)

  • Where: State Street and Chicago Avenue (Near North Side) — the first spadeful; the tunnel then ran south under State Street through the Loop.
  • What happened: On 13 October 1938 President Roosevelt approved a PWA grant of $18,000,000 toward a total cost of $40,000,000 [UNVERIFIED — V_R20] — the Washington Evening Star (14 Oct 1938) wrote that Chicago's subway hopes, "nurtured for 30 years", were "on the threshold of realization". (R09 and Wikipedia say "WPA"; the contemporary report says P.W.A. — Ickes's Public Works Administration, which is right.) Ground was broken on 17 December 1938 at State and Chicago. Among the guests was Robert Ridgway, 76, the New York engineer who had "a part in the construction of every foot of the rapid transit systems of New York" (IRT, the Joralemon Street tunnel under the East River, the northern 60 miles of the Catskill Aqueduct) and who sat on the 1938 board of engineers that studied the Chicago plans. On the train home he suffered a heart attack, was taken off at Fort Wayne, Indiana, and died in Methodist Hospital there on 19 December 1938 — two days after the ceremony. The tube was bored deep enough for tunnelling machines most of the way; only short stretches were cut-and-cover.
  • The odd detail: The man who dug every foot of New York's subways lived to see Chicago's first shovel go in and died on the way home — the dig's first casualty was its most famous engineer.
  • People: Robert Ridgway (1862–1938) — New York subway and aqueduct engineer, consultant to the Chicago board; Harold L. Ickes — PWA administrator (a Chicagoan); Edward J. Kelly — mayor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Ridgway_(engineer) ; https://www.loc.gov/collections/chronicling-america/?q=%22state+street+subway%22&dates=1938/1941&fo=json (Evening Star, 14 Oct 1938, p. 47 snippet) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Street_subway
  • Confidence: High on dates, grant figure and Ridgway's death; the full Evening Star page text could not be opened (snippet only).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (PWA, Ickes, Roosevelt all in office in the setting).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Ridgway rode home beside something he had seen under the first test boring. FBI Section 13 pulled his notebook from his coat at Fort Wayne before the family arrived — and the Secret Service, which had its own man at the ceremony, wants to know why the Bureau got there first.

The Dearborn subway — Ickes's route, 80 per cent dug and then abandoned to the dark — 1938–1951

  • Where: Under Milwaukee Avenue and Dearborn Street, from the Northwest Side into the Loop.
  • What happened: Chicago had asked the PWA for a State Street subway; Harold Ickes, the PWA administrator, pushed for a second line, and the "Dearborn plan" was adopted in 1938 as the price of the money. Work on the Milwaukee–Dearborn tube began in March 1939. When war materials were rationed the federal government let State Street continue but stopped the Dearborn subway in 1942 at about 80–82 per cent complete; the bored tunnels sat finished, unlit and empty until work resumed in December 1945, and the line opened only at midnight on 25 February 1951.
  • The odd detail: For most of a decade Chicago had a nearly finished subway under Dearborn Street that no train ran through — mile after mile of empty tube under the Loop.
  • People: Harold L. Ickes — Secretary of the Interior and PWA chief, who overrode the city's plan.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milwaukee%E2%80%93Dearborn_subway ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_station_(CTA_Logan_Square_branch)
  • Confidence: High (the two pages give 80% and 82%).
  • Divergence check: Dig (1938–39) holds. The 1942 halt BENDS — it was caused by America's entry into the Second World War; in a setting where America stayed out of the Great War and the European order is Germany's, a 1940s halt would need another cause (or the line simply finishes). For a 1930s game the Dearborn tube is a live, half-dug workings from 1939 on.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A half-built subway under the Loop, federally owned, guarded by a watchman and a padlock: the ideal place for a cult's meeting room, or for the Secret Service to keep what it cannot put in a prison.

The Masonic Temple — once the tallest building in Chicago, pulled down for the subway — demolished 1939

  • Where: Northeast corner of State and Randolph streets (the Loop).
  • What happened: Burnham and Root's 21-storey, 302-ft Masonic Temple (1892), also called the Capitol Building, was the tallest building in Chicago from 1895 to 1899. Its central court was ringed by nine floors of shops, with offices above and, at the very top, meeting rooms for the Masons that also served as theatres. Both John Wellborn Root and the Masons' representative Norman Gassette died while it was being built. Its elevators could not cope with its crowds and it fell out of favour; in 1939 it was demolished — poor services, and the new State Street subway would have required expensive foundation retrofitting. A two-storey "taxpayer" with a Walgreens went up in its place.
  • The odd detail: The building's architect and the Masons' own man both died during its construction, and the subway killed it — lodge rooms at the top of Chicago's tallest tower, gone in the year the tunnel came.
  • People: John Wellborn Root — architect, died 1891; Norman T. Gassette — Masonic promoter, died during construction.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masonic_Temple_(Chicago)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 construction; 1939 demolition holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The wreckers took down the lodge rooms floor by floor from the top. Whatever the Masons had sealed into the 20th-floor wall was carried out in a Walgreens delivery truck the week before the Field estate's men arrived.

The first skyscraper is torn down for the last one — Home Insurance Building 1931, Field Building 1931–34

  • Where: LaSalle and Adams streets (the Loop financial district); the Field Building filled the block to Clark St.
  • What happened: William Le Baron Jenney's Home Insurance Building (1884–85; 138 ft, raised to 12 floors in 1891) is the building usually called the world's first skyscraper. In September 1929 Marshall Field's estate announced a large office block spanning Adams, Clark and LaSalle; at least six buildings were cleared and the Home Insurance Building came down in 1931. The Field Building (45 storeys, 535 ft, 1931–34; Graham, Anderson, Probst & White) was the last major office building erected in Chicago until the Prudential in 1955. It had high-speed elevators, air-conditioning, a multi-level arcade from LaSalle to Clark — and no 13th floor. In 1932 a plaque in its lobby credited the demolished building as the first to use skeleton construction.
  • The odd detail: Chicago demolished the first skyscraper to build what turned out to be its last for twenty years, then hung a plaque to the dead building in the new one's lobby.
  • People: William Le Baron Jenney — architect of the Home Insurance Building; the Marshall Field estate — builder.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Insurance_Building ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_Building
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Field Building has no 13th floor on its elevator panel — but the stairwell count does not add up. A floor built in 1934 for tenants who did not want to be listed.

H. H. Holmes's "Castle" is torn down and a post office goes up — 1938 (living memory)

  • Where: W. 63rd Street at S. Wallace Avenue, Englewood (South Side).
  • What happened: Holmes's hotel ("the Murder Castle") was gutted by a fire set by an unknown arsonist shortly after his 1894 arrest, but it was largely rebuilt and stayed in use — including as a post office — until it was torn down in 1938. The Englewood branch of the U.S. Post Office stands on the site.
  • The odd detail: For forty years the building where Holmes killed was an ordinary working building on 63rd Street; it went only in 1938, the same decade the city demolished the Masonic Temple and the first skyscraper.
  • People: H. H. Holmes (Herman Webster Mudgett) — the 1893 fair's murderer, hanged 1896.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._H._Holmes
  • Confidence: High on 1938 demolition and the post-office site; the building's uses between 1895 and 1938 are summarised loosely on the page.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened; demolition holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The 1938 wrecking crew opened the cellar, which had been bricked up since 1895. The federal government owned the lot by then — so the report went to Washington, and to FBI Section 13.

Part 2 — The river, the lake and the Supreme Court

The Chicago Harbor Lock — the gate that keeps the lake out of the river — built 1936–1938

  • Where: The mouth of the Chicago River at Lake Michigan, beside the new Outer Drive Bridge (Streeterville/Grant Park).
  • What happened: After the Supreme Court's diversion decrees (1929–30) limited how much Lake Michigan water Chicago could send down its canal, the Sanitary District of Chicago built a lock and controlling works at the river's mouth, 1936–38, to limit the diversion and keep the reversed river from flowing back into the lake. The chamber is 600 ft long, 80 ft wide and 22 ft deep, with two pairs of 30-ft sector gates; the controlling works have eight 10 × 10 ft sluice gates. A cycle takes 12–15 minutes for a two-to-five-foot difference in level.
  • The odd detail: From 1938 the whole river sits in a box: the lake is literally higher than the city's river, held back by gates the Sanitary District opens in storms.
  • People: the Sanitary District of Chicago (Kelly's old agency).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Harbor_Lock
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the decree's Chief Justice BENDS; see R03).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Running water and thresholds: a lock is a door between two waters. The Kabbalahs' legend (see R09) says the reversal was a warding — in 1938 someone installed a gate in it, and the district keeps the sluice log.

The river moved a quarter-mile — the South Branch straightening — 1927–1929

  • Where: South Branch of the Chicago River between roughly Polk and 18th streets (South Loop).
  • What happened: Between 1927 and 1929 the South Branch was dug a new straight channel and moved about a quarter mile (0.4 km) west to make room for a railroad terminal on the old bed. (Cost and payer were not in the page read.)
  • The odd detail: A mile of the river that a 1920s Chicagoan knew is now under railway yards; the old channel was filled.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_River
  • Confidence: Medium-High (dates and distance from one source; exact limits of the cut unverified).
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Whatever the river had covered in its old bed — the dead of the 1871 fire, the Eastland's losses washed down — went under the fill. Ghost stories about the old river at the rail yards.

Part 3 — Power and gas

Gas from Texas — the first 1,000-mile pipeline reaches Chicago — 29 Aug / 1 Sep 1931

  • Where: From the Amarillo (Texas Panhandle) fields to Chicago, across Kansas, Nebraska, Iowa and Illinois.
  • What happened: Continental Construction Corporation (renamed Natural Gas Pipeline Company of America in December 1931) began laying pipe in Kansas and Nebraska in June 1930; the line was completed 29 August 1931 and gas began moving on 1 September. It was a 24-inch line and "the first 1,000-mile pipeline built in the United States" — the first high-pressure, thin-walled, electrically-welded cross-country pipeline. Standard Oil of New Jersey and Cities Service led; Insull's utilities, Phillips and Skelly took minority stakes; Peoples Gas was a co-owner. Until then Chicago's Peoples Gas had made "water gas" by forcing steam over hot coke.
  • The odd detail: In the worst year of the Depression a steel vein a thousand miles long was welded across the plains to feed the city's stoves — the gas in a Chicago kitchen now came from under Texas.
  • People: Samuel Insull — minority investor; Peoples Gas Light and Coke Co.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_Gas_Pipeline_Company_of_America ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peoples_Gas
  • Confidence: High on the pipeline; the date Chicago's households were converted from manufactured to natural gas was NOT confirmed.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A 1,000-mile pipe has compressor stations in empty country every so often. One of the Kansas stations has a crew that never rotates.

State Line — Insull's giant turbine on the Indiana border — 1926–1929 (and Unit 2, 1938)

  • Where: State Line Generating Plant, on Lake Michigan inside the city limits of Hammond, Indiana, hard against the Illinois–Indiana line — it borders the line; it does not straddle it ⟨fixed: V_R20⟩.
  • What happened: Samuel Insull's companies built State Line in 1926–29, an Art Deco plant by Graham, Anderson, Probst & White. Its Unit 1, 208 megawatts, built by General Electric at Schenectady, was on its 1929 opening the largest turbine-generator ever built, raising Chicago-area capacity by more than 15 per cent to 1,518 MW. Insull meant the plant to reach over 1,000 MW — the world's first gigawatt plant; it never did. Commonwealth Edison added Unit 2 in 1938. It shut on 31 March 2012.
  • The odd detail: The machine that lit Chicago in the 1930s stood just over the state line, in Indiana — the city's biggest generator sat in another state's jurisdiction ⟨fixed: V_R20⟩.
  • People: Samuel Insull — builder (fled 1932; see R03, R13).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Line_Generating_Plant
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A murder in the turbine hall at State Line: Illinois and Indiana each say the body lay on the other side of the line — so the federal men take it.

Part 4 — The Great Lakes ring: bridges, tunnels, canals, towers

The Ambassador Bridge — the world's longest span, and its first jumper — 16 Aug 1927 to 15 Nov 1929; April 1930

  • Where: Over the Detroit River, Detroit, Michigan, to Windsor, Ontario.
  • What happened: Begun 16 August 1927 and opened 15 November 1929 — three weeks after Black Tuesday — the bridge's 1,850-ft main span was the longest suspension span in the world until the George Washington Bridge in 1931. McClintic-Marshall of Pittsburgh built it. Its promoter Joseph A. Bower, a New York specialist in rescuing mismanaged companies, raised the first $12 million after a Toronto financier embezzled the bridge company's securities. In April 1930, shortly after it opened, a Canadian immigration inspector jumped to his death from it; it became a known place for suicides.
  • The odd detail: The bridge's money was stolen before its steel went up, and one of the first men to die on it was the border officer paid to watch who crossed it.
  • People: Joseph A. Bower — financier; the unnamed Canadian immigration inspector (April 1930).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambassador_Bridge
  • Confidence: High on dates; the inspector's name not given.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — Canada's position in a world where Britain lost the war (see R07); the bridge itself holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The immigration inspector had been keeping a private list of who crossed at night. The list was not on him when they pulled him out of the river.

The Detroit–Windsor Tunnel — nine steel tubes sunk in the river — summer 1928 to 3 Nov 1930 (deepens R07)

  • Where: Under the Detroit River, downtown Detroit to downtown Windsor.
  • What happened: Built with three methods — cut-and-cover approaches, hand-bored sections under tunnelling shields, and, for the river, an immersed tube: steam dredges dug a trench in the riverbed and nine tubes, each 250 ft long and 35 ft across, were sunk into it and covered with 4–20 ft of mud. 5,160 ft long; the roadway 75 ft below the river surface at its lowest; about $25 million. Opened 3 November 1930 — the third underwater vehicular tunnel in the U.S. (after the Holland Tunnel and the Posey Tube). In the 1930s it kept a rescue truck with two drivers' positions facing opposite ways, so it never had to back up in the tube.
  • The odd detail: A tow truck that could drive out of the tunnel in either direction without turning — built because in the tube there is nowhere to turn around.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit%E2%80%93Windsor_Tunnel
  • Confidence: High; construction deaths not documented on the page.
  • Divergence check: holds (Canada BENDS lightly, as above).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Seventy-five feet under the river, in a tube built of sections sunk from above — a place where "running water" is overhead on every side. The Hunter Guild uses it to move what cannot cross running water.

The Welland Ship Canal opens — 137 workers dead — 1913–1932

  • Where: Niagara Peninsula, Ontario — St. Catharines/Port Weller (Lake Ontario) to Port Colborne (Lake Erie), bypassing Niagara Falls.
  • What happened: The fourth Welland Canal was begun in 1913, stopped 1916–19 for want of workers, and opened officially 6 August 1932 (dredging to the full 25 ft finished only in 1935). It replaced the old canal's 40 locks with eight: seven lift locks 233.5 m × 24.4 m, each lifting ships 13–15 m, and a 349.9 m control lock at Port Colborne. A memorial unveiled at Lock 3 in 2017 names 137 workers who died building it.
  • The odd detail: Ships climb the Niagara Escarpment on a staircase of water — the upper Great Lakes open to the sea — and 137 men died cutting the steps.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welland_Canal
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the 1916–19 halt came from Canada's war effort; in the setting Canada still fought (Britain lost), so the halt plausibly holds, but a bankrupt Britain would change who paid for a Canadian canal in the 1920s.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The canal lets deep-draught ships from anywhere reach Chicago from 1932 — a German freighter could now come straight up to Navy Pier. Who inspects its hold?

The Van Sweringens: the Terminal Tower's owners buy back their own ruin — 1930–1936

  • Where: Terminal Tower and Cleveland Union Terminal, Public Square, Cleveland; their estate Daisy Hill (Roundwood Manor), Hunting Valley, Ohio.
  • What happened: The reclusive brothers Oris Paxton and Mantis James Van Sweringen, developers of Shaker Heights and its rapid-transit line, built the Terminal Tower (52 floors, 708 ft, on caissons sunk 280 ft; the tallest building in North America outside New York until 1964) over their new underground rail station; the complex was formally opened 29 June 1930 ⟨fixed: V_R20⟩. At its peak their railroad pyramid was reckoned at $3 billion. On 1 May 1935 they defaulted on $48 million of J. P. Morgan & Co. loans; Morgan ordered the collateral auctioned in September 1935, and the brothers, with arranged backing, bought their own empire back for just over $3 million. M. J. died 12 December 1935; O. P. on 22 November 1936, worth less than $3,000. Neither ever married; they shared a bedroom in their 54-room mansion and were never seen in public apart.
  • The odd detail: Two brothers who shared a bedroom all their lives controlled the second-tallest building on earth, lost everything, bought it back at auction for a thousandth of its worth, and died within a year of each other.
  • People: Oris Paxton Van Sweringen (1879–1936); Mantis James Van Sweringen (1881–1935) — railroad and real-estate magnates.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/v/van-sweringen-oris-paxton-and-mantis-james ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Sweringen_brothers ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_Tower
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (IHS's American seat, John Carroll, is a few miles east of Public Square.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Always together": IHS knows why the brothers were never apart. When M. J. died the other lasted eleven months. The Cleveland Torso Murders began in the same years, in Kingsbury Run — beside the Van Sweringens' own rail lines.

Milorganite — Milwaukee sells its sewage by the bag — 1925–1926 onward

  • Where: Jones Island treatment plant, Milwaukee harbour, Wisconsin.
  • What happened: The Jones Island plant, built 1925, had the largest wastewater capacity in the world and was the first plant in the U.S. to succeed in making fertilizer from activated sludge. Milorganite — "Milwaukee Organic Nitrogen", the winning entry in a 1925 naming contest in National Fertilizer Magazine — went on sale in 1926 as the first pelletized fertilizer in the U.S. The sludge (spent microbes) is dried with air at 900–1,200 °F and pelletized; it was pushed to golf courses in its early decades. The programme grew out of the city's Socialist administrations elected from 1911 ("sewer socialism"), with University of Wisconsin agricultural researchers.
  • The odd detail: A socialist city dried its sewage in furnaces and sold it to country clubs' greens.
  • People: Oyvind Juul Noer — the agronomist who sold it to the golf trade.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milorganite
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (socialism without a Soviet Union still fits: Milwaukee's was homegrown and German).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Everything flushed in Milwaukee ends up, heat-dried, on the fairways of the North Shore clubs. A summoner who needs "the leavings of a hundred thousand" buys it by the sack.

The Palace of Purification — Toronto's R. C. Harris water plant — 1932–1941

  • Where: Queen Street East at the Beaches, Toronto, on Lake Ontario.
  • What happened: Works Commissioner R. C. Harris (commissioner 1912–45) had the city buy the site in 1927 for $370,000; construction began in 1932 and the plant opened 1 November 1941 — an Art Deco temple with marble entries and vast halls of filtration pools, nicknamed the "Palace of Purification". Its intakes run 2.6 km out under Lake Ontario in 15 m of water. It still supplies about 30 per cent of Toronto's water.
  • The odd detail: Toronto built a marble palace to clean water all through the Depression — while Chicago was still drinking its lake unfiltered (see R13's crib entry).
  • People: Rowland Caldwell Harris (1875–1945) — Toronto's commissioner of works.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._C._Harris_Water_Treatment_Plant
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — Canada's finances in a world where Britain lost (see R07); the plant itself can hold.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A water-palace built like a temple, still unfinished through the 1930s: a building site on the shore where the pools are dug but not yet filled.

The Blue Water Bridge — Port Huron to Sarnia — opened 10 Oct 1938

  • Where: Over the St. Clair River at the foot of Lake Huron — Port Huron, Michigan, to Point Edward/Sarnia, Ontario.
  • What happened: Proposed in 1927, stalled in the Depression, authorised by Congress in August 1935 (Public Law 411) to be paid off by a 25-cent toll within 30 years. Ralph Modjeski and Frank Masters (with Charles Monsarrat and P. L. Pratley) switched from a suspension design to a cantilever truss because ships needed 150 ft of clearance. 6,178 ft long, 871-ft main span; two lanes and sidewalks; opened 10 October 1938.
  • The odd detail: The river under it is the whole outflow of Lake Huron — the bridge stands over one of the fastest big currents on the lakes.
  • People: Ralph Modjeski — bridge engineer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Water_Bridge
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds (Canada BENDS lightly).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A third crossing out of the Detroit funnel from 1938 — the smugglers and the things they carry move north to Port Huron.

Part 5 — Deaths underground: sewers and tunnels

Poison smoke in the sanitary tunnel — eleven dead 35 feet under the Southwest Side — night of 13–14 Apr 1931

The LaSalle Street tunnel is closed and filled — 27 Nov 1939 (the subway eats the old river tunnels)

Part 11 — How the subway was dug (the city's own 1943 booklet)

Mining the State Street tubes: clay, "bench" and "shield", streetcars riding on planks — Nov 1938 to 1943

  • Where: Route No. 1 — from Armitage and Sheffield, southeast under Clybourn Avenue, east under Division Street, south under State Street to 16th Street; the Loop stretch under State.
  • What happened: The City Council authorised the subway on 3 November 1938; "in six weeks — a record for speed on a project of such size — work was begun". Outside the Loop, in clay "comparatively free from water", miners used the "bench" method; under the Loop, where the soil was as much as 58 per cent water and street and building loads were heavy, they used the shield method "because of its greater safety in such fluid soil". Part 12 — Water: what came out of the tap (a 1934 study)

Chicago's unfiltered water: $5 million a year on bottled water, and a referendum nobody obeyed — 1924–1934

  • Where: The whole city and its 33 water-buying suburbs.
  • What happened: Max R. White's University of Chicago study (1934) found that 80 per cent of the region's people — 3,880,603 — drank unfiltered water "at times below standard", because Chicago, which sold water to 33 suburbs, "has not introduced filtration". Chicago's water averaged 12.5 parts per million turbidity in 1924–30 (the Treasury standard was under 10), was over the limit 33.6 per cent of the time, and reached 185 ppm; tastes and odours bad enough for complaints came on 60 days in 1930. Chicagoans spent an estimated $5,000,000 a year on bottled drinking water and $1,000,000 on private filters — about what filtering the whole supply would have cost. Filtration was first suggested in 1863; in 1930 an advisory referendum — "Shall the city council pass an ordinance providing for filtration?" — carried by a large majority; no ordinance followed. The city applied to the PWA for $22,400,000 for meters and a South Side filtration plant, but South Side residents' objections to the site held it up. (The South District plant was approved in 1930, slowed by the Depression and finished in 1947 — then the largest in the world.)
  • The odd detail: In the 1930s, Chicago voted to filter its water and its council simply did not — while the rich bought bottled water and the poor drank the lake.
