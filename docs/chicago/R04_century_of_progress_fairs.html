Overview

63 entries. The owner's question — what did Germany send to the World's Fair? — has a clean answer: officially, nothing. The 1933 Official Guide Book's "Foreign Participation" gives eighteen countries their own headings (Palestine included) and none to Germany, which appears only in its closing "Foreign Scientific Displays" paragraph through the Robert Koch Institute of Berlin and the Dresden museum ⟨fixed: V_R04⟩; the Weimar treasury was empty [UNVERIFIED — V_R04] and Hitler's four-month-old government never built a pavilion. What Chicago got was German-American: a privately built German-American Building whose committee refused the local Nazis' demand for a swastika and flew the imperial black-white-red instead (the consul general who would not intervene was recalled in August 1933), a 25,000-strong German Day at Soldier Field that same week, the Eitel brothers' Old Heidelberg Inn, and in 1934 a refrigerated Black Forest Village. Germany-proper appeared only as loans inside American halls — the Dresden Hygiene Museum's Transparent Man ("one of only two in the world"), a Robert Koch Institute memorial exhibit on Koch's life and work ⟨fixed: V_R04⟩, the Gutenberg press from Mainz — plus the Zeiss projector already in the Adler, and one day of the Graf Zeppelin with a swastika on one fin and Hitler's ambassador aboard [UNVERIFIED — V_R04]. The 1893 precedent (Krupp's $1.5-million gun fortress with ice fountains, the German Government Building, the Midway German Village, German Day) is what a rich, victorious Kaiser's Germany would copy, and a proposal entry says so. The rest of the file covers the other nations with bend flags (Italy's Balbo armada and fasces-fronted pavilion; China declining and Bendix building a Tibetan temple; Japan advertising Manchuria; Ukraine's self-funded pavilion and Litvinov's objection; Czechoslovakia's glass box and Cermak's death; Sweden's Bernadotte; Belgium's dog-carts; the 1934 villages), the fair's own marvels (Arcturus and the disputed telescope, the Sky Ride and its thermite end, the breathing dome, the houses barged to Indiana, the Zephyr on stage, the balloon that fell in a rail yard), the Midway (Sally Rand, Ripley, the Bavarian Midget Village, Couney's incubators), religion and race (the 150,000-strong Jewish pageant ⟨fixed: V_R04⟩, the Hall of Religion, the Episcopal bishop who blessed the fair, Negro Day and the DuSable cabin), sickness and death (the fingertip plug behind 98 dysentery deaths, the parachute jumper, the mayor shot beside Roosevelt), the afterlife (MSI, the All-Star Game), and the regional fairs (Cleveland 1936–37 with a Torso victim's death mask on the Midway; New York 1939–40 with Germany absent). The biggest bends for the setting: no Nazis anywhere, Czechoslovakia and the USSR do not exist, Mexico and the Dominican Republic are American-held, and Chicago's Germans never had to rename the Bismarck Hotel.

1. Germany at the 1933–34 fair

The short answer to the owner's question. The German government sent nothing official to A Century of Progress. The Weimar Republic was broke [UNVERIFIED — V_R04] and the Nazi government (in power from 30 Jan 1933, four months before the gates opened) never mounted a state pavilion. What Chicago got instead was: (a) a privately organised German-American Building run by a committee of prominent German-descended Chicagoans, which flew the imperial black-white-red flag rather than the swastika and touched off a diplomatic incident; (b) German cultural and scientific loans inside American halls — the Gutenberg press from Mainz, the Transparent Man from the Dresden Hygiene Museum ("composed of cellon… one of only two in the world," says the 1933 Official Guide Book) and a Robert Koch Institute memorial exhibit on Koch's life and work ⟨fixed: V_R04⟩ in the Hall of Science's medical section, the Zeiss projector already in the Adler Planetarium on the fairgrounds; the 1933 Guide Book's "Foreign Participation" section (pp. 92–96) gives eighteen countries (seventeen plus Palestine) their own headings and none to Germany, naming it only through "the Robert Koch Institute of Berlin, the Deutches Museum of Dresden" in its closing "Foreign Scientific Displays" paragraph ⟨fixed: V_R04⟩; (c) German-American concessions — the Old Heidelberg Inn (1933–34) and the Black Forest Village (1934); (d) the German Day rally of 25,000 at Soldier Field (13 Aug 1933); and (e) the one-day visit of the Graf Zeppelin (26 Oct 1933) with a swastika painted on one fin and the former Chancellor Hans Luther aboard [UNVERIFIED — V_R04]. Everything Nazi-flavoured below BENDS in this setting; everything German-American mostly holds.

The German-American Building and the flag it refused to fly — July–August 1933

  • Where: A Century of Progress fairgrounds, Burnham Park lakefront, Chicago (exact plot UNVERIFIED — listed among the fair's pavilions as the "German-American Pavilion")
  • What happened: Germany had no official pavilion, so a committee "composed of leading Chicagoans of German descent" built and ran a German-American building on the grounds. In July 1933 local Nazis ("Friends of New Germany" sympathisers) [UNVERIFIED — V2_R04] demanded that the swastika flag fly over it. The committee "absolutely refused the demand and decreed that as the exhibit was not an official government one, a swastika was not necessary"; instead "the committee is flying the imperial flag of Germany" — the black-white-red of the Kaiser's Reich — over the building. The Chicago Daily Tribune ran "Favor Imperial Flag for German Building at Fair" (July 1933) and the Jewish Daily Bulletin "Demand Swastika Fly on World Fair Bldg. Refused by Germans." [UNVERIFIED — V2_R04] The German Consul General refused to intervene, and was recalled within weeks (next entry).
  • The odd detail: In real 1933 Chicago the Kaiser's flag was the compromise flag — the one everybody could stand under. In this setting it is simply the flag of the winning power.
  • People: the German-American committee (members unnamed in sources read) — civic Germans, mostly anti-Nazi by conduct; Dr. Hugo F. Simon — German Consul General who declined to press the Nazi demand
  • Sources: https://www.jta.org/archive/chicago-consuls-recall-traced-to-stand-against-flying-swastika-at-fair-exhibit (JTA, 13 Aug 1933); https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/johs.12269 (Herman, "Fascists at the Fair," J. Historical Sociology 33, 2020, abstract: "the local German American population was able to organize some resistance to minimize the Nazi presence on the fairgrounds… Neither the Fair's organizing committee nor the U.S. State Department offered any objection")
  • Confidence: High on the flag dispute and the recall (JTA, contemporaneous). Medium on the building's contents/location (not found in pages read; Tribune headline seen only as a citation).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — no Nazis, no swastika, no Friends of New Germany. In this timeline an official Imperial German pavilion is the plausible replacement (see the proposal entry below); the German-American committee's imperial flag is no act of defiance, it is the national flag.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Keep the fight over which flag flies but invert the sides: the German-American civic committee wants the imperial flag; someone else — a Hitler-circle occult society with Golden Circle money — wants a different banner run up on a particular night for a reason that has nothing to do with politics. Consul General Hugo Ferdinand Simon recalled over the swastika — August 1933

  • Where: German Consulate General, Chicago
  • What happened: JTA reported on 13 Aug 1933 that Dr. Hugo Ferdinand Simon, consul general in Chicago since November 1926, was being recalled to Germany, and that Chicagoans traced it to his "refusal to take an active part in the controversy in which local Nazis demanded the swastika flag be flown from the German American building at A Century of Progress." Simon said "he did not feel the matter was one which required official action by him." He was not Jewish (French Huguenot descent) but had been adjutant to the Jewish foreign minister Walther Rathenau, which was thought to make him suspect in Berlin. He was reported "distressed" at growing Nazi influence in Chicago's German community, and was said to be "considering seriously the offer of a home in Chicago made by a friend here" rather than going home ⟨fixed: V_R04⟩.
  • The odd detail: The same day the JTA story ran, the German Day crowd of 25,000 at Soldier Field noted that the Consul General "was not represented" — the consulate had simply gone silent.
  • People: Dr. Hugo F. Simon — consul general 1926–33, Rathenau's old adjutant; Walther Rathenau — Weimar foreign minister murdered 1922 (name only)
  • Sources: https://www.jta.org/archive/chicago-consuls-recall-traced-to-stand-against-flying-swastika-at-fair-exhibit; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_7_1222 (Abendpost, 14 Aug 1933, for the consul's absence)
  • Confidence: High (contemporaneous JTA), Medium on the "considering staying" detail (reported as rumour in 1933).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — no Nazi Berlin to recall him. A Kaiser's consul in Chicago in 1933 would be a victor's diplomat, watched by the Secret Service for the sanctions war and for Germany's habit of funding other people's revolutions.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A consul general who "does not feel the matter requires official action" and then quietly does not go home is a ready-made NPC — a career diplomat who knows something about what the Berlin embassy is really shipping through the fair.

German Day at Soldier Field — Sunday 13 August 1933

  • Where: Soldier Field, on the fairgrounds' northern edge
  • What happened: The German Day Association filled Soldier Field with an estimated 25,000 people. About 1,200 male singers and 800 female singers from German singing societies of Chicago, Milwaukee, Hammond, Appleton, St. Paul, Peoria, Kansas City and Muscatine sang German compositions, with delegations (not choirs) from German-American associations of Omaha, St. Louis, New York, Newark, Detroit, Baltimore and Providence ⟨fixed: V2_R04⟩, the Milwaukee choruses under Hans Marlow (former conductor of the Chicago Singverein). Bernard De Vry (association president) welcomed the crowd; George Seibel, First Speaker of the Amerikanischer Turnerbund, gave the main address on German contributions to America (gymnastics, the kindergarten); Mayor Edward Kelly called German-Americans "the best taxpayers in this great community." The Rosegger Steirer Klub danced a "star dance." Resolutions passed opposing the League of Nations and the World Court, demanding repeal of the national-origins immigration clause, calling for an investigation of the Versailles Treaty's war-guilt article, endorsing Roosevelt's depression measures and urging repeal of Prohibition ⟨fixed: V_R04⟩. The consul general was not represented. No swastika flew; per Herman (2020) "the Nazi flag was never flown at German American days or events during the Century of Progress." [UNVERIFIED — V_R04] (German Days were held at Soldier Field every year 1925–37.)
  • The odd detail: The day's politics came in its resolutions — 25,000 Chicagoans, at a World's Fair, voting to reopen the Versailles war-guilt question and to scrap the immigration quotas ⟨fixed: V_R04⟩.
  • People: Bernard De Vry — German Day Association president; George Seibel — Turnerbund speaker; Hans Marlow — conductor; Mayor Edward J. Kelly — spoke
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_7_1222 (Newberry Foreign Language Press Survey, Abendpost 14 Aug 1933); https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/johs.12269
  • Confidence: High (translated contemporaneous German-language press).
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — there is no Versailles to condemn; the resolution would instead be about the American position toward a victorious Germany (sanctions, the tariff war). The choirs, the Turners, the mayor's line all hold.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): 2,000 singers rehearsing in a stadium the night before is cover for anything; a Turner hall's gymnasium is where a werewolf pack would hide in plain sight. "The best taxpayers in this great community" is the mayor telling the Secret Service to leave the Germans alone.

The Graf Zeppelin over Chicago — Thursday 26 October 1933

  • Where: Circled the fairgrounds and the Loop; landed at Curtiss-Wright Airport, Glenview, Ill.
  • What happened: The LZ-127 Graf Zeppelin, 776 ft long, left Friedrichshafen 14 Oct on its "Century of Progress" flight via South America (Recife, Rio) and Miami, then Akron, reaching Chicago on 26 Oct. Under new German Air Ministry rules the port tail-fin carried the swastika, the starboard fin the old black-white-red tricolour; Commander Hugo Eckener, no friend of Hitler, "was careful to fly a clockwise pattern so that Chicagoans would see only the tricolor German flag on the starboard fin, and not the swastika." After some two hours over the lake and the fair [UNVERIFIED — V_R04] it set down at Glenview for only about twenty minutes because of weather, leaving Eckener behind for a 24-hour round of fair events, then flew under Capt. Lehmann over the fairgrounds again and on to Akron, docking there at 3 p.m.; Eckener rejoined it at Akron by United Air Lines plane, and it went back to Germany via Seville ⟨fixed: V_R04⟩. Aboard: Eckener, Capt. Ernst Lehmann, F. W. von Meister, and Dr. Hans Luther [UNVERIFIED — V_R04] — former Chancellor (1925–26), now Hitler's ambassador in Washington. The Union League Club gave a banquet for 600 (bankers, industrialists, German-Americans) at a table "draped with the swastika banner and the Stars and Stripes" where Luther "set forth the creed of Adolf Hitler" — the Washington Times of 27 Oct 1933 (p. 10) reported that Luther "outlined the ideals and aims of the Hitler government," "told of the Nazi position on disarmament," "vigorously denied charges of German imperialism" and "criticized the Jewish American boycott of German goods"; Rufus Dawes received the party and Mayor Kelly received Eckener at City Hall; North German Lloyd gave a luncheon at the Midday Club. A planned Milwaukee flyover was cancelled. The U.S. issued a 50-cent Zeppelin airmail stamp (on sale 7 Oct) and mail was carried at 50c per half-ounce to Akron, Seville and Friedrichshafen.
  • The odd detail: The pilot flew in circles so a city would see only one side of his ship.
  • People: Dr. Hugo Eckener — commander, anti-Nazi; Dr. Hans Luther — ex-Chancellor, ambassador; Capt. Ernst A. Lehmann — later died in the Hindenburg; F. W. von Meister — Eckener's American aide; Rufus C. Dawes — fair president (brother of the setting's President Dawes)
  • Sources: https://chicagology.com/centuryprogress/1933fair74/ (reprints the Tribune coverage); https://www.airships.net/lz127-graf-zeppelin/history/; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Century_of_Progress
  • Confidence: High on date, route, the fin trick, the banquet; Medium on the exact landing duration ("twenty minutes" from the Tribune reprint).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — no swastika, no Hitler creed at the banquet. But the visit itself holds better in this setting: a victorious Imperial Germany's zeppelin calling on the fair of a former neutral is a natural act of showing the flag; Luther would come as the Kaiser's ambassador.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The airship is the perfect sealed room: something (or someone) that crossed the Atlantic in the Graf's hold was on Chicago soil for twenty minutes at Glenview and then gone. Eckener's clockwise circuit becomes a ritual circuit — a widdershins/deosil choice made over a city for reasons his passengers understood.

The Old Heidelberg Inn — the Eitel brothers' fair café, 1933–34 (and its Randolph Street afterlife)

  • Where: On the fairgrounds 1933–34 (plot UNVERIFIED); then 14–16 W. Randolph St., Chicago, from 1934
  • What happened: The Eitel brothers (Emil, Karl, Robert, Max — Stuttgart-born Chicago hoteliers since 1894; Bismarck Hotel, Randolph Street) won a 1932 contract to build and run one of the biggest restaurants at the fair, the Old Heidelberg Inn, a German beer-hall replica that was "massive and massively successful." [UNVERIFIED — V2_R04] Its success led Max and Robert Eitel to open a permanent Old Heidelberg in 1934 on the site of the short-lived Randolph Theatre [UNVERIFIED — V2_R04], designed by Graham, Anderson, Probst & White as a Bavarian beer cellar seating up to 3,500 [UNVERIFIED — V_R04] (sold to Allgauer's in 1951).
  • The odd detail: The fair's German beer hall opened the summer Prohibition was still on (3.2 beer became legal 7 April 1933; full repeal 5 Dec 1933) — the Old Heidelberg poured legal beer at a World's Fair for the first time in fourteen years.
  • People: Max and Robert Eitel — restaurateurs; Emil and Karl Eitel — Bismarck Hotel hoteliers
  • Sources: https://www.wbez.org/reset-with-sasha-ann-simons/2019/12/27/whats-that-building-argo-tea-at-16-w-randolph-st; https://chuckmanchicagonostalgia.wordpress.com/2019/05/23/postcard-chicago-the-eitel-brothers-old-heidelberg-restaurant-14-w-randolph-opened-1934-one-of-the-original-dining-rooms-note-in-1951-the-eitels-sold-old-heidelberg-to-allgauers/ (both seen via search summaries this session; Wikipedia "Eitel Brothers" for the family)

The Black Forest Village — winter in July, 1934

  • Where: Fairgrounds, 1934 season (plot UNVERIFIED)
  • What happened: A 1934 concession recreating a Black Forest hamlet in winter: "snow banked on cottages and chalet roofs," icicles on the eaves, a frozen mill pond used for ice-skating exhibitions, all kept cold by an air-conditioning plant in a Chicago summer. Craftsmen demonstrated cuckoo-clock making, cane carving, blacksmithing and kirsch distilling; German orchestras and strolling musicians played; attendants wore "quaint German mountaineer costumes." A German restaurant with a grill and lunch counter, orchestral floor shows and dancing "afternoon and evening" (Popular Mechanics, June 1934). A Phoenix Art Museum print records it as "The Black Forest Village, A Century of Progress, Chicago, 1934."
  • The odd detail: A refrigerated German winter, with real ice, on the lakefront in August.

What a victorious Kaiser's Germany would send instead — PROPOSAL (not history)

  • Where: A hypothetical official German Government Pavilion at A Century of Progress
  • What happened (real baseline): Real 1933 Germany could afford nothing [UNVERIFIED — V2_R04]; real 1893 Germany sent the largest and costliest foreign showing at the Columbian Exposition (Krupp's gun pavilion, the German Government Building, the German Village, a Germania fountain, German Day). The 1893 model is the one to copy for a rich, victorious empire.
  • Plausible contents, by precedent: (1) A Krupp hall — the 1893 firm returned with the "Thunderer" coastal gun; a 1933 Krupp would show the Paris Gun's successor and armour plate — and be a live diplomatic problem in a city whose president-brother runs the fair and whose government is in a tariff war with Berlin. (2) Zeiss — already present in fact: the Adler Planetarium (opened 1930, the first in the Western Hemisphere) ran a Zeiss projector, and Zeiss instruments would anchor an Imperial science hall. (3) The Dresden Hygiene Museum's Transparent Man — actually sent in 1933 (see the Hall of Science entry) — as the state's own exhibit. (4) The Gutenberg press from Mainz — actually lent in 1933. (5) Lufthansa / Zeppelin — the Graf Zeppelin's call would be an official state visit with the Kaiser's ambassador, not a stunt. (6) A German Village on the Midway model — the Black Forest Village and Old Heidelberg exist as private concessions anyway. (7) Reparations-funded largesse: an imperial Germany collecting from France, Britain and Russia could afford the kind of gift Italy actually gave — a monument on the lakefront (Italy gave the Balbo column; Germany's 1893 precedent was the Germania fountain).
  • The odd detail: In the real 1933 fair the only two governments with a visible ideology on the grounds were Fascist Italy (official pavilion, fasces on the façade) and, by proxy, Nazi Germany (the swastika fin). In this setting the ideological presence is the Kaiserreich — monarchy, army, science, church — and the Italian presence sits beside it as a junior friend, not a rival fascism.

  • Divergence check: BENDS by design.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Germany as the patron of other people's revolutions" — the Imperial pavilion is the one place on the fairgrounds where an Irish republican, a Mexican revolutionary, an Indian nationalist and a Black Legion organiser might all be seen leaving by the same door. 2. The 1893 precedent — Germany at the World's Columbian Exposition

The Krupp Gun Pavilion and "The Thunderer" — May–October 1893

  • Where: Jackson Park lakefront, between the La Rabida convent replica and the Leather exhibit, south of the Casino (the site is now La Rabida Children's Hospital)
  • What happened: Fried. Krupp of Essen built its own fortress-villa pavilion (entrance hall 138 × 25 × 30 ft; main hall 197 × 82 × 43 ft), modelled as a cross between a fort and the family's Villa Hügel [UNVERIFIED — V2_R04], at a cost put at about $1.5 million to build and as much again to ship. Its centrepiece was the largest gun in the world: 42 cm (16.54 in) bore, 46 ft long, breech 6.5 ft across, barrel weight given as "just over 240,000 pounds" (drloih, about 109 metric tons) or 122.4 metric tons, about 270,000 lb (Wikipedia) — the sources disagree ⟨fixed: V2_R04⟩, costing $200,000 to make and $80,000 to bring across; the shell weighed 2,000–2,500 lb driven by 900 lb of brown powder, with an advertised range of 13–15 miles, and a shrapnel round throwing some 3,400 quarter-pound steel balls. Krupp offered it to the U.S. Army for $223,000 with turret and mounts; the Army declined it as too dangerous and too dear (about $1,500 a shot). Two other "world's largest" steel cannon in the hall "worked poorly." Krupp also installed the fair's first indoor air-conditioning — two "Glacier Fountains" (Dr. William Raydt of Hanover, 1884–86 design) spraying water over brine-chilled copper coils, so ice formed in the hall and visitors "plac[ed] their hands close to the fountain to cool themselves." By late March 1894 the dismantled pavilion was on a steamer home.
  • The odd detail: The biggest gun on earth was displayed in the only air-conditioned room in Chicago [UNVERIFIED — V_R04], and the ice came from a carbonic-acid refrigerator.
  • People: Friedrich Alfred "Fritz" Krupp (1854–1902) — owner, Germany's largest employer (~45,000), asthmatic, pioneer of company pensions; Dr. William Raydt — Hanover engineer of the ice fountains
  • Sources: https://drloihjournal.blogspot.com/2019/04/krupp-gun-pavilion-at-the-1893-chicago-worlds-columbian-exposition.html; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World%27s_Columbian_Exposition
  • Confidence: High on the gun's existence, size and the pavilion; Medium on cost figures (sources disagree between $1M and $1.5M) and range claims (Krupp's own marketing).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): In 1933 the old men who saw the Thunderer as boys run the city. A Krupp return to the 1933 fair (see proposal above) reopens the 1893 site — and whatever was buried under a $1.5-million fortress that stood for six months and then vanished.

German Day at the Columbian Exposition — Thursday 15 June 1893, and the German Government Building

  • Where: Jackson Park; the German Government Building stood on the lakefront near the north pier with the Germania fountain just to its north
  • What happened: The fair's official timeline records German Day on Thursday, 15 June 1893, one of the great national days of the fair. Together with the Krupp pavilion and the Midway village this made Germany the most conspicuous foreign presence of 1893.

4. The fair itself

Arcturus lights the fair — Saturday 27 May 1933, 9:15 p.m. (also in 09-22 archive: W01)

  • Where: Hall of Science courtyard, Burnham Park
  • What happened: Edwin B. At 9:15 p.m. photocells at four observatories — Yerkes, Harvard, Allegheny (Pittsburgh) and the University of Illinois (Urbana) — converted the starlight to current, relayed by Western Union to the grounds; operators called "Harvard, are you ready?" — "Yes" — down the line, threw the switch, and a searchlight atop the Hall of Science and then the coloured floods of the whole fair came on before about 30,000 people. The Golden Pavilion of Jehol — Vincent Bendix's Lama Temple, 1933–38

  • Where: The fairgrounds (near the Chinese and Japanese pavilions, Northerly Island side); disassembled March 1938

3b. The other nations, 1933–34 — continued

Who actually came, officially. Herman (2020) and the fair's own critics agree the fair had "tremendous industrial representation but, by comparison, 'poor support' by foreign countries." [UNVERIFIED — V2_R04] Governments that built or sponsored pavilions in 1933 included Italy, Japan, Sweden, Czechoslovakia (and, per the fair's lists, others — a full roster was not retrievable this session: worldfairs.info's pavilion list returned 503 three times; see leads). China and Germany declined officially; their showings were private. Ukraine had the only ethnic pavilion funded by no government at all. The 1934 "villages" (Belgian, English, Irish, Dutch, Swiss, Tunisian, Mexican, Spanish, Moroccan, Italian, Black Forest, Streets of Shanghai, Colonial) were mostly "moneymaking enterprises by Americans" (Newberry).

7. Sickness, death, crime and cons

The amoebic dysentery outbreak — August 1933 to February 1934 (also in 09-22 archive: H01)

  • Where: Congress Hotel (520 S. Michigan Ave; opened 1893 as the Auditorium Annex) and the Auditorium Hotel across the street, linked by Peacock Alley, a marble-lined tunnel under Michigan Avenue
  • What happened: Cases began in June 1933 and were recognised as amoebic dysentery in mid-August; the peak ran 13 Aug–11 Oct. Between 1 June 1933 and 30 June 1934, 1,409 cases and 98 deaths were recorded, from "over 400 cities scattered in 43 states, the Territory of Hawaii, and three Canadian Provinces" — two-thirds of them out-of-town fairgoers; about 160,000 visitors had stayed at or eaten in the two hotels. The joint investigation (Chicago Board of Health, the city's Division of Water Purification, the U.S. Public Health Service, the State University of Iowa) found the two hotels' shared house-water system cross-connected through leaking condenser valves, and in the Congress a wooden plug in a sewer line "so loose that it was easily removed by the finger tips," letting sewage into the drinking-water cooling tank at about two-tenths of a gallon a minute during storms. Repairs were finished by February 1934 and a committee of experts formally blamed "defective plumbing." It was "the first recognized water-borne outbreak, and the only known extensive epidemic of this disease in a civilian population." Congress published it as House Document 74-376.
