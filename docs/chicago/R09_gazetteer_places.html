R09 — A GM's gazetteer of 1930s Chicago: the working map

  • Lane: R09 — places, neighbourhoods and institutions an investigator team would actually go to in 1930s Chicago (legends/hauntings → R08; ethnic community life → R15; Black Chicago → R14; cross-referenced where they overlap)
  • Date: 2026-09-26
  • Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent)
  • Entry format: place entries (### <Place> — <neighbourhood / address>), per the R09 prompt, not the event format.

Overview

106 place entries — a working map a GM can hand investigators: where the money, the law, the dead, the poor and the rich actually were in 1930s Chicago, each with a 1930s-accurate description, a reason to go, the steal-able odd detail, sources read this session, a divergence check against the 1916 break, a hook, and (per the owner's addendum) an Image line (Wikimedia Commons file page with its licence — public-domain period photos and postcards flagged in bold where found) plus a Google Maps link to the modern site. The strongest pattern: the 1930s city was full of brand-new or freshly-broken giants — the Board of Trade and its faceless Ceres (1930), the Merchandise Mart (world's largest building, 1930), the Civic Opera (opened six days after the crash, its builder ruined by 1932), the Stevens (world's largest hotel, in receivership), the Medinah Athletic Club (Shriners' Assyrian tower, bankrupt 1934), the Auditorium (dark all decade), the Loop "L" (in receivership). Several places bend directly on the setting's 1916 break: the Blackstone's "smoke-filled room" and the Coliseum's 1920 convention (in this setting they made Leonard Wood, not Harding), Dawes's LaSalle Street bank failing in 1932 (in this setting its chairman is an ex-president), Soldier Field and Navy Pier (named for a war America never fought here), FDR's 1937 Quarantine Speech at the Outer Drive bridge (no Nazi Germany to quarantine), Lincoln Square (German Chicago never persecuted, proud of a victorious Reich), Pilsen/Ukrainian Village (exiles of a surviving Austria-Hungary; no USSR), and the Oriental Institute digging at Megiddo under Ottoman permits. Corrections to the prompt: the Lexington Hotel was at 2135 S. Michigan; the 1909 crib fire was at the Intermediate Crib (~70 dead), not the 68th St crib. Cross-references: legends → R08, Black Chicago → R14, ethnic community life → R15, cults/churches → R05, fair → R04. Entries that overlap the 09-22 archive are tagged in their titles (W05, W06, W07, W08, W10, W12, W13, C05, C06, H08).

Part 1 — The Loop: money, hotels, towers

Chicago Board of Trade Building and the faceless Ceres — 141 W. Jackson Blvd, at the foot of LaSalle Street (the Loop) (also in 09-22 archive: W06)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Brand new. Holabird & Root's Art Deco tower opened June 9, 1930: 44 storeys, 604 ft, grey Indiana limestone under a copper pyramid roof, built for $11.3 million — and Chicago's tallest building from 1930 until 1965. Inside, a six-storey trading room held what was then the world's largest trading floor (19,000 sq ft) with octagonal pits for each commodity. It closes the vista down the LaSalle Street banking canyon, so every banker on LaSalle looks south at it. On the roof stands John H. Storrs's Ceres, goddess of grain: 31 ft tall, 6,500 lb of aluminium in 40 pieces, a sheaf of wheat in one hand and a bag of corn in the other.
  • Why an investigator goes there: to trace money — grain futures, commodity fortunes made and lost in the Depression, a victim's broker. The visitors' gallery over the pit is where you watch a crowd of men shout a price.
  • The odd detail: Ceres has no face. Storrs told the Tribune (May 4, 1930) that "the face, too, is suggested, inasmuch as there are no regular features" — from the street she would read only as a silhouette. Within weeks the soot blackened her, and she was not cleaned for 12 years. Beneath the trading floor ran over 2,700 miles of hidden telephone and telegraph wire.
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chicago_Board_Of_Trade_Building.jpg — modern photo, CC BY-SA 3.0, Joe Ravi, 2011 (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Chicago+Board+of+Trade+Building%2C+141+W+Jackson+Blvd%2C+Chicago
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Board_of_Trade_Building ; https://mysteriouschicago.com/why-the-ceres-statue-on-the-board-of-trade-has-no-face/
  • Confidence: High (dates, dimensions, Storrs quote via the Tribune as quoted by Mysterious Chicago).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (A commodity exchange in a world where German sanctions closed European markets would be even more feverish — the setting's cause of the Depression lands right on this floor.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A faceless harvest goddess raised over the grain exchange in the year the markets died. Someone in the pits believes she was consecrated, not just installed — and every bad harvest-futures crash lines up with an offering made on the roof.

Merchandise Mart — 222 Merchandise Mart Plaza, north bank of the river at Wolf Point (Near North / Loop edge) (also in 09-22 archive: W05)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Opened May 5, 1930 by Marshall Field & Co.: 4 million square feet, the largest building in the world until the Pentagon (1943). Graham, Anderson, Probst & White's Art Deco block — an 18-storey base with a 25-storey central tower — consolidated 13 Field warehouses into one wholesale city where buyers came to see showrooms. The site was once a trading post and the abandoned Wells Street Station of the Chicago & North Western. NBC opened radio studios on the 19th floor in October 1930, turning out up to 1,700 programmes a month, Amos 'n' Andy among them.
  • Why an investigator goes there: radio. A voice heard on a network broadcast, a transcription disc, a sound engineer who heard something on the line. Also wholesale records — who ordered what, shipped where.
  • The odd detail: The world's biggest building opened just as the wholesale trade it was built for collapsed; Field's could not fill it. (Joseph P. Kennedy bought it cheaply in 1945 — outside the window, but a nice irony.)
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Merchandise_Mart_080405.jpg — modern photo, CC BY-SA 3.0, User:JeremyA, 2008 (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Merchandise+Mart%2C+222+Merchandise+Mart+Plaza%2C+Chicago
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchandise_Mart
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A live broadcast from the 19th floor carries a voice nobody in the studio spoke. The NBC engineers keep the disc. Section 13 wants it.

The Stevens Hotel — 720 S. Michigan Ave (South Loop, facing Grant Park)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Opened May 2, 1927 as the largest hotel in the world: 3,000 rooms, about $30 million. Built by James W. Stevens and his son Ernest (the family also ran the Illinois Life Insurance Company and owned the Hotel La Salle), it sold itself as a "City Within a City": its own bowling alley, barber shop, movie theatre, ice-cream shop, drug store and a rooftop miniature-golf course, the "High-Ho Club".
  • Why an investigator goes there: conventions — every fraternal order, trade body and political gathering in the city books the Stevens; a thousand strangers in one building. And the ruin of a family.
  • The odd detail: The Depression broke the Stevens family. Illinois charged the owners with financial corruption; they were convicted of embezzlement, then the conviction was reversed unanimously on appeal. The hotel went into receivership and by the late 1930s was valued at about $7 million — under a quarter of its cost. (In 1942 the Army bought it for barracks — out of window.)
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Stevens_Hotel--Hilton_Chicago_2020-0444.jpg — modern photo, CC BY-SA 4.0, Paul R. Burley, 2020 (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Hilton+Chicago%2C+720+S+Michigan+Ave%2C+Chicago
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilton_Chicago
  • Confidence: High on hotel facts; Medium on the family details (father's fate, trial dates not confirmed here — see Leads).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (The 1942 Army purchase BENDS — no American entry into a world war in this timeline, so the Stevens may stay a hotel.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The biggest hotel in the world is in receivership, half empty, with 3,000 keys and no one checking who holds them — the perfect place for a society vampire's dinner to go unnoticed, or for a cult to rent a whole floor for a convention of "insurance men".

The Palmer House — 17 E. Monroe St (the Loop)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The third Palmer House, rebuilt 1925 to Holabird & Roche's design: a 25-storey hotel that opened with 2,268 rooms, briefly the largest in the world by room count. Still owned by the Palmer Estate through the 1930s (Conrad Hilton bought it in 1945). Its Empire Room was the showroom of the city's night life. Potter Palmer built the first hotel as a wedding present for his bride Bertha Honoré (1870).
  • Why an investigator goes there: old money meets the out-of-town expense account; the Palmer name opens doors on the Gold Coast.
  • The odd detail: The barber shop floor had silver dollars set into the tile in a diamond pattern — a legend from the old hotel. The chocolate brownie was invented at the second Palmer House for the 1893 fair.
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Palmer_House_%287184381863%29.jpg — modern photo, CC BY-SA 2.0, Teemu008, 2012 (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Palmer+House%2C+17+E+Monroe+St%2C+Chicago
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palmer_House_Hilton
  • Confidence: High on building facts; Medium on whether the silver-dollar floor survived into the 1925 building (article places it in the hotel's history without dating it clearly).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Bertha Palmer died in 1918; the estate that owns the hotel is run by trustees. A GM can put an old high-society patron in the Palmer family tree who never seems to age — and who keeps a permanent suite.

The Congress Hotel and Peacock Alley — 520 S. Michigan Ave (South Loop)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Opened 1893 for the Columbian Exposition, expanded 1902 and 1907; about 871 rooms. Through the 1930s it was run by hotel pioneer Ralph Hitz's National Hotel Management Company. A marble-lined underground passage, "Peacock Alley," connected it to the Auditorium Building across Congress Street. Part of the hotel hosted a Benny Goodman NBC radio show.
  • Why an investigator goes there: A hotel with a private tunnel to the theatre across the road is a way to move a person — or a body — without crossing a street.
  • The odd detail: In 1939 a guest, Adele Langer, leapt from a 13th-floor window with her two sons — a refugee family fleeing the occupation of Czechoslovakia.
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Congress_Plaza_Hotel_-Chicago-_October_2012.JPG — modern photo, CC BY-SA 3.0, IvoShandor, 2012 (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Congress+Plaza+Hotel%2C+520+S+Michigan+Ave%2C+Chicago
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_Plaza_Hotel
  • Confidence: High on the tunnel and the management; the Langer death is from Wikipedia (date not given there beyond 1939). UNVERIFIED: the often-repeated claim that FDR used the Congress as his 1932 convention HQ — the article does not confirm it.
  • Divergence check: Hotel holds. The Langer story BENDS — in this timeline there is no Nazi Germany, so a 1939 refugee family from Czechoslovakia needs a different reason to flee (Austria-Hungary survives as Germany's junior — a Czech flight from Habsburg police would fit).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Peacock Alley is the one tunnel in the Loop that everyone knows about and nobody thinks about. Something that cannot cross running water or a public street uses it nightly.

The Blackstone Hotel — 636 S. Michigan Ave at Balbo (South Loop)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Opened April 6, 1910; the "Hotel of Presidents" — at least twelve stayed there, Theodore Roosevelt, Wilson and FDR among them. In June 1920 Republican leaders met in a suite here to break the convention deadlock, and a journalist's phrase for it — the "smoke-filled room" — entered the language.
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is where deals are cut out of sight. Any political fixer from out of town books here.
  • The odd detail: In the real record the smoke-filled room chose Warren G. Harding, who beat the front-runner General Leonard Wood (and Governor Frank Lowden) for the nomination.
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:20080409_Blackstone_Hotel_Exterior2.JPG — modern photo, CC BY-SA 3.0, TonyTheTiger, 2008 (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Blackstone+Hotel%2C+636+S+Michigan+Ave%2C+Chicago
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackstone_Hotel
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — directly. In this setting Harding was never president and Leonard Wood was (1921–27). So in 1920 the smoke-filled room at the Blackstone chose Wood, not Harding — or the room never met. This is one of the few Chicago places where the 1916 break is visible in a hotel register.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The suite where the setting's 1920 nomination was fixed for Wood still has its guest book. Someone paid to have one night's page cut out. The Golden Circle is the obvious suspect; the Secret Service would like to know who else was in the room.

The Morrison Hotel — Madison & Clark, SE corner (the Loop)

  • What it was in the 1930s: A 1915 Marshall & Fox hotel with a 46-storey tower completed 1925 — 526 ft, the first building outside New York with more than 40 floors and for three decades the tallest hotel in the world. The Boston Oyster House restaurant was in the basement; the Terrace Casino nightclub opened in 1936 with Sophie Tucker. Jack Dempsey was a regular. Demolished 1965.
  • Why an investigator goes there: A great height in the middle of the Loop — a view, a window, a fall.
  • The odd detail: The site had held a Morrison Hotel since 1860 (21 rooms, burned in 1871). The name outlasted three buildings.
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Morrison_Hotel_Postcard.jpg — period postcard c. 1930–45, public domain (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Madison+St+and+Clark+St%2C+Chicago+%28site+of+the+Morrison+Hotel%2C+now+Chase+Tower+plaza%29
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morrison_Hotel_(Chicago)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The tallest hotel in the world has a top floor that the elevator operators don't take guests to after midnight.

The Hotel Sherman and the College Inn — Randolph & Clark, NW corner (the Loop, across from City Hall)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The fourth Sherman House on the site (1911, Holabird & Roche; enlarged 1920 and with a 23-floor annex in 1925). Home of the College Inn, the big-band room broadcast across the country. It was the headquarters of the Cook County Democratic Party until 1932, when the party moved its headquarters to the third floor of the Morrison Hotel (Madison & Clark) — so for most of the Kelly–Nash decade the machine's front office was at the Morrison, not here ⟨fixed: V_R09⟩. Radio station WLS began broadcasting from the hotel in April 1924.
  • Why an investigator goes there: Across the street from City Hall and, until 1932, home to the Democratic machine ⟨fixed: V_R09⟩ — the place to find a ward boss, a judge's fixer or the man who can make a coroner's verdict change.
  • The odd detail: A two-storey presidential suite sat on the roof; the Coolidges stayed in it. The 1938 NFL Draft was held here.
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hotel_Sherman%2C_Chicago%2C_Ill_%2872169%29_%281%29.jpg — Tichnor Bros. postcard c. 1930s, public domain (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=James+R.+Thompson+Center%2C+100+W+Randolph+St%2C+Chicago+%28site+of+the+Hotel+Sherman%29
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherman_House_Hotel
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. The Coolidge stay BENDS only in label: Coolidge was never president in this timeline, so he stayed as a private citizen (or not at all).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The machine's headquarters is a hotel full of strangers; the party keeps a room nobody rents. A ward committeeman meets "the Circle's man" there every Election Day.

The Lexington Hotel ("Capone's Castle") — 2135 S. Michigan Ave at 22nd St (Near South Side, the old Levee)

  • What it was in the 1930s: A ten-storey hotel built 1891–92 for the Columbian Exposition. Al Capone made it his headquarters and residence from July 1928 until his 1931 arrest, after the Metropole. After him the building slid downhill — later renamed the New Michigan Hotel and run as a 400-room brothel; closed 1980, demolished 1995.
  • Why an investigator goes there: In 1930–31 it is the Outfit's front office; after 1931 it is where the leftovers of Capone's staff still drink.
  • The odd detail: On April 21, 1986, Geraldo Rivera opened "Al Capone's vaults" in its basement on live television for 30 million viewers — and found a few empty bottles. The legend of what was sealed down there is older than the TV special.
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Lexington_Hotel_Chicago_%28Front%29.tif — historic photo dated 1906, public domain (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=2135+S+Michigan+Ave%2C+Chicago
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexington_Hotel_(Chicago)
  • Confidence: High. Note: the prompt had 2300 S. Michigan; Wikipedia gives 2135 S. Michigan — use 2135.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. Capone is already in the setting (the Secret Service "took him anyway").
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The vault was empty in 1986 because Section 13 emptied it in 1931, the week the Secret Service took Capone. The inventory is in a file nobody has read since. ⟨fixed: factions⟩

Part 2 — Law, death and the evidence room

Central Police Station (police headquarters) — 1121 S. State St at 11th (South Loop)

  • What it was in the 1930s: New in 1928, built on a 1925 bond issue: a 13–14-storey stone-and-buff-brick building designed by Charles W. Kallal, housing the Detective Bureau, the First Precinct, the Bureau of Records and Identification, the Vehicle Bureau, the superintendent's office and several branch courts (Domestic Relations, Boys' Court, Speeders' Court). A covered prisoner concourse and three dedicated prisoner elevators moved the arrested to cells and courtrooms out of public view. In 1938 the police bought the Northwestern crime lab and moved it here.
  • Why an investigator goes there: mugshots, fingerprint cards, arrest records, a detective's desk — and the lockup where your witness is held.
  • The odd detail: The 8th floor long housed the city's Film Review Board, which censored every movie shown in Chicago. Cost overruns of about $595,000 came from redesigns ordered mid-build by Commissioner Michael Hughes.
  • Image: none found with a clear free licence this session (see the Sources page and the Map link).
  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=1121+S+State+St%2C+Chicago
  • Sources: https://chicagology.com/skyscrapers/skyscrapers005/ (search summary also drew on https://www.chicagopolice.org/about/history/)
  • Confidence: High on opening and functions. Chicagology gives the address as 1111 S. State; the usual address is 1121 S. State. The Film Review Board's 8th-floor location is attested for later decades — UNVERIFIED for the 1930s.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Bureau of Identification's card files hold a set of fingerprints that have been taken three times since 1908 — from a man who looks thirty every time.

Maxwell Street Station ("Bloody Maxwell") — 943/949 W. Maxwell St at Morgan (Near West Side)

  • What it was in the 1930s: A red-brick-and-Joliet-limestone Romanesque fortress built in 1888 (Edbrooke & Burnham), with walls three feet thick at the base — part of the city's answer to Haymarket. In 1906 the Tribune called the district "Bloody Maxwell," "the Wickedest Police District in the World." It policed the crowded immigrant streets around the Maxwell Street market — Irish, German, Polish, Italian, Greek, Russian and Jewish.
  • Why an investigator goes there: Anything that happens in the market, the Jewish West Side, Greektown or the Taylor Street Italians gets booked here.
  • The odd detail: Its front was the precinct house of TV's Hill Street Blues (1981–87).
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Precinct.jpg — modern photo taken 2005 (uploaded 2008), released to the public domain by its author Andy Lambert ⟨fixed: V_R09⟩ (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=943+W+Maxwell+St%2C+Chicago
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7th_District_Police_Station
  • Confidence: High (address given variously as 943 and 949 W. Maxwell).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened (building); holds for the 1930s.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Built as a fortress after Haymarket, and never quite trusted by its own neighbourhood. The Kabbalah of the West Side keep a list of which desk sergeants can be asked about "the other kind of case".

Cook County Jail and the Criminal Courts Building ("26th and Cal") — 26th St & California Ave (South Lawndale / Little Village)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Brand new. In 1929 the county closed the old Hubbard Street jail and courts downtown and moved its prisoners and criminal trials to 26th and California, next to the city's House of Correction (the Bridewell) on the same grounds. The Criminal Courts Building is a Neoclassical/Art Deco courthouse of the late 1920s. Illinois's electric chair was here: used 67 times between 1928 and 1962.
  • Why an investigator goes there: arraignments, trials, visiting a prisoner, watching a jury — and executions, which the press attended.
  • The odd detail: Its nicknames in later decades, "26th and Cal" and "Hotel California"; in the 1930s it is simply the new county jail out on the edge of the Czech southwest side.
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cook_County_Jail_complex%2C_Chicago.jpg — modern photo, CC BY 2.0, David Wilson, 2005 (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Cook+County+Criminal+Courts+Building%2C+2650+S+California+Ave%2C+Chicago
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cook_County_Jail
  • Confidence: High on 1929 move and electric chair count; Medium on exact courthouse opening date (1929 — see Leads).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A man is due for the chair on a night of the December 1936 eclipse. Someone wants him to die on schedule; someone else needs him alive past midnight.

Stateville Penitentiary and its roundhouses — Crest Hill, about 2 miles north of Joliet

  • What it was in the 1930s: Built from 1917 and opened 1925 to replace old Joliet. Its signature is the "roundhouse" — a panopticon cellhouse with an armed guard tower in the centre of an open floor ringed by tiers of cells, so every cell faces the watcher. Leopold and Loeb were moved here from Joliet and ran the "Stateville Correspondence School" for inmates. Richard Loeb was killed in a shower room on January 28, 1936, by inmate James Day with a straight razor; Day claimed Loeb had propositioned him, which Leopold and the prison's Catholic chaplain disputed. Bootlegger Roger Touhy was held here too (framed in 1934; his famous escape was 1942).
  • Why an investigator goes there: to question a lifer; to see who visits whom.
  • The odd detail: The roundhouse design means a prisoner is never out of sight — and never sees who is watching.

  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Stateville+Correctional+Center%2C+Crest+Hill%2C+Illinois
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stateville_Correctional_Center ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_and_Loeb

Part 3 — Towertown and the Near North

Towertown, the Dill Pickle Club and Bughouse Square — Tooker Place / Washington Square, 901 N. Clark St (Near North Side)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Towertown, named for the Water Tower, was Chicago's bohemia — studios, tearooms, salons, speakeasies and forums west of the Gold Coast. At its heart was Washington Square Park ("Bughouse Square"), the city's oldest small park (1842), where from the 1910s to the 1960s soapbox orators argued everything from free love to revolution; the orators even elected their own "king".   • The odd detail: Jones was arrested for selling liquor in 1931 and collected 150 citations in one winter; the club was finally shut in 1933 under a rarely used law against dance halls near churches. Bughouse Square was also a known meeting place for gay men.

Part 6 — Underground and on the water

The freight tunnels — 60 miles under the Loop, 40 ft down

  • What it was in the 1930s: A hidden railway: about 60 miles of two-foot-gauge electric railway dug from 1899, in revenue service from August 15, 1906, run by the Chicago Tunnel Company. Egg-shaped tunnels 7½ ft high and 6 ft wide carried merchandise, coal in and ash out for downtown buildings, and excavation spoil. In 1914 it had 132 locomotives and more than 2,000 cars; in 1929 it still ran 200–300 train movements a day. Part 8 — The dead: cemeteries

Graceland Cemetery — 4001 N. Clark St (Uptown / Lakeview edge)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The North Side's great garden cemetery, founded 1860 by Thomas Barbour Bryan: about 121 acres around a reflecting lake, landscaped for strolling. The old money of the city lies here — Potter and Bertha Palmer, Marshall Field, Daniel Burnham, Louis Sullivan (with his Getty Tomb and Ryerson mausoleum nearby) — and Allan Pinkerton, the detective. Jack Johnson, the heavyweight champion, was buried here in 1946 (out of window). Lorado Taft's hooded bronze "Eternal Silence" (1909) stands over the Graves family plot.
  • Why an investigator goes there: to see who visits the rich dead; family plots show family trees.
  • The odd detail: From its founding Graceland was the only white-owned cemetery in Chicago that was integrated. Legend says looking into the face of "Eternal Silence" shows you your own death (folklore — R08's lane).

  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graceland_Cemetery

  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Allan Pinkerton ran the Union's first "secret service" before Col. Wood founded the real one in 1865. His grave here makes Graceland the place where the Secret Service's rival founding story is buried — and a Pinkerton heir who wants it dug up.

Rosehill Cemetery — 5800 N. Ravenswood Ave (Ravenswood / West Ridge, North Side)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Chicago's largest cemetery (350 acres) and its first private one (1859), entered through W. W. Boyington's castellated Gothic gate of Lemont limestone — by the architect of the Water Tower. The Rosehill Mausoleum (1914), the largest in Chicago, has Tiffany windows. Sixteen Union generals, Richard Sears, Aaron Montgomery Ward and Oscar Mayer lie here — and later Charles G. Dawes (1951).
  • Why an investigator goes there: mausoleums (a crypt is a locked room); the German and Swedish North Side's dead.
  • The odd detail: The name may be a clerk's error for "Roe's Hill" (after settler Hiram Roe) — or from wild white roses.
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rosehill_cemetery_entrance_Chicago_%28cropped%29.jpg — Boyington's gate, CC0, MauraWen, 2025 (Wikimedia Commons)
  • Map (modern site): https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Rosehill+Cemetery%2C+5800+N+Ravenswood+Ave%2C+Chicago
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosehill_Cemetery
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds. Note: in this setting Dawes is an ex-president, so his eventual tomb here would be a presidential grave in a Chicago cemetery.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The mausoleum's 1914 crypts were sold before the Tiffany glass went in. One family bought a crypt and a key to the service corridor.

Calvary Cemetery — 301 Chicago Ave, Evanston (on the lake at the Chicago city line)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The archdiocese's old lakeside Catholic cemetery, consecrated on All Souls' Day, November 2, 1859, by Bishop James Duggan, behind James J. Egan's arched limestone gate; over 50,000 graves. Irish Chicago lies here — Charles Comiskey (White Sox founder, died 1931), congressmen, governors, and victims of the Iroquois fire and the Eastland.
  • Why an investigator goes there: Irish-Catholic funerals; the edge of the city, where Sheridan Road bends along the lake.
  • The odd detail: The graves run down almost to the water; the lake is on the other side of the road. Local legend tells of a drowned man walking out of the lake toward the gate (folklore, UNVERIFIED — R08's lane).

  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The IHS's Chicago dead are buried at Calvary under ordinary names, facing east over the water.

Mount Carmel Cemetery — Wolf Rd & Roosevelt Rd, Hillside (west suburbs)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The Catholic cemetery of the Italian West Side, founded 1901 (214 acres today, 226,000+ burials). Its centrepiece is the Bishops' Mausoleum (1905–12, William J. Brinkmann), a Romanesque chapel holding Chicago's bishops and archbishops, Archbishop Feehan first. Italian families put photographs of the dead on the headstones. Dean O'Banion, killed in his flower shop in 1924, is here; later Capone, Nitti and Giancana.
  • Why an investigator goes there: gangland funerals — the Outfit buries its own here, and everyone watches who comes.
  • The odd detail: From 1906 to 1934 the Chicago, Aurora & Elgin Railroad ran funeral trains to the cemetery, with a shuttle service from downtown [UNVERIFIED — V_R09] — the line quit in the middle of the decade.

  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The archbishops' mausoleum and the gangsters' graves share one cemetery. The IHS and the Outfit meet on neutral ground at funerals — the only place both come unarmed.

Oak Woods Cemetery and the Confederate Mound — 1035 E. 67th St (Greater Grand Crossing, South Side)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Chartered February 12, 1853, laid out by Adolph Strauch, 183 acres. After the Civil War thousands of Confederate prisoners who died at Camp Douglas (the Union prison camp on the South Side) were reburied here in the Confederate Mound; its monument lists more than 4,000 names — called the largest documented mass grave in the Western Hemisphere. The cemetery stayed racially segregated until September 1963 — yet Ida B. Wells was buried here in 1931.
  • Why an investigator goes there: South Side funerals; the Southern dead in a Northern city.
  • The odd detail: A mass grave of Confederates sits in what became the heart of Black Chicago.
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Oak_Woods_Cemetery_Main_Building_Chapel.JPG — chapel, CC BY-SA 4.0, MrHarman, 2012 (Wikimedia Commons)

  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened; holds. Ties to the setting's Golden Circle — the Knights of the Golden Circle were accused of plotting a Camp Douglas breakout in 1864 (UNVERIFIED here; see R05/R01 or Leads).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Golden Circle keeps its Chicago ritual at the Confederate Mound — 4,000 names, a Circle-era plot to free them, and a monument raised in 1895 by men who never forgave the Union.

Bohemian National Cemetery — 5255 N. Pulaski Rd (North Park, Northwest Side)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Founded 1877 by Czech Chicagoans after a Czech Catholic woman, Marie Silhanek, was refused burial in Catholic cemeteries because she had supposedly missed her Easter Duty. The community bought its own land — a freethinkers' cemetery under Czech, not Church, control. A limestone gatehouse; a columbarium whose niches hold photographs and mementos; victims of the Iroquois fire and the Eastland. Mayor Anton Cermak, shot in Miami in 1933, is entombed here.
  • Why an investigator goes there: Czech Chicago (Pilsen, Little Village, Cicero, Berwyn); Cermak's tomb; a burial ground where priests have no say.
  • The odd detail: The mayor killed beside the President lies in a cemetery born from a quarrel with the Church.

German Waldheim (Forest Home) and the Haymarket Martyrs' Monument — Des Plaines Ave, Forest Park (west suburbs)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The German freethinkers' Waldheim Cemetery, where the five executed or dead Haymarket defendants — August Spies, Adolph Fischer, George Engel, Louis Lingg and Albert Parsons — were buried; Michael Schwab (1898) and Oscar Neebe (1916) joined them. Over them stands Albert Weinert's monument, dedicated June 25, 1893: a bronze Justice over a fallen worker on a 16-ft granite shaft, carved with Spies's last words: "The day will come when our silence will be more powerful than the voices you are throttling today." Radicals chose to be buried near it — the ground became a pilgrimage site.
  • Why an investigator goes there: anarchists, labour men and freethinkers gather here; a radical's funeral is a roll call.
  • The odd detail: An 1892 time capsule at the monument's base has never been found; a 2016 dig turned up an urn and a concrete box instead.

Resurrection Cemetery — 7201 Archer Ave, Justice (southwest suburbs) (also in 09-22 archive: C06)

  • What it was in the 1930s: A Catholic cemetery of the archdiocese on Archer Avenue, consecrated 1904, serving the Polish and Catholic Southwest Side; Eastland victims lie here. Archer Avenue ran out past it through the roadhouse and dance-hall country of the southwest suburbs.
  • Why an investigator goes there: the road itself — Archer Avenue at night, the ballrooms, the cemetery gates.
  • The odd detail: Resurrection Mary, the hitchhiking girl in a white dress who vanishes at the cemetery gates, is Chicago's best-known ghost; the legend's earliest sightings are usually placed in the 1930s (R08 covers the legend; the 09-22 archive has it as C06).

Part 9 — Neighbourhoods: the Near North and the West Side

The Gold Coast — Lake Shore Drive to Clark St, Oak St to North Ave (Near North Side)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The city's richest few blocks. It began in 1882 when Potter Palmer filled a swamp on the lakefront and built his 42-room "castle" (1350 N. Lake Shore Drive, by Henry Ives Cobb and Charles Sumner Frost) — society followed him north from Prairie Avenue. Astor Street's townhouses and the new lakefront apartment towers held the families that ran the banks, the stores and the opera. In 1929 sociologist Harvey Zorbaugh's The Gold Coast and the Slum made the neighbourhood famous for its contrast: the richest street in Chicago a few blocks from Little Sicily's "Death Corner".
  • Why an investigator goes there: high society — the old families, the dinner parties, the servants who see everything. The Catholic archbishop's residence stands at the north end (1555 N. State Pkwy — UNVERIFIED from a page read; see Leads).
  • The odd detail: The Palmer castle had no exterior doorknobs — you could only be let in (a well-known story, UNVERIFIED here). In the 1930s Bertha Palmer's heirs still owned it.
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Palmer_Castle_Chicago.jpg — the Potter Palmer 'castle', historic photo, public domain; also https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Residence_of_Mr._Potter_Palmer,_Chicago.jpg (1900, public domain) (Wikimedia Commons)

  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Coast_Historic_District_(Chicago) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabrini–Green_Homes
  • Confidence: High on history; items marked unverified.

  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The setting's "vampires conspiring in high society" have an address: a Lake Shore Drive house built so you cannot open it from outside. Zorbaugh's 1929 field notes on the Gold Coast name a family that keeps odd hours.

Streeterville — east of Michigan Ave, north of the river to Oak St (Near North lakefront)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Land that did not exist in 1880. George Wellington "Cap" Streeter claimed his steamboat grounded on a sandbar here in the late 1880s; as landfill for Lake Shore Drive built up about 186 acres, he declared it the "District of Lake Michigan", an independent territory, and fought the shoreline millionaires for decades — with gun battles against police in the Thompson era — until the courts ruled against him in 1918; he died in 1921. In the 1920s boom it became luxury apartments, the Drake Hotel, the Medinah Athletic Club and Northwestern's downtown campus (law school, medical school — and the crime lab).
  • Why an investigator goes there: Northwestern's medical and law schools, the hospitals, the rich apartment towers along the lake.
  • The odd detail: A squatter's kingdom became the most valuable land in the city.

Little Sicily, "Little Hell" and Death Corner — Oak St & Milton Ave (now Cleveland Ave) (Near North Side, west of the Gold Coast)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Low ground along the North Branch, first Swedish and Irish shanties, nicknamed "Little Hell" for a nearby gas works that threw visible flames and fumes; by the early 1900s a dense Sicilian colony, "Little Sicily". In the 1920s it was known for poverty and crime. A 1931 map marked the corner of Oak Street and Milton Avenue as "Death Corner" — the site of about 50 murders, many attributed to Black Hand extortion and vendetta. Zorbaugh's 1929 study set it against the Gold Coast a few blocks east. (Cabrini–Green was built over it from 1942.)
  • Why an investigator goes there: extortion letters, vendettas, a witness who won't talk — the Black Hand's Chicago home.
  • The odd detail: "Little Hell" was named for real fire — the gas-works flare lit the neighbourhood at night.
  • Image: none found with a clear free licence this session (see the Sources page and the Map link).

  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabrini–Green_Homes ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_North_Side,_Chicago

Taylor Street (Little Italy) and Hull House — Taylor St around Halsted, 800 S. Halsted St (Near West Side)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The largest Italian neighbourhood in the city, around Taylor Street, with Our Lady of Pompeii (1911) and Mother Cabrini's Holy Guardian Angel (1898/99), the first Italian parish. By 1927 Italians in Chicago owned 500 groceries, 257 restaurants and 240 pastry shops. The Genna brothers ran their Prohibition alcohol-cooking empire from these streets. At its edge stood Hull House (800 S. Halsted) — Jane Addams's settlement, founded September 18, 1889, grown to 13 buildings by 1907: clubs, classes, a gym, a theatre, the city's first public playground. Addams died May 21, 1935.
  • Why an investigator goes there: Hull House's residents know every family on the West Side, and keep records; the Italians of Taylor Street know the Outfit.
  • The odd detail: In 1913 a rumour swept the West Side that a "Devil Baby" had been born and was hidden at Hull House; crowds came asking to see it, and Addams later wrote about it (UNVERIFIED this session — R08's lane; see Leads).

The Greek Delta (old Greektown) — the triangle of Halsted St, Blue Island Ave and Harrison St (Near West Side)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The "Greek Delta", a triangle north and west of Hull House, home by 1930 to over 30,000 Greeks, foreign-born and native. Holy Trinity (1897) was the first Greek Orthodox church in the Midwest, and its Socrates School (1908) a pioneering parochial school. Greeks dominated the fruit trade — the Tribune said in 1895 they had "almost run the Italians out of the fruit business". Coffeehouses, restaurants, candy makers. (The Delta was destroyed for the UIC campus and the expressway around 1960.)
  • Why an investigator goes there: coffeehouse gossip, fruit peddlers who cover the whole city, an Orthodox priest's view.
  • The odd detail: The fruit and candy trade put Greek pushcarts on every street in Chicago — perfect eyes.

Maxwell Street Market — Maxwell St at Halsted, just south of Roosevelt Rd (Near West Side)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Chicago's great open-air market, born as a Jewish pushcart market of Eastern European immigrants in the 1880s, officially recognised by the city in 1912, busiest on Sundays; locals called the area "Jew Town". Everything was sold: clothes, produce, tools, used goods, stolen goods. Benny Goodman grew up nearby (born 1909). Watched over by the Maxwell Street police station.
  • Why an investigator goes there: to buy anything with no questions — a gun, a coat, a stolen item — or to find who sold it.
  • The odd detail: Merchants were already paying street musicians to draw crowds, the start of the Maxwell Street blues scene that bloomed after the war.

Pilsen — 18th St west of Halsted (Lower West Side)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Named for the Bohemian city of Plzeň and formally founded in 1878; one of the few areas with buildings older than the 1871 fire. Settled by Czechs (who replaced Germans), and by 1934 a working-class neighbourhood of Czechs, Croatians, Poles and Slovaks — Slovenes and Austrians too, nearly all from Austria-Hungary; Lithuanians as well. (The Mexican Pilsen came only in the 1960s.) Its lodges, gymnastic Sokol halls and beer halls were the heart of Czech Chicago before the move west to Little Village, Cicero and Berwyn.
  • Why an investigator goes there: Czech lodges and freethinkers, Cermak's people; Slavic factory workers; the lumber district on the river.
  • The odd detail: A neighbourhood of Austria-Hungary's subject peoples in the middle of Chicago — exactly the people whose homelands the 1916 break keeps under Vienna.

Part 10 — Neighbourhoods: the stockyards and the South Side

The Union Stock Yards and Packingtown — Halsted to Ashland, Pershing Rd (39th) to 47th St (New City, South Side)

  • What it was in the 1930s: A square mile of pens, chutes, railroad sidings and packing houses — Swift, Armour and the rest — where the Midwest's animals came to die. You entered through the limestone Stone Gate (1877) on Exchange Avenue; the Stockyard Inn fed the buyers; the smell covered the South Side when the wind was wrong. On May 19, 1934, fire destroyed about 90 percent of the yards, including the Exchange Building and the Exposition building — about $6 million in damage, one employee and 8,000 cattle dead. The International Amphitheatre rose at 42nd & Halsted in 1934 for the International Live Stock Exposition.
  • Why an investigator goes there: a place where bodies can disappear (rendering, tanks, rail cars); thousands of workers on shifts; union organisers.
  • The odd detail: The 1934 fire cut power, gas and phones to the district (the 09-22 archive has this as H02/H03) — a whole district dark for a night.

Back of the Yards — west and south of the stockyards, around Ashland Ave & 47th St (New City)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The packinghouse workers' neighbourhood, Upton Sinclair's The Jungle (1906): Lithuanians, Poles, Slovaks, Bohemians and Moravians, the Irish earlier — crowded frame houses by Bubbly Creek (the stockyards' fouled arm of the river) and a church for every nation. In 1939 Saul Alinsky and park director Joseph Meegan founded the Back of the Yards Neighborhood Council — the start of American community organising (Bishop Bernard Sheil backed it — UNVERIFIED from page read).
  • Why an investigator goes there: the workers who see what the packers do at night; the parish priests of half a dozen nationalities.
  • The odd detail: Every nationality had its own church within a few blocks — you could hear Mass in six languages on one Sunday.

Bridgeport — along the South Branch west of the stockyards to Halsted and 39th (South Side)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Chicago's oldest Irish neighbourhood, settled in the 1830s–40s by Irish canal diggers on the Illinois & Michigan Canal; also Lithuanian, Polish (St. Mary of Perpetual Help, St. Barbara) and Italian. It is the machine's home ground: Mayor Edward J. Kelly (1933–47) was from Bridgeport, and so were the later Daleys — the young Richard J. Daley was a ward politician here in the 1930s.
  • Why an investigator goes there: the Democratic machine's roots — precinct captains, athletic clubs, patronage jobs.
  • The odd detail: Five of the city's mayors came from this one neighbourhood.

The Bahá'í House of Worship — 100 Linden Ave, Wilmette (North Shore)

  • What it was in the 1930s: A temple being built all decade. ʻAbdu'l-Bahá laid the cornerstone in 1912; Foundation Hall was done by 1922; the superstructure and dome were finished in 1931 (George A. Fuller Co.), and from 1932 John J. Earley's workmen began casting the lace-like concrete ornament that covers it — not finished outside until January 1943, dedicated 1953. Architect Louis Bourgeois (died 1930) built the whole design around nine: nine sides, nine entrances, nine alcoves, nine dome sections, nine fountains; the ornament mixes the cross, the Star of David, the star and crescent and other symbols.
  • Why an investigator goes there: a new faith of universal unity on the North Shore; scaffolding and a half-finished dome visible from the lake.
  • The odd detail: Through the whole Depression, a white temple covered in the symbols of every faith rose slowly on the lakeshore — dome done, walls still bare concrete.
