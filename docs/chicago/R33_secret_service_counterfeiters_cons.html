R33 — Counterfeiters, forgers, con men, swindlers and the Secret Service's real work (Chicago & the Midwest, 1925–1940)

Completed 2026-09-27 by a follow-up pass; 22 entries; fact-checked 2026-09-27 (V_R33).

  • Lane: R33 — the Secret Service's bread and butter (counterfeiting, cheque and bond forgery, presidential protection) and the swindlers, con men and mail-fraud artists of Chicago and the Midwest, 1925–1940.
  • Date: 2026-09-26
  • Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent)
  • Cross-references (not repeated here): R01 (Frank J. Wilson and the ledger; O'Hare), R17 (O'Hare/Wilson/Rogers; the Klutas gang's counterfeit fives), R18 (Moran as chief; Treasury BARs: ATU, Narcotics), R03 (Insull; Cermak; the 1932 conventions; the Quarantine Speech), R08 ("Yellow Kid" Weil — short entry), R05 (fake mediums), R06 (Harry Powers).
  • Faction note (per the corrected brief): the Secret Service ("the old guard", Treasury, IHS's ally since 1866) and FBI Section 13 (K. Edgar Hoover's Bureau) are two separate, rival federal factions.

Overview

Most of this chapter comes from the Service's own paper trail as the Midwest's newspapers printed it: Indiana's digitised papers (Hoosier State Chronicles) carry the wire stories out of Chicago that the big-city archives lock away. The best find is a name: Capt. Thomas J. Callaghan, the Secret Service's senior man in Chicago from at least 1931 to 1939. He led the December 1933 raid on a northwest-side cottage that held $500,000 in fake notes. In January 1933 he announced that a fake German count, "Enrique von Buelow" (Henry Dechow), had brought about $2,000,000 in notes printed in Berlin into the country and sold them to Chicago racketeers at 30 cents on the dollar. In 1939 he traced "Lustig money" buried on a golf course. And in March 1932 Al Capone offered to hunt the Lindbergh baby "accompanied everywhere by Thomas Callaghan" of the Secret Service. For a faction that "knew what Capone was and took him anyway", no real detail fits better. Around Callaghan: the Indianapolis office's line of succession (Halls → Mazey → Gallagher/Loy) and its paperwork war against forged bonus and WPA cheques. There are country bankers who printed their losses back after the 1932–33 closings, and the Midwest's great swindles: Oscar Hartzell's Drake estate (a Sioux City conviction in 1933, then a Chicago trial of 43 defendants in 1935–36), "Yellow Kid" Weil's Mexican-oil stings, a Springfield "aid society" that sold worthless policies to 130,000 old people, and Kansas's forged-bond scandal. On presidential protection, the Service shows up as "members of the secret service" in Hoover's 1931 Indianapolis visit and as one grazed hand at Bayfront Park. Nothing specific was found on the Service's arrangements for the 1937 Quarantine Speech. No Midwest spiritualist mail-fraud prosecution was found in the papers searched (see Leads).

A. The Secret Service itself

Chief Frank J. Wilson's "Know Your Money" campaign — 1937–1943

  • Where: Nationwide from the Treasury, Washington; delivered through schools, merchants and banks in every field district (Chicago and Indianapolis among them).
  • What happened: Frank J. Wilson — the Intelligence Unit man who built the Capone tax case (see R01, R17) — became Chief of the Secret Service after W. H. Moran (see R18) and made crime prevention the Service's anti-counterfeiting weapon. Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau Jr. approved the program and authorised a booklet and a Secret Service motion picture, both titled Know Your Money (the film is catalogued as a 1939 production: a grocer's alertness catches a counterfeiting gang; the Treasury Building and the currency presses appear on screen). The 1943 edition of the booklet claims victims of counterfeit passers fell from 102,800 (1933–36) to 5,980 (1942) and 2,877 (1943) — "a 97 percent drop" in victims and in losses — and that juvenile counterfeiting arrests fell "nearly 60 percent." Its public lessons: study the bills you receive; compare a suspect bill with a genuine one of the same type; check the portrait, the seal's saw-teeth (sharp and regular on a genuine bill), the silk threads in the paper; and memorise the portraits (Washington $1, Jefferson $2, Lincoln $5, Hamilton $10, Jackson $20, Grant $50, Franklin $100). It warns that "a $1 bill might be altered to represent $10" — the raised note.
  • The odd detail: the Secret Service fought the counterfeiters by turning every shopkeeper and schoolchild into an examiner — the booklet even prints the penalty (up to $500 or three years) for knowing of counterfeiting and not reporting it.
  • People: Frank J. Wilson — Chief (Wikipedia gives 1936–47; the brief's 1937–46 is the commonly cited span — the exact start is not settled from pages read); Henry Morgenthau Jr. — Treasury Secretary from Nov 1933.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/stream/knowyourmoney1943depa/knowyourmoney1943depa_djvu.txt ; https://archive.org/details/gov.archives.arc.13214 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_J._Wilson
  • Confidence: High on the booklet's figures and the film's description (read this session). The campaign's exact launch month and the number of school lectures were not found — UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (Morgenthau as Roosevelt's Treasury Secretary holds; Roosevelt is president in the setting.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Know Your Money" is the Secret Service's perfect public cover — a schoolroom lecture circuit that lets agents walk into every town hall and high school in the Midwest, look at every face, and hand out a booklet teaching people to look closely at things that are almost right. The old guard is training a nation of spotters without telling it what else to spot.

The Secret Service's own count, fiscal 1937–38: $619,290 in bad notes seized — August 1938

  • Where: Treasury annual report, summarised in the Walkerton (Ind.) Independent, 25 Aug 1938, p. 2.
  • What happened: "Chief Frank J. Wilson of the secret service reported that counterfeit notes aggregating $619,290 were seized during the 1938 fiscal year, as compared with $654,787 in the preceding year." Counterfeit coins confiscated: $54,423 in fiscal 1938 against $67,393 in 1937. (Fiscal years ended 30 June.)
  • The odd detail: the coin figure — tens of thousands of dollars of false coins a year — is the forgotten half of the trade: lead and alloy halves and quarters, cast in kitchens (see the Muncie half-dollars below).
  • People: Frank J. Wilson — Chief.
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=WLKI19380825.1.2 (Hoosier State Chronicles; OCR text read via the page's XML view)
  • Confidence: High (quoted from the page's OCR).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a GM's scale bar: roughly $600,000 a year in seized paper nationwide. A single Chicago plant turning out $50,000 is a national case — big enough to pull the Chief himself to the city.

An Indianapolis agent's week: counterfeit tens at the 500 — June 1938

  • Where: Indianapolis; taverns along South Illinois Street; the Secret Service office in Indianapolis.
  • What happened: The Indianapolis Times of 6 June 1938 reported that the passing of counterfeit money during the 500-mile race week "was at a minimum," per George B. Loy of the U.S. Secret Service office there: only 12 counterfeit $10 bills had been turned in since the race, most passed "at taverns along S. Illinois St.", and they were "the same type recently passed in New York." On 25 May 1938 another agent, Julian T. Baber, told the Indiana Bankers Association that counterfeit circulation was "steadily decreasing" because of public co-operation, and urged that high school and college students be taught to tell genuine from false.
  • The odd detail: the passers work the big crowd — race week, a fair, a convention — and the Service counts the bills afterwards, like a tide line.
  • People: George B. Loy — Secret Service, Indianapolis office; Julian T. Baber — Secret Service agent, Indiana.
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19380606.1.3 ; https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19380525.1.5
  • Confidence: High (OCR read this session).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Service's field men are few and known by name in their districts (Loy in Indianapolis). A player agent in Chicago is a public figure at the Bankers' Association lunch — and a private one at night.

Capt. Thomas E. Halls — the Indiana chief who posed as a Chicago doctor — reported 14 Feb 1936

  • Where: 2825 Ruckle St., Indianapolis (retirement home); the case in Chicago (date not given).
  • What happened: On his 91st birthday the Indianapolis Times ran a note on Capt. Thomas E. Halls, "formerly chief of the Indiana branch of the United States Secret Service." He blamed his health on a bullet he said went through him when he was ambushed hunting "the gang" — the paper says the Jesse James gang — in Kansas. "Once he set up as a physician in Chicago to catch a counterfeit gang. He got an emergency maternity case, had to admit he wasn't a physician and narrowly missed being prosecuted."
  • The odd detail: an undercover Secret Service man in a Chicago doctor's office, found out by a woman in labour.
  • People: Capt. Thomas E. Halls — retired chief of the Service's Indiana branch, detective 50+ years.
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19360214.1.5
  • Confidence: Medium — it is the old man's own story as a newspaper told it; the Jesse James claim and the Chicago date are UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened (as reported).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a living veteran of the Service's founding generation, still in Indianapolis in the 1930s: the players' first stop for what the old guard knew before the files were cleaned. He remembers the "counterfeit gang" in Chicago — and what it was really printing.

Capt. Thomas J. Callaghan — the Secret Service's man in Chicago — 1931–1939

  • Where: Chicago (the office's street address was not found — see Leads); his cases reach Gary, Newark and a golf course.
  • What happened: In the Midwest's papers of the 1930s, the Secret Service in Chicago has one face. "Captain Thomas J. Callaghan of the United States secret service" was called in by Gary police in June 1931. He announced the Dechow/"von Buelow" counterfeiting plot from Chicago in January 1933. He led police and agents in the December 1933 raid on a northwest-side counterfeit plant. In March 1939 the Indianapolis Times called him "ace investigator for the U. S. secret service" when he traced $17,880 in buried fake $20s to "Lustig money" (entries below). The papers spell him both Callaghan and Callahan, and they call him "Captain", a title the Service's senior field men carried (compare Capt. Thomas E. Halls, above). Whether he was formally the Chicago district's agent in charge is not stated in the pages read.
  • The odd detail: In 1932 Al Capone named him personally as the escort he would accept (next entry). The Chicago mob's most famous prisoner knew the Secret Service captain by name.
  • People: Capt. Thomas J. Callaghan — U.S. Secret Service, Chicago.
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=DCDD19310612.1.4 ; https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19330104.1.1 ; https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=DCDD19331207.1.1 ; https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19390309.1.3
  • Confidence: High that he was the Secret Service's leading investigator in Chicago in 1931–39 (four papers read). His formal title, his office address and his years of service are UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Callaghan is ready-made as the old guard's Chicago captain: a counterfeit-man by trade, known to Capone by name, and the one agent who has seen what the Berlin notes carry besides ink. Section 13's men in the Bankers Building would like to know why Capone asked for him.

Capone's offer: find the Lindbergh baby, "accompanied everywhere by Thomas Callaghan" — March 1932

  • Where: Cook County Jail, Chicago (Capone was held there awaiting appeal; per R01), reported nationally in Arthur Brisbane's column.
  • What happened: Two weeks after the Lindbergh baby was taken (1 March 1932), Arthur Brisbane's syndicated column (Syracuse Journal, 17 March 1932) reported his talk with Al Capone. Capone believed that if he were let out of jail he could "connect with his various associates, or 'angles,' and seek information throughout the country." His terms: he would "put up a bond for his return, leaving his young brother in prison as a hostage", and he would make the search "accompanied everywhere by Thomas Callaghan, a skilled man of the United States secret service." The offer was not taken up. Capone went to federal prison in May 1932 (per R01).
  • The odd detail: the escort Capone proposed was not a policeman or a G-man but a named Secret Service captain, and he offered his own brother as a hostage.
  • People: Al Capone; Arthur Brisbane — Hearst columnist; Thomas Callaghan — Secret Service.
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=TSYJB19320317.1.1
  • Confidence: High that Brisbane printed it (OCR read). Whether Callaghan agreed, or was even asked, is UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the Lindbergh kidnapping and Capone's jailing both hold; Hoover is president in March 1932 in the setting as in the record).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): This is the brief's "it knew what Capone was and took him anyway" in a single newspaper paragraph. In the Secret Service's files the offer was not refused. It was accepted quietly, for a different missing child, and Callaghan walked beside Capone through three nights the papers never heard about.

Charles Mazey — sixteen years in Indianapolis, then St. Paul — 1926–1936

  • Where: the Federal Building, Indianapolis; from November 1936, the St. Paul district office.
  • What happened: On 28 Nov 1936 the Indianapolis Times reported that Charles Mazey, the Secret Service agent in charge in Indianapolis since the 1926 retirement of Capt. Thomas E. Halls (above), was leaving to take charge of the St. Paul district. His successor was Thomas A. Gallagher, five years with the bureau. Mazey was "credited with directing investigations which resulted in the breaking up of two big alleged counterfeiting gangs": the Pete Davitto gang at Terre Haute and the Dante Colza ring in Michigan City. In June 1936 he had warned veterans to watch their mail for bonus cheques (see C). By 1938–39 George B. Loy is named as the Indianapolis head (above, and C).
  • The odd detail: the old guard's line of succession in one small office runs Halls (Jesse James days) → Mazey → Gallagher → Loy, and Mazey's reward was St. Paul, the gangster haven.
  • People: Charles Mazey — Indianapolis agent in charge 1926–36, then St. Paul; Thomas A. Gallagher — successor; Pete Davitto, Dante Colza — counterfeit-ring leaders (as named).
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19361128.1.3 ; https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19360618.1.1
  • Confidence: High (OCR read). The Davitto and Colza cases themselves were not researched.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a posting ladder for Secret Service player characters. Indianapolis is the quiet office where you learn the trade; St. Paul, where the Twin Cities' police let the bank robbers rest, is where the Service sends a man it trusts.

"Members of the secret service": Hoover's Indianapolis visit — 15 June 1931

  • Where: Indianapolis: the Governor's mansion, the Marott hotel, a dinner at the state fairground.
  • What happened: The Indianapolis Times of 12 June 1931 previewed President Hoover's Monday visit (15 June). The presidential party would stay at the Governor's mansion and the Marott hotel and dine at the state fairground. "A corps of newspaper correspondents, photographers and members of the secret service" would travel with him, and "all of the streets around the mansion will be roped off and closed to traffic." That is the whole of the protective plan as the paper printed it.
  • The odd detail: the same page carried Capt. Callaghan's Gary counterfeiting case (next section). In 1931 the Service's two jobs, the President and the money, shared a column.
  • People: Herbert Hoover — President.
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19310612.1.15
  • Confidence: High on what the paper printed. The number and names of agents are UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — Hoover is president 1929–33 in the setting. (If the rest of his June 1931 Midwest trip is used: the real trip is usually described as including the Harding Memorial dedication at Marion, Ohio, which BENDS — Harding was never president. UNVERIFIED here, not read this session.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Roped off and closed to traffic" around the Governor's mansion for one night: a GM's template for what a presidential visit does to a Midwest city. It also gives any cult one known night, one known address and one known route.

Bayfront Park from the Service's side — the grazed hand — 15 February 1933

  • Where: Bayfront Park, Miami (Chicago's mayor was the one who died; see R01, R03, R04 for Cermak).
  • What happened: President-elect Roosevelt gave "an impromptu speech at night from the back of an open car." Giuseppe Zangara, about five feet tall, stood on "a wobbly bench" and fired five shots from a .32 revolver he had bought for $8 in a local pawn shop. Lillian Cross, standing on the same bench, "pushed up and twisted Zangara's shooting arm." Five people were hit, including Cermak (mortally) and a New York detective-bodyguard, William Sinnott. The one Secret Service agent recorded as hit, Bob Clark, took "a grazed hand, possibly caused by the bullet that struck Cermak." Roosevelt "cradled the mortally wounded Cermak in his arms as the car rushed to the hospital." Zangara was executed on 20 March 1933. The famous "I'm glad it was me, not you" was printed by the Chicago Tribune without a witness, and "most scholars doubt it was ever said."
  • The odd detail: the protection that night was one open car, night, a crowd on benches, and a housewife's handbag hand. The Service's own casualty was a graze.
  • People: Bob Clark — Secret Service agent; Lillian Cross — bystander; William Sinnott — NY detective; Giuseppe Zangara — shooter.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Zangara ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attempted_assassination_of_Franklin_D._Roosevelt
  • Confidence: High on the event. What the Service changed afterwards was NOT found in the pages read (UNVERIFIED; see Leads).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Roosevelt is president-elect in Feb 1933 in the setting).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Clark's grazed hand and the bullet "possibly" the one that struck Cermak is the Service's private question. Whose bullet reached the Chicago mayor, and why Chicago's own people (R01's Cermak-and-the-Outfit theory) keep saying it was meant for him.

No Secret Service for a candidate: Roosevelt at the Chicago convention — July 1932

  • Where: Chicago Stadium and the Chicago airport (see R03 for the convention and the flight).
  • What happened: The Secret Service took "full-time responsibility for presidential protection" in 1902, and the White House Police (founded 1922) "was fully integrated into the Secret Service in 1930." But Congress "authorized protection of major presidential and vice presidential candidates and nominees" only in 1968, after Robert Kennedy's assassination. So when Governor Roosevelt flew to Chicago in July 1932 to accept the nomination, he was a candidate, and the Service had no statutory duty to guard him. That is an inference from the 1968 date; the pages read do not describe who did guard him in Chicago.
  • The odd detail: the Service met Roosevelt as its charge only after the November election. Its first big night with him was Bayfront Park.
  • People: Franklin D. Roosevelt — nominee.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Secret_Service
  • Confidence: Medium. The 1902/1930/1968 dates were read; the conclusion for July 1932 is inferred, and who guarded him in Chicago is UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): in the gap between nomination and election, a candidate belongs to no federal faction. That is the window in which Section 13 could reach Roosevelt first, and the old guard has never forgiven the 1932 convention for it.

B. Counterfeiters

"Count Enrique von Buelow" and the notes printed in Berlin — January 1933 to February 1934

  • Where: Chicago (where the notes were passed and the ring broken); Newark, N.J. (the arrest); Berlin (where, per the Service, the notes were made).
  • What happened: On 4 Jan 1933, in a Chicago-datelined story, Capt. Thomas J. Callahan [sic] of the Secret Service revealed "a large international counterfeiting plot" after the arrest at Newark of Henry Dechow, "once known here as 'Count Enrique Dechow von Buelow.'" Posing under the title, Dechow had married a wealthy widow, Mrs. Lottie Brenner, in 1930; after the deception came out she filed divorce suits. About 25,000 counterfeit notes had been passed in Chicago and about $2,000,000 in fake currency brought into the country. The counterfeiting "was done in Berlin." Eight Chicago racketeers had bought $100,000 in counterfeit notes for $30,000. The agents recovered most of it, and the racketeers threatened retaliation. A year later (1 Feb 1934) a syndicated story on the Service's war on the counterfeiting wave said von Buelow "is said to have had $90,000 in bogus $100 bills when he was taken into custody," and that the ring was among "the biggest international rings ever to operate in this country."
  • The odd detail: the Chicago mob were the customers: they paid 30 cents on the dollar for Berlin-made money, the Secret Service took the stock, and the gangsters threatened the government for their losses.
  • People: Henry Dechow, alias "Count Enrique von Buelow" — counterfeiter; Mrs. Lottie Brenner — the widow he married; Capt. Thomas J. Callaghan/Callahan — Secret Service.
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19330104.1.1 ; https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=WLKI19340201.1.7
  • Confidence: High on what the papers printed (OCR read). The trial outcome and sentence are UNVERIFIED; a Getty photo caption (search snippet only) mentions "$30,000 in spurious US currency" delivered to Montreal — see Leads.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Germany. In the setting Berlin is the capital of the Great War's victor, so dollars printed in Berlin are not a crook's workshop job but, potentially, a state's weapon against a rival currency. A fake German count also carries more social weight in a Chicago where Germany won.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Germany as the patron of other people's revolutions", in banknotes. The Secret Service's file holds that the Berlin plates were cut in a government print shop. The false count who married a Chicago widow was the courier, and the widow's house on the North Side is where the unrecovered notes went.

The northwest-side cottage plant: $500,000 in the basement — 7 December 1933

  • Where: "the basement of a cottage in a north-west side district," Chicago (address not given).
  • What happened: A United Press story datelined Chicago, 7 Dec 1933: "Captain Thomas Callaghan of the secret service" led police and agents in a raid that seized "$500,000 in spurious bills" in $5, $10, $20, $50 and $100 denominations, with "printing, engraving and marking equipment," dies, money plates, ink and specially treated paper. Arrested or named: William E. Davis, 53, described as a former Capone gang member and "passer"; Harry Davis, a "police character" and passer; an unnamed woman (Davis's wife); and Milton C. Werling, believed to head the ring. The agents had learned of the "nation-wide" ring by questioning two men arrested weeks earlier. The raid came two days after repeal (5 Dec 1933).
  • The odd detail: half a million dollars in five denominations, printed under a family cottage by a Capone alumnus: the Outfit's men moving from beer to banknotes in the week the beer became legal.
  • People: Capt. Thomas Callaghan — Secret Service; William E. Davis — ex-Capone passer; Harry Davis — passer; Milton C. Werling — alleged ring head.
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=DCDD19331207.1.1
  • Confidence: High on the report (OCR read). The address, the trial and the sentences are UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (repeal Dec 1933 holds).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): repeal week, and the rackets need a new product. A northwest-side basement full of fresh plates is the Secret Service's classic scene. The occult layer: one of the plates is not a banknote.

Gary: the men who couldn't be charged for thinking — June 1931

  • Where: the Washington hotel, Gary, Indiana.
  • What happened: Gary police called in "Captain Thomas J. Callaghan of the United States secret service" over what they took to be a "mammoth counterfeiting scheme." A raid on "Daum's room at the Washington hotel" found "masks, a pistol, blackface and fake stock certificates." Three men (Daum, Clayton and Martin) were held, and the scheme was said to include a plan to kidnap an unnamed person. Callaghan's ruling was that they could not be prosecuted for "thinking" about counterfeiting, since they had not acted on it.
  • The odd detail: masks, a pistol and blackface in a hotel room: a disguise kit for a crime that never happened, and a Secret Service captain who declined to charge a thought.
  • People: Capt. Thomas J. Callaghan — Secret Service; Daum, Clayton, Martin — suspects (first names not given).
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=DCDD19310612.1.4
  • Confidence: Medium-High (OCR read; the kidnap target and the outcome are UNVERIFIED).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "You can't prosecute a man for thinking" is the old guard's rule, and the thing that separates it from Section 13, which is quite willing to act on what people intend. A scene template: the players find the kit before the crime, and must decide which agency they answer to.

"Lustig money": the Nebraska chemists, the Times Square locker, and the golf-course cache — 1930–1939

  • Where: Nebraska (the platemakers); New York (the arrest); Chicago (the wholesale market); a golf course (location not given in the page read).
  • What happened: From 1930 "Count" Victor Lustig partnered with the Nebraska pharmacist William Watts and chemist Tom Shaw, who engraved the plates while Lustig ran a courier ring. The operation put "thousands of dollars of counterfeit money (nicknamed 'Lustig money')" into circulation "each month for the next five years." He was arrested in New York on 10 May 1935. A key on him opened a Times Square subway locker holding "$51,000 in counterfeit bills and the plates." He escaped the Federal House of Detention on 1 Sept 1935 by rope, was retaken 27 days later in Pittsburgh, and on 9 Dec 1935 got fifteen years plus five for the escape, served on Alcatraz. The money outlived him: in March 1939 Capt. Callaghan traced $17,880 in counterfeit $20s buried on a golf course to a two-million-dollar supply of Lustig notes. Indictments followed against Frankie Parker and August Englehart, and Englehart was alleged to have wholesaled the bills in Chicago at $30 per $100. (The 1939 paper says the supply was "reportedly printed in Europe", which differs from Wikipedia's Nebraska platemakers.)
  • The odd detail: four years after Lustig went to Alcatraz, his money was still coming out of the ground, and Chicago was still the market price-setter: 30 cents on the dollar, the same rate von Buelow's Berlin notes fetched in 1933.
  • People: Victor Lustig — con man, counterfeiter; William Watts, Tom Shaw — Nebraska platemakers; Frankie Parker, August Englehart — indicted 1939; Capt. Thomas J. Callaghan — Secret Service.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Lustig ; https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19390309.1.3
  • Confidence: High on the Wikipedia facts and the 1939 report as printed. Where the notes were printed (Nebraska vs. "Europe") conflicts between sources, and the golf course's location is UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): buried money on a golf course, found years later, is a GM's ready-made dig site. In the Secret Service's reading, Lustig's twenties and von Buelow's Berlin hundreds came off related plates. The going rate of 30 cents is a single Chicago buyer setting the price for everyone.

The Essig bankers who printed their losses back — 1931–1933

  • Where: Essig and Mankato, Minnesota; passed across southern Minnesota, the Dakotas, Wisconsin, Iowa, Missouri, Kansas and the West Coast; caught at Monroe, Missouri.
  • What happened: Emil Winkelmann was cashier, later manager, of the Essig Bank in Essig, Minnesota, until it closed in May 1932. With his brother William (a former bank cashier at Searles) and Raymond Marti (a Mankato commercial artist, once a reporter and handyman at the Sleepy Eye Herald-Dispatch), he printed counterfeit notes on presses, adding a second press after a 1932 move to Mankato. They passed them for about two years. In September 1933 the brothers tried to pass notes at a drug store in Monroe, Missouri. The shopkeeper matched the serial numbers to a Secret Service warning and called the St. Louis office. About $37,000 was seized at the Winkelmann home and $7,260 at Marti's. All three served their first terms at Leavenworth. A 1934 syndicated story on the counterfeiting wave told the same kind of case: two Minnesota country-bank brothers who, after the March 1933 bank holiday, "conceived a scheme for making good their losses by making spurious bills." (The dates differ; it may be the same case.)
  • The odd detail: the Depression's bank failures produced counterfeiters from inside the banks: a cashier who knew exactly what money should look like.
  • People: Emil Winkelmann — Essig Bank cashier/manager; William Winkelmann — brother, ex-cashier; Raymond Marti — artist.
  • Sources: https://www.nujournal.com/news/local-news/2025/09/26/making-money-the-illegal-way/ ; https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=WLKI19340201.1.7
  • Confidence: High on the New Ulm Journal's account. That the 1934 wire story describes the same brothers is UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — the setting's Depression runs through bank panics.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Know your money", from the other side. A country cashier who has handled every note in his county is the best forger in the state. An occult version: he isn't copying the Treasury's plates, he's copying the notes his dead depositors left behind.

C. Forged government paper

The bonus bonds come home — June 1936

  • Where: Indianapolis Post Office and Federal Building (the certification rooms), and every post office in the region.
  • What happened: The Adjusted Compensation Payment Act passed over Roosevelt's veto on 27 January 1936. Roosevelt had said the able-bodied veteran "because he wore a uniform and for no other reason should be accorded no treatment different". It paid World War veterans in Treasury bonds of $50 denominations, cashable from 15 June 1936; about $1.745 billion in bonds were issued. On 18 June 1936 the Indianapolis Times reported cheques covering $1,500,000 being prepared for Marion County veterans. 3,000 veterans had turned in bonds the day before; postal substations stayed open evenings. "Charles Mazey, head of the Secret Service here, warned veterans to keep a close watch for their checks arriving by mail." Postmaster Seidensticker asked them to report any cheque not arriving within 48 hours.
  • The odd detail: the Secret Service's warning in June 1936 was not about forgers printing bonus cheques. It was about mail theft, the cheque lifted from the box and signed by someone else, the same crime that returns in the WPA-cheque wave (next entry).
  • People: Charles Mazey — Secret Service, Indianapolis; Postmaster Seidensticker — Indianapolis.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjusted_Compensation_Payment_Act ; https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19360618.1.1
  • Confidence: High (both read). Specific Chicago bonus-forgery prosecutions were not found — UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — America never entered the Great War in the setting, so there are no 1917–19 "World War veterans" to pay. A bonus in 1936 would go to veterans of the Southern War (Mexico, 1916–17) and the occupations, a smaller and different body of men. (The 1932 Bonus Army bends the same way.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Southern War bonus is the setting's version: money paid to men who came back from Mexico and the occupations. The Secret Service handles the stolen cheques, and in the files some of those cheques were cashed by men the Army lists as dead in Veracruz.

Forged WPA cheques: $600 a week, 55 men who didn't need to go to prison — April 1939

  • Where: Indiana: Indianapolis (about 75 cheques), Evansville, Muncie, Ligonier, Richmond; the southern Indiana district.
  • What happened: The Ligonier Banner of 20 April 1939 quoted George B. Loy, "secret service head in Indianapolis": "Merchants in Indiana cities are losing approximately $600 weekly through acceptance of forged WPA checks." More than 200 stolen and forged relief cheques had been cashed in the southern Indiana district in recent months, losses over $5,500, about 75 in Indianapolis alone. In one Saturday's mail his office received seven from Evansville, five from Muncie, one from Ligonier and one from Richmond. The thieves stole the cheques from the mail and forged the endorsement. Loy wanted the WPA to hand cheques to workers on their projects instead of mailing them, and said that change eight months earlier would have prevented "at least 55" imprisonments. He told merchants to "identify definitely the bearer of a WPA check before cashing it."
  • The odd detail: the head of the Secret Service in Indianapolis counting the prison sentences his own agency could have avoided: an unusual kind of arithmetic for a cop.
  • People: George B. Loy — Secret Service, Indianapolis.
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=LPLTWO19390420.1.1
  • Confidence: High (OCR read). Chicago's equivalent figures were not found — UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the WPA holds).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): relief-cheque forgery is the Depression's small, sad federal crime, and it gives Secret Service player characters a daily beat: mailboxes, tenement hallways, grocers. The occult layer hides there. The same forged endorsement turns up on cheques from four towns in one week, in the handwriting of a man dead since 1932.

Ronald Finney's forged bonds and the Chicago brokerage — June 1933 to 1935

  • Where: Emporia, Topeka, Eureka and Neosho Falls, Kansas; a Chicago brokerage (name not given in the page read).
  • What happened: Kansas examiners began questioning bonds in June 1933. More than $1 million in forged municipal bonds had been used as collateral for deposits and loans, including $600,000 found in the state's own vaults. More were found at a Chicago brokerage. Ronald Finney of Emporia was arrested in August 1933, and three Finney-controlled banks (Fidelity State and Savings Bank of Emporia, Eureka State Bank, Farmers State Bank of Neosho Falls) failed that month ⟨fixed: V_R33⟩. Governor Alf Landon called out the National Guard. The State Treasurer, Tom Boyd, resigned and was later convicted of mail fraud; the Attorney General and the State Auditor were impeached and acquitted. In December 1933 Finney pleaded guilty to 31 counts of forgery after an insanity defence failed and was sentenced to 31 to 635 years. His father Warren Finney, who pleaded guilty to embezzlement, killed himself in June 1935 after his appeal failed.
  • The odd detail: 31 to 635 years, and a state whose own treasury vault was full of forged paper. It was government bonds, not banknotes, and the trail ran to Chicago.
  • People: Ronald Finney — forger; Warren Finney — father; Alf Landon — Kansas governor (1936 Republican nominee); Tom Boyd — state treasurer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Finney
  • Confidence: High on the Wikipedia facts. The Chicago brokerage's name and whether federal agents (Secret Service or other) worked the case are UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Landon as governor and 1936 nominee holds).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the 1936 Republican nominee's state ran on forged paper. The Secret Service's interest is the Chicago brokerage that took Finney's bonds without asking, and whose partners are still in the Board of Trade building.

D. Swindlers and mail fraud

Oscar Hartzell and the Drake estate — Sioux City, November 1933

  • Where: London (Hartzell's base); Adrian, Minnesota, and southwest Minnesota, northern Iowa, eastern South Dakota (the "hotbed"); federal court at Sioux City, Iowa.
  • What happened: Oscar M. Hartzell (born Monmouth, Illinois, 6 Jan 1876) sold Midwestern farmers shares in the "estate of Sir Francis Drake", said to be worth $22.5 billion, in return for "donations", with promises of up to $7,000 per dollar. At Adrian, Minnesota, over 1,500 locals pooled money. He ran it from London. On 4 Feb 1933 an Indiana wire story reported a British deportation order "against Oscar M. Hartzell, of Monmouth, Illinois, on charges of swindling hundreds". He was arrested by federal agents in New York on 16 Feb 1933. His investors raised $68,000 for his defence. Trial opened at Sioux City in October 1933. On 15 Nov 1933 Judge George C. Scott found him guilty "on twelve counts of mail fraud" and "immediately sentenced him to a total of ten years' imprisonment and $12,000 fine", remanding him to U.S. Marshal Don Preussner. (Wikipedia gives a $2,000 fine and a November 1934 conviction; the newspaper of the day says Nov 1933.) The postal inspector who built the case was John Sparks, with British police. Hartzell entered Leavenworth on 16 Jan 1935, was sent to the Springfield, Mo., prison hospital in May 1936, was diagnosed with paranoid schizophrenia, and died in custody on 27 Aug 1943. His line to a lieutenant, May 1929: "What does newspapers or anybody in States know about what happened 330 years ago."
  • The odd detail: the victims paid for the swindler's defence, $68,000 in the depth of the Depression, because they still believed.
  • People: Oscar M. Hartzell — swindler; Judge George C. Scott — Sioux City; John Sparks — postal inspector; Arthur Lyon Cross — Univ. of Michigan historian, expert witness; Sudie Whiteaker, Milo Lewis — associates (per Wikipedia).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Hartzell ; https://www.duluthnewstribune.com/news/the-vault/would-you-have-fallen-for-the-sir-francis-drake-pirate-treasure-hoax ; https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19331115.1.1
  • Confidence: High on the trial and sentence (newspaper of the day). The victim totals conflict wildly (10,000 to 80,000 victims; $80,000 to $2 million) — treat any single number as a claim.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — Britain. In the setting Britain is bankrupt and paying reparations to Germany, so an American farmer's claim to a $22.5-billion English fortune is a sharper joke, and Britain deporting its claimant is a Britain in no position to argue with anyone. The Iowa trial holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "What happened 330 years ago" is the question a cult asks too. The Secret Service's hook: Hartzell's London "research" went into real Elizabethan records, and one of the documents he paid a clerk to copy was genuine and should not have left the archive.

The Chicago Drake trial: 43 indicted, 8 convicted — May 1935 to February 1936

  • Where: federal court, Chicago; the money had been mailed "to offices in Chicago and Sioux City, Iowa."
  • What happened: In May 1935 a Chicago federal grand jury indicted "thirty-nine men and four women on charges of using the mails to defraud 270,000 persons" through "the threadbare Sir Francis Drake estate swindle." Hartzell was brought from Leavenworth. His brother Canfield Hartzell, Otto Yant ("formerly an Iowa banker"), Dellmar Short, Lester Ohmart, James H. Hauber and others were named. On 1 Feb 1936 a federal jury in Chicago convicted eight: Oscar Hartzell ("the baron", "master-mind"); Canfield Hartzell of New York; A. R. Gregory of Wenatchee, Wash.; Joseph H. Hauber, Lester Ohmart, D. C. Short and Otto Yant, all of Chicago; and Emil R. Rochell of Madison, Wis. The rest of the 41 on trial were dismissed. The myth "had taken more than $1,200,000 from farmers," and some were promised "as high as $1800 on a $1 'donation'", from "70,000 credulous midwesterners."
  • The odd detail: the Drake estate had an office in Chicago staffed by Chicagoans, a whole collection bureau for a dead pirate's money, still running after its "baron" was in Leavenworth.
  • People: as above; Otto Yant — ex-Iowa banker.
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=WLKI19350516.1.2 ; https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19360201.1.1
  • Confidence: High on the reports (OCR read). The OCR reads the take as "81,000,000" in the 1935 story, probably "$1,000,000"; the sentences imposed after 1 Feb 1936 are UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Chicago office that takes money from 70,000 believers by mail is an organisation that could run anything: a cult's tithe, a Golden Circle war chest. The Secret Service would want the mailing list.

"Yellow Kid" Weil in the 1930s — Kalamazoo, Toledo, Gary, and the postal inspectors — 1930–1941 (see R08)

  • Where: Chicago; Kalamazoo, Michigan; Martinsville and Indianapolis, Indiana; Toledo, Ohio; Gary; New York.
  • What happened: R08 has Weil's life. This entry is his 1930s record from the papers. Aug 1930: five months out of Leavenworth and promising "legitimate business", Weil was sought for selling Joseph Kindelberger of Kalamazoo $15,000 of stock "in a Chinese oil well." Jan 1931: arrested, paraded through a Chicago lineup and taken to Rockford; he told the lineup, "Sure, I am a con man–the best. But I've only taken from those who can afford the education." He beat that charge. Feb 1931: in jail at Gary. Apr 1931: held for taking $37,000 from Matthews Reinart, a retired Indianapolis grocer. Reinart was met at Martinsville by a "J. A. Kirk" and introduced to a "G. A. Mayo" who had made millions "in Mexican oil"; his stake grew to $300,000 on paper, and he handed over $37,000 "collateral" in Toledo. Dec 1939: "Postal inspectors seized" Weil in Chicago on mail-fraud charges for a $4,000 "Mexican oil deal" against a New York advertising man, Warren Edmonds. He faced a removal hearing before U.S. Commissioner Edwin K. Walker. He "retired" in 1941 after 27 months in the Atlanta federal prison for a phony oil-lease scheme.
  • The odd detail: every one of his 1930s stings is oil in a far country: Chinese wells, Mexican oil, paper fortunes grown in a week.
  • People: Joseph R. "Yellow Kid" Weil — "the midwest's most notorious confidence man"; Joseph Kindelberger — Kalamazoo victim; Matthews Reinart — Indianapolis victim; Warren Edmonds — New York victim; Edwin K. Walker — U.S. Commissioner, Chicago.
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19300804.1.1 ; https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19310429.1.1 ; https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=IPT19391215.1.27 ; https://www.wbez.org/culture/2013/01/25/the-yellow-kid-rides-again ; https://jot101.com/2013/11/100-year-old-con-man-yellow-kid/
  • Confidence: High on the dated newspaper items. A February 1934 Peoria arrest with suitcases of cut newspaper marked "$200,000" appeared only in a search snippet — UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the empire. In the setting the Mexican north is American-ceded and Tampico is held, so "Mexican oil" is colonial speculation, the very boom the setting blames for the Crash. Weil's stings become a small copy of the thing that broke the country.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Weil sells shares in Tampico wells to grocers. The Secret Service's file notes that one of his "worthless" leases was real, and something in the occupation zone paid out.

The National Aid Society — policies for the old that paid nothing — Springfield, Illinois, June 1935

  • Where: Springfield, Illinois.
  • What happened: On 20 June 1935 four former officers of the National Aid Society were indicted for "using the mails to defraud thousands of aged poor": Frank A. Auten (president), Henson C. Robinson (vice-president), A. C. Littlejohn (secretary) and Hugo W. Greb (treasurer). They had sold "policies" of up to $3,000, with annual assessments as high as $16, that "carried no benefits whatever." Members were nearly all between 60 and 70, and the society had reached 130,000 members. There were twelve counts, each carrying five years, and bond was $5,000 apiece.
  • The odd detail: 130,000 old people paying yearly dues on a promise, in the same months the country was arguing over the Townsend old-age pension (UNVERIFIED as linked).
  • People: Frank A. Auten, Henson C. Robinson, A. C. Littlejohn, Hugo W. Greb — officers.
  • Sources: https://newspapers.library.in.gov/?a=d&d=DCDD19350620.1.2
  • Confidence: High on the indictment (OCR read); the verdict is UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a mail-order "aid society" with a membership list of 130,000 aged, lonely Midwesterners is a vampire court's dream roster, and a medium's. Postal inspectors have the list, and so does the Secret Service.
