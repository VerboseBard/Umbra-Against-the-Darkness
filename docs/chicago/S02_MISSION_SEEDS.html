S02 — Thirty mission seeds from the Chicago & Great Lakes library

  • Lane: S02 — mission seeds built from the 2026-09-26 research sweep (R01–R23) and its fact-checks (V_R*)
  • Date: 2026-09-26
  • Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (mission-seed writer)
  • Standing: PROPOSAL, NOT CANON — every seed in this file. Nothing here has been ruled by the owner. The real anchors are taken from the chapters as fact-checked; where a checker corrected a fact (⟨fixed …⟩ or a V_R* verdict) the corrected fact is used, and nothing marked [UNVERIFIED …] is used as an anchor.
  • Status: COMPLETE — 30 seeds and a summary table.
  • Audited: V_S02.md (2026-09-26) re-checked every real anchor against the chapters' fact-checks (V_R*, V2_R*); each change is marked ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩. No seed fell.

Overview. Thirty mission seeds, each built the owner's way — the real record kept exactly, the occult layer put underneath. Seeds 1–7 develop the seven seeds in the owner's own design doc (his words at the top of each; the facts from R17, which checked and corrected his numbers). Seeds 8–30 are new, drawn from R01–R22 and the fact-checks, and spread across Chicago's neighbourhoods and the Great Lakes ring (Cleveland, Detroit, Akron, St. Paul, Milwaukee and the lake, the Wisconsin northwoods, Ottawa), across 1929–1939, across all five factions (always two at odds), and across all six threats. Most clocks end on a real date the record supplies — a witness's death, a broadcast, a contract, a ship going down — so the team races the calendar and the calendar never moves (§31).

How to read a seed

Every seed has the same parts, in the owner's method: real anchors first (file + entry, dates, names and addresses kept exactly as the fact-checked chapter gives them), then three layers of truth (what the period papers said · what the folklore says · what is actually there), who sends the team (and what each faction wants — they rarely want the same thing), three clue sites (one clue carries a cost), the clock (tied to a real date wherever the record offers one — the scenario owns its clock, §29), the divergence underneath, another road (no seed requires one Specialty; the §29 "three roads back" — the guy, the air, the ground — are named where they fit), and one twist.

What is record and what is invention: the Real anchors are the record, as the chapters and their fact-checks give it. Everything under what is actually there, the contents found at each clue site, the twists, and anything marked proposal is invention for play — the owner's layer underneath. Where a seed attributes a secret to a real person, that is the method the owner uses (Booth as a half-vampire in his own Senate transcript), not a claim about the person.

Rules this file keeps: the rulebook's §29 (clues are given freely; rolls price clarity and time; nothing on the critical path behind one roll), §32 (five factions; every badge is a leash; IHS stands aside from a door to the dead), §33 (vampires in society, werewolves, demon summoners and their cults, the fae, ghosts, the Golden Circle — and the Circle is unease, never revelation). Where no real folklore exists for an event, the folklore layer says so and offers a proposal instead of inventing a legend.

Seeds 1–7 are the owner's own (from the MISSION STUFF section of his design doc, dossiers in R17_owner_seed_dossiers.md); his words stand at the top of each. Seeds 8–30 are new.

Seed 1 — Thirteen Chimes (owner's seed: the Merchandise Mart) — proposal, not canon

Owner's words: "MERCHANDISE MART / 1930-Merchandise Mart was completed and was the world's largest commercial building at the time.(4 million sq ft)"

Pitch: Every Wednesday at midnight the city switches off its lamps to listen to a horror show — and under the thirteenth chime, something is being said to every dark room in Chicago at once.

Real anchors

  • Merchandise Mart — R17 §6 and R09 (Loop). Opened 5 May 1930, ~4,000,000 sq ft, eighteen floors plus a 25-storey tower, 650-foot corridors of showrooms, a private freight station on the river; Field's wholesale lost $5M in 1931 and $8M in 1932 and shrank from four floors to a floor and a half. NBC's Central Division on floors 19–20 (studios opened October 1930; up to 1,700 programmes a month): six studios, two hundred miles of wire, and a battery room to keep the network on the air if the city's power failed; WMAQ and WENR broadcast from here; enlarged by 11,500 sq ft in 1935 [UNVERIFIED — V_R17: in no page read] ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩. NBC chief engineer O. B. Hanson laid it out.
  • "Lights Out" — R13 §5. Wyllis Cooper's midnight serial, on WENR from 3 January 1934, fifteen minutes, thirty by April 1934; coast-to-coast on NBC from 17 April 1935. The opening: a deep voice, "Lights out, everybody!", then thirteen chimes. About 600 fan clubs by mid-1936. (Kept honest: R13 marks UNVERIFIED that the earliest episodes originated in the Mart's studios — the seed only needs WENR, which broadcast from there.)

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a hit show that frightens housewives; complaint letters; a white-elephant building a third empty in the Depression.
  • What the folklore says: none recorded. Proposal: listeners' letters (the kind Section 13 already reads) say there is a voice under the last chime, and that people who sleep with the set on wake up standing at their front doors.
  • What is actually there: a small demon-summoning cell — a dozen people, two of them NBC night staff — renting a dark showroom "to the trade only" on an empty floor. They have learned that a working spoken into the carrier at the thirteenth chime is heard in every listening room as if each were a circle. A room is a small thing. A network is not.

Who sends the team

  • Section 13 — more than half its money goes to keeping outbreaks out of the papers, and radio is the paper that talks. It wants the working stopped and the show kept on the air: pulling a hit programme is itself a headline.
  • Kabbalah of David (on contract) — wants the text of the invocation, whole, before anyone burns it: the tongue it is in says who taught it. The two agencies want the same disc for opposite reasons.

Three clue sites

  1. NBC studios, floors 19–20 (R09 Merchandise Mart; R17 §6) — the transcription discs of each midnight show; the engineer's log; a sound man who noticed the level meter moving on the thirteenth chime when no one was speaking.
  2. The dark showroom floors and the river freight station — a buyer's badge opens the corridors; a lease in a dead firm's name; chalk under the carpet; the freight doors the cell uses at 1 a.m.
  3. The battery room — costed clue: played at half speed, the disc gives up the words — 1 Sanity per full listening, and it takes three to transcribe it. One listening is free and tells you there is a voice; clarity costs (§29 rule 1).

The clock — Start: Wednesday 27 March 1935, the morning after a listener's letter. Apparent deadline: none. Real deadline: Wednesday 17 April 1935, midnight, the first coast-to-coast broadcast. Escalation: each Wednesday before (3 and 10 April) more sleepers at more doors, and one who walks into the river. After the 17th, still worth saving: the fan clubs — by mid-1936 there are about 600, and every one is a room the working has already visited.

The divergence underneath — Holds (domestic calendar). The break shows only in the budget: Section 13 can pay to keep a story out of the Tribune; it cannot pay NBC to drop its best new show.

Another road — The Circuit Man is the obvious key (Dead Frequencies hears the carrier; Kill the Lights is useless — that is what the battery room is for). Without one: the guy is a sound-effects man (the show's sound men really built props as elaborate as a gallows, R13), reached through a wheelman; an Arcanist can Unpick the working live at the thirteenth chime; a Holy Man in the studio can stand between the microphone and what answers.

Twist — The voice under the chime was not the cell's first. Section 13's own Chicago office sits on a dark floor of the Mart and has been passing coded traffic to field units under the midnight carrier (proposal); the cell found the channel by answering it. Stopping the working means shutting down the team's own faction's radio.

Seed 2 — The Gate Stands (owner's seed: the Union Stock Yards fire) — proposal, not canon

Owner's words: "UNION STOCKYARDS FIRE / 1934 there was a major fire at the Union Stockyards, Chicago's second major fire. / Link it to the first fire?"

Pitch: The yards burned in an afternoon and one man died under a wall — and the Hunter Guild wants his body before the coroner sees what he was turning into.

Real anchors

  • The fire, Saturday 19 May 1934 — R17 §4. Began 4:14 p.m. in straw-bedded cattle pens under the 43rd Street viaduct; cause never settled (a cigarette from the viaduct, a match, or spontaneous combustion); under four inches of rain since 1 January; 92°F; gusts to 60 mph. About 2,200 firemen; at least 31 suburban departments. Destroyed: the International Amphitheatre, the Stock Yard Inn (42nd & Halsted), the Saddle and Sirloin Club (Dexter Park Ave), the Drovers National Bank (4201 S. Halsted), the Live Stock National Bank (Exchange & Halsted), the Exchange Building, WAAF, the Daily Drovers' Journal. One yards employee, about 33, found under a fallen wall; about 150 families burned out; $8M. Mayor Kelly and Fire Marshal Michael J. Corrigan: worst since 1871. The Stone Gate (bull "Sherman" over the arch) and its gatehouse survived. The new Amphitheatre opened 1 December 1934. (Figures vary by source: R09 gives 90% of the yards and $6M — use R17's.)
  • The Saddle and Sirloin gallery — R17 §4. A gallery of the trade's great breeders and packers, each elected by his peers (the "nearly 350" is the club's 2003 centenary roll, not the 1934 count; how many burned is not given in any page read); club painter Robert Grafton replaced 104 in eighteen months — "from photographs and memory" is unsupported (V_R17) ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩.
  • The yards' earlier fire, 22 December 1910 — R17 §4: the Nelson Morris warehouse, 4300 S. Loomis, Chief James J. Horan and twenty firemen killed (21 firefighters, plus three civilians — V_R17); memorialised on the same gate. This is the "first fire" of the owner's own source sentence: Chicago in the 1930s calls 1934 "the second major fire in its history after the 1910 fire" — the 1871 links below are a second reading, not the owner's (V_R17) ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩. Union Stock Yards and Packingtown, Back of the Yards, Canaryville — R09.

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a careless cigarette off a viaduct in a drought; the worst since 1871.
  • What the folklore says: the city's own habit — legend says a comet fell on the night of 1871 (Biela's comet; no scientific support, R17 §4), and a cow kicked a lantern (Mrs O'Leary, exonerated 1997). Chicago blames its great fires on small careless things.
  • What is actually there: a werewolf lineage that has lived inside the livestock aristocracy since the yards were young — the portraits were its family tree, each "elected by his peers." A Hunter Guild cell set the pens alight to burn the pack out on a Saturday afternoon when half of it would be at the club. It killed one workman and burned out 150 families, and most of the pack walked away.

Who sends the team

  • IHS — the Back of the Yards parishes lost homes and a man. The order wants to know who set a fire that killed a Catholic workman, and it will not like the answer.
  • The Hunter Guild — wants the survivors finished before they regroup, and wants its own man's body back before the inquest. It will offer to work with the team. It is the least safe to tell (§32).
  • The Secret Service — two banks' vaults burned. The cover story is counterfeiting and tax evasion, and bank ledgers are exactly that; it pulls rank on the vaults.

Three clue sites

  1. The Stone Gate, Exchange Ave & Peoria St (R09 Union Stock Yards) — the only building left standing, with the 1910 dead on its memorial. Costed clue: Psychometry, The Long Touch on the bull's head (1 Mana + 1 Sanity) goes back through 1934 to 1910 — and shows something on all fours in both fires.
  2. Robert Grafton's studio / the rebuilt Saddle and Sirloin rooms — which 104 faces did he choose to repaint, and who told him which? The list of the repainted is the list of the living pack.
  3. The 43rd Street viaduct and the burned Engine Company 59 house — where it started; a Guild man's matchbook in the ash; and the coroner's inquest on the unnamed dead man (coroner Frank J. Walsh, R09 Cook County Morgue).

The clock — Start: Sunday 20 May 1934, the ash still warm. Escalation: the Guild is hunting now, in a burned-out neighbourhood full of homeless families. Real deadline: 1 December 1934, when the new International Amphitheatre opens for the stock show and the pack sits down to dinner under Grafton's new faces. After that: what is still worth saving is the 150 families, who are mostly ordinary (cult versus population).

The divergence underneath — Holds (domestic calendar). Proposal — historical sense: in this America the packers' largest buyer is the Army of occupation in Mexico, so the yards' old families are federal contractors — which is why the Secret Service is so quick to the vaults.

Another road — A woodsman keeps the trail through the rail sidings (You don't lose them); without one, the guy is a Canaryville bartender who serves the night shift, and the air is the police and fire band from the afternoon of the 19th. No Doctor for the dead man? The coroner's physician will talk for a price, and Grafton will talk for nothing.

Twist — The one man found under the wall, about 33, unnamed in every page the research read, was the Guild's torch — and he had been bitten. The Guild wants him before the inquest because the autopsy will show he was already turning. (His real name is a Lead in R17: the Tribune of 20–21 May 1934.)

Seed 3 — Frame by Frame (owner's seed: the Republic Steel clash) — proposal, not canon

Owner's words: "REPUBLIC STEEL CRASH / May 26 1937 there was a massive strike that had 85000 steel workers walking out. With five mills and 22000 of them being from Chicago. This led to the Republic Steel Clash four days later. / Ten demonstrators were killed by police bullets during the "Little Steel Strike" of 1937. … 10 demonstrators were killed and 60 injured; 40 police officers were hurt." (R17 §5 checked the numbers: 10 dead holds; ~30 shot, ~90 hurt; "five mills" holds — the owner's source reads "Five Chicago-area mills were shut with 22,000 workers going on strike"; the Tribune's "70,000 in five states" is a different count; 70–80,000 strikers; "40 police hurt" is the police's own claim — the Tribune counted 26. V_R17.) ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩

Pitch: Ten marchers died on the prairie, and the Paramount film shows who fired first — a man in the police line who is not police, carrying a bullet he was given when he swore an oath to God and the Devil.

Real anchors

  • The field, Sunday 30 May 1937 — R17 §5 and R09. From Sam's Place (113th St & Green Bay Ave) toward the Republic gate at 116th St & Burley Ave (no page read gives 117th — V_R17) ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩; 1,500–2,500 marchers; the police line under Capt. James Mooney and Capt. Thomas Kilroy. The ten dead, with the dates the last six died: Hilding Anderson (d. 3 June), Alfred Causey, Leo Francisco, 17 (d. 15 June), Earl Handley, Otis Jones (d. 8 June), Sam Popovich, Kenneth Reed, Joseph Rothmund (d. 31 May), Anthony Tagliori (d. 1 June), Lee Tisdale, 50 (d. 19 June). Republic Steel — not the police — had acquired over $50,000 of tear gas ten days before and procured for the police the white hatchet-handle clubs that were not standard issue; the Senate found the gas and clubs came through Republic Steel (V_R17) ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩.
  • The film and the verdicts — R17 §5, R03. Paramount cameraman Orlando Lippert; the reel banned in Chicago, withheld nationally; screened in full and in slow motion to the La Follette committee on 2 July 1937. Commissioner James P. Allman testified 30 June. Coroner Frank J. Walsh's inquest opened 14 July 1937 — justifiable homicide; the Senate: clearly avoidable.
  • The Black Oath — R10 (Black Legion): sworn kneeling at gunpoint, "In the name of God and the Devil, one to reward and the other to punish"; each initiate given a bullet. The rulebook already says the Legion's real enemy was labour.

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a Red mob attacked police; the police defended themselves (in this setting, with no Red Scare, the slur is "German-paid agitators", R09).
  • What the folklore says: none recorded beyond the film itself — the reel "not fit to be seen." Proposal: the mill hands say the men who fired first "didn't blink in the gas."
  • What is actually there: half a dozen Legion men — the Golden Circle's northern arm — wore borrowed police coats in the line. Each fired the bullet he was given at his oath. Oath-bullets do not miss; that is what the Devil half of the oath buys. The Circle wanted a massacre to break the strike, and got one.

Who sends the team

  • Section 13 — its liaison sits behind Allman at the witness table (a proposal from R17). It wants the frame that proves the Legion was in the line shown in closed session only, and the Legion's Chicago names.
  • The Secret Service — its chief since 1937 is Frank J. Wilson (R17 §3). It wants the reel's frame to go no further than it already has. Whatever you saw, the report says counterfeiting (§32). Neither agency wants the Senate to see it in public — for different reasons.

Three clue sites

  1. Sam's Place, 113th & Green Bay (R09 Republic Steel) — the union's headquarters; the wounded; Lupe Marshall, the Hull-House social worker who testified in her bloodied suit.
  2. The Cook County morgue and the Scientific Crime Detection Laboratory (R09, R13) — the slugs from the ten; the private crime lab on the Near North Side will match any bullet for any sheriff. Two of the slugs are hand-cast, with a mark on the base.
  3. Paramount News / the Senate hearing room — costed clue: the reel, frame by frame. 1 Sanity to watch it through at slow speed — it is men being shot in the back — and the one frame with the plainclothes man firing first is near the end.

The clock — Start: Monday 31 May 1937. The witnesses die on real dates: Rothmund 31 May, Tagliori 1 June, Anderson 3 June, Jones 8 June, Francisco 15 June, Tisdale 19 June — each one who dies before the team reaches him is a witness lost. Then 30 June (Allman testifies), 2 July (the reel is screened — whatever is in it by then, the Senate sees), 14 July (the inquest opens). After the inquest the verdict is fixed; the Legion's names are still worth having.

The divergence underneath — Holds for the strike (the CIO, SWOC and the Wagner Act are domestic). Bends in the slur: no Soviet Union, so the marchers are called German-paid, and the Circle's men get to play patriots against "the Kaiser's agitators."

Another road — No Doctor to read the wounds? The coroner's physician and the crime lab both will. No talker for Lupe Marshall? Hull-House sends her to you — she wants the truth told. The ground: a woodsman on the prairie can read where the line stood and where the first man knelt.

Twist — The man in the frame who fired first is the Secret Service's own informant inside the Legion. That is why Wilson wants the frame cut — and why the informant fired: to keep his cover.

Seed 4 — A Quarter a Scrap (owner's seed: Dillinger) — proposal, not canon

Owner's words: "JOHN DILLINGER / July 22nd 1934 John Dillinger killed by FBI. WE CAN MAKE SOMETHING FROM THAT." (R17 §7: in July 1934 the Bureau was the Justice Department's Division of Investigation; "FBI" dates from July 1935.)

Pitch: The crowd at the Biograph mopped up Public Enemy No. 1's blood with handkerchiefs — and, the retellings say, sold it at a quarter a scrap (the price is unverified; the handkerchiefs are not) ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩ — and somebody in the Gold Coast is buying every scrap back.

Real anchors

  • The Biograph and the morgue — R17 §7, R02, R09. 2433 N. Lincoln Ave; about 10:40 p.m., 22 July 1934; one round through the back of the neck, out under the right eye; bystanders Theresa Paulas and Etta Natalsky hit. "Handkerchiefs were whipped out and used to mop up the blood"; scraps sold for a quarter; $100 offered for a stained brick, $1,000 for the shirt [UNVERIFIED — V_R17: the prices are in none of the pages cited; the handkerchiefs and dipped newspapers are] ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩. The Cook County morgue (Polk & Wood): ~15,000 viewers in a day and a half; the FBI's file is said to record that the deputy coroner took bribes for viewings and mask-making [UNVERIFIED — V_R17: not on the page cited] ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩. McCready's, 4506 Sheridan Road. Four death masks; Harold N. May of Reliance Dental made two with "Reprolastic" — the undertaker complained the moulds stripped hair and skin. The autopsy (lost ~50 years in a shopping bag) records brown eyes; the brain was removed; 1 August 1934 the father took out an exhumation permit; 2½ tons of concrete over the coffin at Crown Hill.
  • The surgery at Probasco's, 28 May 1934 — R02. 2509 N. Crawford Ave; Dr. Wilhelm Loeser (German-born, on parole for narcotics) and Dr. Harold Cassidy (anaesthetic); fingertips treated with acid; Dillinger swallowed his tongue under the ether and nearly died; Van Meter had the same work. James Probasco went out of a 19th-floor window of the federal office on 26 July 1934. (Cassidy later rented upstairs at the Green Mill, 1937 — R16, corrected by V_R16.)

The three layers

  • What the papers said: the G-men got their man; the Lady in Red; the crowds at the morgue.
  • What the folklore says: legend says it was not Dillinger at all but a look-alike, "Jimmy Lawrence" (Jay Robert Nash's theory, rejected by historians — R17 §7); the sheet photograph; the Smithsonian tale.
  • What is actually there: on 28 May at Probasco's, the patient who stopped breathing under the ether came back up turned — a vampire in North Side society had paid for the surgery and sent the "surgeon's assistant" who revived him. Lead through the neck does not kill a vampire; the Division shot a new-made one in the alley, and what finished him was the morgue taking his brain. His blood carries the turning. Every handkerchief in the crowd is a door.

Who sends the team

  • Section 13 — its parent Division shot him and its evidence room holds the brain. It wants the handkerchiefs back quietly and the maker found, without the Division's story changing by a word.
  • The Hunter Guild — hired by nobody it will name, it is buying the same handkerchiefs to find the maker — and would happily see the Division's version fall if that is the price.

Three clue sites

  1. The Biograph alley (R09 The Biograph Theater) — the souvenir sellers; who bought the brick; a boy who sold three scraps to "a lady in a car with the blinds down."
  2. The Cook County morgue (R09) — the deputy coroner's bribe book (proposal — the bribes themselves are unverified, above) ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩; May's Reprolastic moulds, with hair and skin in them — a sample of what the body was.
  3. 2509 N. Crawford Ave — the ether room. Costed clue: Psychometry, Deep Read on the room (1 Mana + 1 Sanity + 1 Focus) shows the patient stop breathing, and who leaned over him.

The clock — Start: 11 p.m., 22 July 1934. 24 July: McCready's, 5,000 in the street. 25 July: burial under concrete — after that the body can only be reached with a permit and a crew. 26 July: Probasco goes out of the 19th-floor window — the team's one living witness to the surgery, unless they reach him first. 1 August: the father's exhumation permit. Escalation: each night, somebody who kept a handkerchief does not come home.

The divergence underneath — Holds (domestic calendar), bending lightly: the Division is K. Edgar Hoover's, and Section 13 sits inside it (§32). The Bureau's legends of 1934 (R02 "The Bureau's legends, catalogued") are in this world partly cover stories.

Another road — No Doctor for the autopsy? The Indianapolis embalmer L. W. Howe handled the body and will say what he saw; Dr. Patrick Weeks's prison record of the heart settles the identity. No Chemist to load wood? Dead man's blood (§28) weakens without killing — which is how you take the maker alive. The guy: Louis Piquett's investigator Arthur O'Leary, who collected Loeser at 7:30 p.m. on 28 May.

Twist — Two faces were done that night. Van Meter "had the same work done" (R02) — and he is the one who came back from the ether. Dillinger was just a man. The handkerchiefs are ordinary blood, bought up by a collector of famous relics; the real trail runs to a St. Paul alley on 23 August 1934, where Van Meter was shot to pieces (R02).

Seed 5 — Something That Wears Men (owner's seed: the College Kidnappers) — proposal, not canon

Owner's words: "College kidnappers / Chicago College Kidnappers(2 years of kidnappings)1931-1933 / Skinwalkers? Shapeshifters? Doppelgangers? Implanting themself into society for some reason."

Pitch: A gang that only kidnaps men the police will never look for, led by a man rumoured to have changed his face and his fingerprints — his own prints identified him when he died — who went about as a Secret Service agent ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩ — and the list of who goes into the Bellwood bungalow is never the same as the list of who comes out.

Real anchors

  • The College Kidnappers — R17 §1. Theodore "Handsome Jack" Klutas (U. of Illinois 1923, no degree); members incl. Edward Doll ("Eddie LaRue"), Russell Hughes, Frank Souder, Gale Swolley, Ernest Rossi, Eddie Wagner, Earl McMahon, Julius "Babe" Jones, Walter Dietrich. Victims chosen from the underworld because they "were less likely to approach the police"; James Hackett, Blue Island gambler, kidnapped twice ($75,000 paid by his wife, May 1931; the second demand was double — and the period press says the gang "was paid $1500" for it, V_R17) ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩. Take: nearly $500,000. November 1933: ASA Mal Coghlan said "stories had come to him" that Klutas had had his fingerprints changed and his visage marred — a rumour he relayed, not a finding; at Bellwood "fingerprints established the identities of Klutas and Detrick, officers said" (V_R17) ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩. What holds is the disguise: he had "posed to the owner as a Government Secret Service agent" (Evening Star, 7 Jan 1934, re-read by V_R17; the Helena Independent's separate wire: "living under the guise of a secret service operative"). 24 Nov 1933: indicted for counterfeiting $5 bills. 13 Nov 1933: Hughes shot dead in a Peoria barber shop.
  • The end at Bellwood — R17 §1, R01 (Gilbert). 619 South 24th Avenue, Bellwood; watched ten days on Babe Jones's information; Saturday 6 January 1934 — Dietrich taken shaving; Klutas "mowed down as he came out of the bungalow and reached for a pistol" (both wire stories — the drove-up-and-rang-the-bell version is a hobby blog's; the Thompson is Sgt. Joe Healy's in the blog account) ⟨fixed: V_S02⟩; operation under Capt. Daniel "Tubbo" Gilbert. Klutas buried in Lyndon Township Cemetery beside his mother.
  • Dietrich and the Michigan City break, 26 September 1933 — R02: the ten who walked out with the pistols Dillinger had smuggled in. The Lindbergh Law, 22 June 1932 — R02: kidnapping across state lines made federal.

  • The Ford Hunger March, 7 March 1932 — R07, R03. 3,000–5,000 unemployed marchers down Miller Road to Gate 3 of the River Rouge plant; Harry Bennett, hit by rocks, fired into the crowd; four killed — Joseph York (20), Kalman Leny (26), Joseph DeBlasio (31), Joseph Bussell (16) — and Curtis Williams (36) died months later; Woodmere Cemetery refused to bury Williams because he was Black, so his ashes were scattered from an airplane over the Rouge.
  • Harry Bennett and the Ford Service Department — R07. Football players, boxers, wrestlers and Detroit River gang members, spying on and beating union men; his Huron River "castle" near Ypsilanti with pet lions and tigers and hidden passageways; his Farwell lodge with a secret passage behind a hinged bookcase and a moat of pointed posts crossed by a bridge wired with dynamite.
  • The Battle of the Overpass, 26 May 1937 — R07, R03. At the Miller Road overpass, Gate 4; about 40 Service men beat Walter Reuther and Richard Frankensteen; Detroit News photographer James "Scotty" Kilpatrick hid his exposed plates under the back seat and handed Ford's men blanks.
  • Detroit's werewolves in print — R22: Marie Hamlin's Legends of Le Détroit (1883/84) — Jacques Morand turned to stone at Grosse Pointe ("Voilà le Garou!"), and the wolf that stole Archange Simonet, shot with a silver bullet, which fled "down the throat of a catfish."

The three layers

  • What the papers said: Ford's men beat union organisers; Bennett denied everything.
  • What the folklore says: legend says — Detroit's own French werewolves, in period print, silver bullet and all; and the old women on the river who still won't cook catfish (R22).
  • What is actually there: werewolves — the inner core of the Service Department, a dozen men out of the Detroit River gangs. Bennett keeps big cats at the castle because big cats explain bones. At the Overpass the pack lost its temper in daylight, and Kilpatrick's hidden plates caught one face mid-turn.

Who sends the team

  • The Hunter Guild — hired by a UAW man who saw the plates and asked no agency, because every agency in Detroit has a friend at Ford.
  • Section 13 — has a working arrangement with Bennett's river men, as it has with the Outfit (§32). It wants the plates too — to keep them out of the papers and keep the arrangement.

Three clue sites

  1. The Miller Road overpass, Gate 4 — the stairs Reuther was kicked down; scratches on the concrete that no boot makes.
  2. Kilpatrick's car / the Detroit News darkroom — the real plates. Costed clue: printing the one plate full-size and looking at it for as long as it takes to be sure costs 1 Sanity.
  3. Bennett's castle on the Huron River, near Ypsilanti (R07) — the big-cat house, the hidden passages; the keeper who feeds the lions on the night after a full moon.

The clock — Start: 26 May 1937, the afternoon of the beating. Escalation: the pack wants the real plates and knows a photographer lied. Real deadline (proposal): the next full moon — the GM checks the almanac. And 30 May 1937, four days later, Chicago police fire on the Memorial Day march (Seed 3): the same week, two cities, two private armies.

The divergence underneath — BENDS for the 1932 march (R07): its real organisers were Communist-led Unemployed Councils; with no Soviet Union, the setting reassigns them, and the press slur becomes "German-paid." And Ford's own Dearborn Independent and the Protocols (R05, living memory) read very differently in a world where a Jewish-integrated Germany won — the Kabbalah keeps a file on Dearborn.

Another road — Silver suppresses regeneration, wolfsbane harms (§28). No Chemist? A Detroit silversmith; a woodsman's Gather it here along the Huron. No Circuit Man for the castle's alarms? The dynamite bridge at Farwell is Bennett's — the castle has only cats and a keeper who drinks. The guy: an old French-Detroit woman on the river who knows the Simonet story and knows which boathouse.

Twist — Bennett is the only human in his own inner circle — and he is afraid of his men. The lions are not there to explain the bones. They are there to keep the wolves in line, and he is running out of lions.

Seed 28 — Things Look Bad — proposal, not canon

Pitch: Three ships and some ninety souls lost on Lake Michigan in seven weeks, the last of them on the night the market broke — and the drowned captain of the first one is still writing notes.

Real anchors

  • The SS Andaste, 9–10 September 1929 — R07. Grand Haven for Chicago with about 2,000 tons of sand and gravel for Chicago's construction, Lake Shore Drive among it; no radio, missed the gale warnings; all 25 died; 16 bodies, eleven in life jackets; never found. A pencilled driftwood note, "Worst storm I have ever been in. Can't stay up much longer. Hope to God we are saved. A.L.A.", attributed to Capt. Albert L. Anderson — its authenticity disputed. Earl Zietlow, 14, on his first voyage.
  • The car ferry SS Milwaukee, 22 October 1929 — R07. Capt. Robert H. McKay, into a 37-mph gale with 27 railcars (lumber, food, bathtubs, Nash automobiles); all 52 died; one man's watch stopped at 9:35; a message case found 27 October: "… sea gate is bent in and can't keep the water out … Seas are tremendous. Things look bad." Wreck seven miles north-east of Milwaukee.
  • The SS Wisconsin, 29 October 1929 (Black Tuesday) — R07. Chicago for Milwaukee; sank about 7:10 p.m., 6.5 miles south-south-east of Kenosha; Capt. Dougal Morrison went down with her; the dead are counted anywhere from 8 to 18.
  • The lakes' vanishings — R22: the Rosabelle (1921) and the Thomas Hume (1891), crews gone off the Michigan shore; the Christmas Tree Ship (1912).

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a terrible autumn on the lake; old ships, no radio, captains who sailed in any weather.
  • What the folklore says: the lakes' own — ships and crews that vanish, a Flying Dutchman (R22), and the Andaste note that nobody can prove was his.
  • What is actually there: ghosts. The lake's drowned do not rest in autumn; they sail. The Andaste's crew, never found, have been calling other captains out into the weather, and each ship lost gives them more hands. The note was real — written after she went down.

Who sends the team

  • The Hunter Guild — hired by the car-ferry men of Milwaukee, whose crews refuse to sail after the Milwaukee's message case washes up.
  • The Secret Service — proposal: a Chicago broker aboard the Wisconsin on Black Tuesday carries a case of colonial bonds that are the paper behind the crash (§31's cause). The Service wants the case, not the ghosts.

Three clue sites

  1. The beach where the message case came ashore, 27 October — the case, the handwriting; a second slip folded inside the first that the papers did not print.
  2. Grand Haven — the Andaste's home port; the owner's copy of Anderson's handwriting. Costed clue: a Spirit Medium's Blood and Bone on the driftwood note (1 Mana + 1 Sanity) holds one question — and Anderson answers in the present tense.
  3. The Chicago dock of the Wisconsin — the passenger list for the night of 29 October; a broker's clerk who saw him board with the case.

The clock — Start: 27 October 1929, the message case ashore. Real deadline: 7:10 p.m., 29 October 1929 — the Wisconsin sinks in the record, and the team cannot stop the calendar (§31: the American calendar lands where the record puts it). What the team can change is the count: the dead are recorded as anywhere from 8 to 18, and the difference is how well the team did. After: the lake closes for winter; and on 28 April 1937 Capt. George R. Donner vanishes from a cabin locked on the inside, near Port Washington, on his 58th birthday (R07, R06) — the calling has not stopped.

The divergence underneath — Holds (domestic calendar) — and the crash itself is the rulebook's: colonial paper, German sanctions, margin calls (§31). The Wisconsin goes down on the night the paper stops being worth anything.

Another road — Salt (§28): ordinary rounds pass through ghosts; a ship's galley has salt. A Holy Man Sends it back — or last rites for the drowned on the Kenosha beach (IHS will send a priest and want to know how). No wheelman to get to Kenosha in a gale? It is the same road north the Edithton buyers drove (Seed 9). The ground: where the Andaste's eleven life-jacketed dead came ashore.

Twist — On the morning of 30 October, a new driftwood note is found on the Kenosha beach in Albert Anderson's hand. It gives the Wisconsin's dead as a number — the number the papers could never agree on — and it is signed by one more name than A.L.A.

Seed 29 — The Society of the Living Dead — proposal, not canon

Pitch: In a river town eighty miles from Chicago, women whose bones glow testify from their deathbeds against the company that told them the paint was harmless — and the dead painters are still in the room, because they are still in the paint.

Real anchors

  • The Radium Girls of Ottawa, Illinois — R13 (with V_R13). Radium Dial Company, 1022 Columbus Street, a former high-school building; the girls "lip-pointed" their brushes and were told it was harmless. Catherine Wolfe Donohue, ill from 1925, fired in 1931 ("my limping was causing much talk"), sued with four others; by 1938 carried to the hearings on a stretcher; in February 1938 the hearing continued at her home; the Illinois State Journal of 6 April 1938 called the plaintiffs "the society of the living dead"; she died 27 July 1938, under 60 pounds. Margaret "Peg" Looney, who started at 17, was told she had diphtheria and quarantined in the company hospital; she died at 24. Chicago attorney Leonard Grossman took the case pro bono. Joseph A. Kelly Sr., ousted from Radium Dial in 1934, reopened "a few blocks away" as Luminous Processes, which painted dials until 1978. (The 6 July 1938 award figure is UNSUPPORTED — not used.)
  • Rulebook §32: the Kabbalahs are "contracted by the Church for work it forbids its own people" — chief among them the door to the dead (§19).

The three layers

  • What the papers said: industrial poisoning; a courtroom at a bedside; "the society of the living dead."
  • What the folklore says: none recorded in the pages read. Proposal: Ottawa children say the girls glow in their graves.
  • What is actually there: ghosts — the dead dial painters, bound into the glow they swallowed. Every dial painted from the same stock carries a sliver of a dead girl, ticking on a wrist somewhere in America. They cannot pass. They gather where they can: at 1022 Columbus Street, and at Catherine Donohue's bedside.

Who sends the team

  • Kabbalah of David — contracted by IHS for the part IHS will not do: someone has to Open the Door and ask the dead where they are. The Kabbalah wants a full record of what it hears, as payment.
  • IHS — the women are its parishioners (proposal). It wants the dead released with last rites — and no record at all of how they were found. The contractor wants to write it down; the client wants it forgotten.

Three clue sites

  1. 1022 Columbus Street (R13) — the old workroom; the lip-pointing tables; a glow in the floorboards at night.
  2. Catherine Donohue's bedside — the hearing room in her home; costed clue: Open the Door (§19: 1 Mana + 1 Sanity + 1 Focus) to Peg Looney in that room — the IHS agent stands aside and does not report it; Peg comes easily, because she never left.
  3. Luminous Processes, a few blocks away — Kelly's new firm, still buying from the same stock; its shipping book says where the dials went.

The clock — Start: February 1938, the bedside hearing. 6 April 1938: the newspaper names them "the society of the living dead." Real deadline: 27 July 1938, Catherine Donohue's death — after that, the one living person the dead painters gather around is gone. After: the dials are still ticking across the country; finding them is a campaign, one wrist at a time.

The divergence underneath — Holds (domestic calendar). Proposal — historical sense: in this world the dial trade's biggest buyer is the Army of occupation in Mexico, and a good share of the glowing watches are on soldiers' wrists in Sonora.

Another road — A Holy Man's last rites let the dead go quietly (§11); without one, the Kabbalah's own rite does the same, and IHS will pretend it did it. No Spirit Medium? The Echoes are free at the workroom tables (§19), and the shipping book at Luminous Processes gives the rest to anyone who can read an invoice. The guy: Leonard Grossman, who has read every page of the company's records.

Twist — The dead painters are why Catherine Donohue is still alive. They have been keeping her breathing long enough to win. Release them, and she dies — on 27 July, exactly as the record says — and the team will never know if they chose the date or only kept it.

Seed 30 — The Underpass at Randolph Street — proposal, not canon

Pitch: A Tribune legman is shot in the back of the head in a crowded pedestrian tunnel on his way to the races — eight days after the Treasury reached out to make him its informant — and he is still walking to the 1:30 train.

Real anchors

  • The murder of Jake Lingle, 9 June 1930 — R01. In the pedestrian underpass to the Illinois Central's Randolph Street station, beneath Michigan Avenue at Randolph; heading for the 1:30 p.m. train to the races; shot once in the back of the head with a .38; dozens saw a thin, blond, blue-eyed gunman. On $65 a week he had more than $60,000 in the bank, two homes and a suite at the Morrison Hotel; he fixed beer prices; a boyhood friend of Police Commissioner William F. Russell. Frank J. Wilson's own report (21 Dec 1933): on 1 June 1930 Wilson had arranged to interview Lingle at the Tribune to recruit him as an informant on Capone's finances. Within three days federal agents came asking what the Tribune knew of Capone's income. In early November 1930 Capone offered to hand over "the dead body of the murderer." Leo Vincent Brothers of St. Louis, arrested January 1931, convicted — Wilson notes "no motive was proven."
  • Frank J. Wilson and the ledger — R01. Ordered to Chicago 19 May 1930; the Hawthorne Smoke Shop ledger, seized in 1926, sat unrecognised four years among a departed agent's papers; cashier Leslie Shumway found in Miami 18 Feb 1931; Fred Ries hidden in South America on the Chicago Association of Commerce's money. Wilson became Chief of the Secret Service later in the decade.
  • The Morrison Hotel, Madison & Clark — R09.

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a crusading reporter murdered by gangsters — until the bank book came out, and the Tribune's martyr became a fixer.
  • What the folklore says: none recorded. Proposal: the Illinois Central porters say a man in a straw hat hurries through the underpass at 1:29 every afternoon and never reaches the platform.
  • What is actually there: a ghost — Lingle, who does not know he missed the train. He kept a notebook of who paid whom in the Loop — the police, the Outfit, the Tribune, the Treasury — and it was never recovered. He knows where it is. He is the only one who does.

Who sends the team

  • Section 13 — the Bureau wants Capone as badly as the Treasury does. It wants the notebook first, while the Tribune is still grieving and before the Treasury men arrive.
  • The Secret Service — the rulebook says the Service took Capone (§32); in 1930 its road runs through Wilson's Treasury unit. It wants the notebook too, and it wants Section 13 kept off its informant's grave. The two federal factions race each other to a dead man.

Three clue sites

  1. The Randolph Street underpass (R01) — the spot; the 1:29 walker. Echoes are free there (§19); costed clue: Open the Door (1 Mana + 1 Sanity + 1 Focus) gets Lingle himself — who will talk about the races and not about the notebook until someone pays him in his own coin: a tip on the next day's card.
  2. The Morrison Hotel suite, Madison & Clark (R09) — searched by the police, the Tribune and someone else; the hat-box nobody opened.
  3. The Tribune — Lingle's desk; the editor who arranged Wilson's appointment for 1 June, and knows who else in the building knew.

The clock — Start: the evening of 9 June 1930. Real deadline: 12 June 1930 — "within three days" the federal agents come asking, and whoever reaches the notebook first owns the Capone case. After: Capone's offer of a dead killer (November 1930); Brothers arrested (January 1931); Capone convicted (October 1931, R01) — and the notebook decides which federal badge gets the credit.

The divergence underneath — BENDS through the factions (R01): in this world the Secret Service and K. Edgar Hoover's Bureau are rivals, and the Service "knew what Capone was and took him anyway" (§32). The race for Lingle's notebook is where that rivalry starts.

Another road — No Spirit Medium? The notebook's hiding place is findable the long way: Lingle's bank book, his brokerage account, his bookmaker. The guy: Commissioner Russell's driver, who drove Lingle more often than he drove Russell. The air: in June 1930 Chicago's police bulletins still went out over WGN, interrupting its programmes; the police's own station opened a week later, on 16 June 1930 (R13) — the radio log of the afternoon of the 9th is at WGN.

Twist — Lingle remembers the thin blond gunman's face, and it belongs to a clerk who will be working at Frank Wilson's elbow by the autumn. The leak that killed the Treasury's informant was inside the Treasury — and the Secret Service would rather the Bureau never learned it.
