R29 — Chicago's hinterland, 1925–1940: the collar counties, downstate Illinois and Northwest Indiana

  • Lane: R29 — places an hour or a day from the Loop with something strange in the record: the collar counties and suburbs (Lake, McHenry, Kane, DuPage, Will, south and west Cook), downstate Illinois (the Illinois River towns, Springfield, "Bloody Williamson", the Mississippi bluffs), and Northwest Indiana ("the Region": Gary, Hammond, Whiting, East Chicago, the Dunes, Michigan City, La Porte, Valparaiso, Crown Point, South Bend); the roads and interurbans that tie them to Chicago.
  • Date: 2026-09-26
  • Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent)
  • Overview: Fifty-nine entries on places an hour to a day from the Loop, most of them not in any other file of this sweep. The owner's Woodstock lead turns out to sit in a dense ring of real strangeness: in Woodstock itself, Welles filmed himself as Death (The Hearts of Age, 1934), the Todd School had its own radio station and airstrip, and Debs sat in the jail on the same square. Geneva had Riverbank, where Colonel Fabyan paid a Harvard physicist to tune a levitation machine decoded from "Bacon's" Shakespeare. Waukegan had the carnival man who touched Ray Bradbury with an electric sword. Evanston holds Charles Dawes's house, which in the setting is an ex-president's. Downstate, Birger's 1928 hanging (confirmed as the state's last public hanging) and Vachel Lindsay's 1931 suicide beside the governor's mansion stand out, and Route 66 and the Lincoln Highway work as campaign roads. In the Region: a 200-foot dune carried off in canning jars, the Klan nearly buying Valparaiso University, Notre Dame students unrobing Klansmen, and a dispute over Diana of the Dunes's death (1925 or 1930). Several entries BEND at the 1916 break, among them Great Lakes Naval Station's war boom, East Chicago's "Arsenal of America" name, Riverbank's WWI cipher school, the Hammond wreck's troop train, East St. Louis's wartime labour, Dawes's presidency and Cantigny. The Library of Congress newspaper search was rate-limited all session, so newspaper-only details are listed as leads.

Part A — Woodstock and McHenry County (the owner's lead town)

The Woodstock Opera House — built 1889; the Welles festival of July 1934 (already in the setting — deepened)

  • Where: 121 Van Buren St., on the town square, Woodstock, McHenry County, Illinois (~60 miles NW of the Loop, on the Chicago & North Western).
  • What happened: Built 1889 for $25,000 to plans by Smith Hoag of Elgin (contractor Simon Brink), the building was a whole civic machine under one roof: the city library, the council room, the justice court and the fire department downstairs, the auditorium upstairs. In July 1934 the 19-year-old Orson Welles (Todd class of 1931) ran a six-week Todd Theatre Festival there — Trilby, Hamlet and Tsar Paul — bringing in Hilton Edwards and Micheál MacLiammóir of Dublin's Gate Theatre, with Louise Prussing and Charles O'Neal. The stage was formally dedicated in 2013 as the site of Welles's American debut as a professional director. After the war (1947) the Woodstock Players trained Paul Newman, Geraldine Page, Tom Bosley, Betsy Palmer and Shelley Berman here.
  • The odd detail: The courtroom and the fire engines shared the building with the stage — a trial could be heard downstairs while Trilby's Svengali hypnotised his singer upstairs.
  • People: Orson Welles — director, 19; Hilton Edwards & Micheál MacLiammóir — Gate Theatre, Dublin; Louise Prussing — actress; Charles "Blackie" O'Neal — actor (father of Ryan O'Neal — from memory, UNVERIFIED).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodstock_Opera_House ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodstock,_Illinois
  • Confidence: High (dates, plays, Gate actors). A ghost legend ("Elvira", a seat in the balcony) is widely told locally but not on the pages read — UNVERIFIED, do not use as record.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. BENDS slightly: the Gate Theatre men come from a free Ireland in the setting (Ireland freed by Berlin's peace) — they arrive as citizens of a German-made republic.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Dublin actors are the perfect cover for a Kabbalah or IHS courier crossing from Europe in July 1934; Welles never knew what his guests carried in their trunks.

The Hearts of Age — Welles plays Death on the Todd campus — summer 1934

  • Where: Todd School for Boys, Woodstock (filmed during the July 1934 festival).
  • What happened: An 8-minute silent surrealist short co-directed by Welles and his college friend William Vance. Welles plays Death; his future first wife Virginia Nicolson plays an old woman who rides a rocking bell pulled by a bell-ringer (Edgerton Paul, in blackface), is courted on a stair, and is found dead inside a grand piano; the film ends on tombstone-shaped cards bearing epitaphs. Vance gave the only print to the Greenwich (Connecticut) Public Library; film historian Joseph McBride announced its rediscovery in Film Quarterly, spring 1970. Welles is quoted (via cinematographer Gary Graver): "Why did Joe have to discover that film?"
  • The odd detail: A teenage Welles made himself up as Death and filmed it in the same fortnight he was staging Trilby — and later wished the film had stayed lost.
  • People: Orson Welles — Death, age 19; Virginia Nicolson — the old woman; William Vance — co-director; Edgerton Paul — the bell-ringer; Joseph McBride — rediscoverer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hearts_of_Age
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Something in the Woodstock leads can be seen in the frames — a figure no cast list accounts for. The single print's odd route (Woodstock → Vance → a Connecticut library) is a real chain of custody for a cursed object.

The Todd School for Boys under Roger Hill — 1929–1954

  • Where: Campus bounded by McHenry Ave, Seminary Ave, Northampton St and Mansfield Ave, Woodstock; 20 acres of woods to the north; its own airstrip (now Marian Central Catholic High School).
  • What happened: Founded 1848 by the Rev. Richard K. Todd (from Vermont), renamed repeatedly (Woodstock University 1861 … Todd Seminary for Boys 1873, Todd School for Boys 1930). Headmaster Roger Hill (1929–54) ran it as a progressive school where boys were "created creators": a working farm, a radio station, a theatre company with touring buses, film production, and flight training with a simulator. Welles attended September 1926–1931 (ages 11–16), directing 8–10 plays a year (a Dr. Faustus, an experimental Everyman); the Todd troupe won first prize at the 1933 Century of Progress drama festival with Twelfth Night; Hill and Welles co-wrote Everybody's Shakespeare (1934–41, 100,000+ copies by 1942). Winter campus: Todd Island, Marathon Key, Florida; summer camp: Camp Tosebo, Onekama, Michigan. Closed 1954; only Rogers Hall survives (apartments).
  • The odd detail: A boys' boarding school an hour from Chicago with its own radio station and its own airfield — boys could be flown in and out without passing a railway station. (Later alumni: physicist Robert R. Wilson; cartoonist Gahan Wilson.)
  • People: Roger "Skipper" Hill — headmaster; Orson Welles — pupil 1926–31; Richard K. Todd — founder.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Todd_School_for_Boys
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A school that trains boys to broadcast, fly and perform is exactly what the Hunter Guild or FBI Section 13 would scout for recruits; Hill's radio station is a second transmitter in a town that already has a lead.

Eugene Debs in the Woodstock jail — 1895 (living memory)

  • Where: Old McHenry County Courthouse and jail (built 1844), on the Woodstock square — a short walk from the Opera House.
  • What happened: After the 1894 Pullman strike, the socialist leader Eugene V. Debs served his federal contempt sentence in the Woodstock jail in 1895. He later called it one of the "greatest school[s]" — he read his way into socialism there. He was released in November 1895 to crowds gathered in Woodstock Square.
  • The odd detail: The founding cell of American socialism is a county lock-up on the same square as the theatre the setting already uses.
  • People: Eugene V. Debs — railway union leader, later five-time Socialist presidential candidate.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodstock,_Illinois
  • Confidence: High on the jail and year; the months of his stay are not on the page (UNVERIFIED).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. (In the setting, with no Red Scare and the IWW never broken, Debs's memory is a live political cult rather than a martyr's.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The jail's graffiti or a cell no one will use; the Debs faithful make an annual November pilgrimage to the square — a crowd that covers anything.

"Typewriter City" and Chester Gould — Woodstock, 1919 / 1931 onward

  • Where: Woodstock Typewriter Co. works (Emerson Typewriter Co. until 1919; the Oliver Typewriter Co. also had a plant); Chester Gould's home in Woodstock.
  • What happened: By 1922 Woodstock workers had built more than half the world's typewriters; Emerson became the Woodstock Typewriter Company in 1919 and the factory ran until 1970. Chester Gould, a Tribune cartoonist, launched Dick Tracy on Sunday 4 October 1931 (first in the Detroit Mirror), inspired — by his own account — by a Sherlock Holmes radio series and newspaper stories about Capone, and drew it for 46 years "from his home in Woodstock".
  • The odd detail: The town that typed half the world's letters is where the most famous fictional Chicago detective was drawn — every ransom note in the decade may have come off a Woodstock machine.
  • People: Chester Gould — creator of Dick Tracy.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodstock,_Illinois ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester_Gould
  • Confidence: High on the typewriter figure and Tracy's launch; Medium on when Gould physically moved to Woodstock (the page says he drew it from Woodstock "for the next 46 years" from 1931 — the move date itself is not given).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Typewriters have signatures (typefaces wear uniquely); a Woodstock factory serial ledger identifies which machine typed a threatening letter. Gould's strip keeps "guessing" real cases a little too well — see R08's Dick Tracy entry.

Part B — The Fox Valley and Kane County (Elgin, Geneva, St. Charles, Batavia, Aurora)

Riverbank: Colonel Fabyan's estate of codes, sound and a levitation machine — Geneva, 1912–1936

  • Where: Riverbank estate, Geneva, Kane County, Illinois (~40 miles west of Chicago), on the Fox River; 325 acres. Now Fabyan Forest Preserve (the Fabyan Villa is a museum); Riverbank Laboratories still tests acoustics.
  • What happened: The textile millionaire Col. George Fabyan built a private research estate: a Japanese garden, a private zoo, a Roman-style pool, a lighthouse, and a Dutch windmill moved to the site in 1914. His Riverbank Laboratories (building 1912, founded 1913) housed a team hunting ciphers that "proved" Francis Bacon wrote Shakespeare (led by Elizabeth Wells Gallup); a young geneticist-turned-photographer William F. Friedman and a Shakespeare scholar Elizebeth Smith met there, married, and became America's founding codebreakers (their book later disproved the Bacon ciphers). In 1913 Fabyan hired Harvard's Wallace Sabine to tune an acoustic levitation machine built to specifications decoded from a Bacon text; Sabine persuaded him it would not work, and the lab became a serious acoustics house under Paul Sabine. The NSA's 1993 plaque credits Riverbank with cryptologic training "on the eve of America's entry into World War I". Fabyan died 17 May 1936 (pleurisy); his widow Nelle died 1939; Kane County bought the estate for $70,500.
  • The odd detail: A millionaire paid a Harvard physicist to make a machine that would lift objects by sound, from instructions he believed were hidden in Shakespeare.
  • People: George Fabyan — "Colonel", patron; Elizabeth Wells Gallup — Baconian cipher-hunter; William & Elizebeth Friedman — codebreakers; Wallace Sabine / Paul Sabine — acousticians; Agnes Meyer Driscoll — later Navy codebreaker, also on staff.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Fabyan ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverbank_Laboratories
  • Confidence: High on the estate, staff, the levitation commission and the death date. The famous "furniture hung from chains" detail is not on the pages read — UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Riverbank's WWI army cipher school prepared for an American entry into the Great War that, in the setting, never came. Re-ground it as training for the Southern War in Mexico (1916–17) — which fits the date better anyway (the Zimmermann-era Mexican cipher traffic).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The most on-the-nose find in this lane: a real estate outside Chicago where a rich man hunted hidden texts for a machine that defies gravity by sound. In 1936 Fabyan dies and his papers go somewhere; the Secret Service (old guard, cipher-literate through the Friedmans' orbit) and FBI Section 13 both want the Bacon notebooks.

Elgin: the watch works, the observatory that kept America's time, and the road races — 1866–1964; races 1910–1920 and 26 Aug 1933

  • Where: Elgin National Watch Company works, Elgin, Kane County (~30–40 miles NW); the company observatory (built 1910); the 8.5-mile road circuit west of town.
  • What happened: For nearly a century Elgin's works were "the world's largest site dedicated to watchmaking", making about half of all American pocket watches. In 1910 the company built its own astronomical observatory to set precise time for its watches — railway time for much of the country ran, in effect, off an Elgin star-sighting. The Elgin National Road Races (1910–1920) ran on 8.5 miles of oil-soaked dirt and gravel with "no crossroads, railroad tracks, high hills or towns"; five drivers died (three in 1911, two in 1914); farmers' objections ended them after 1920. They were revived for the World's Fair on 26 August 1933: Phil Shafer won the National Trophy in a Buick, and one 1933 race is counted among the first organised stock-car races in the US.
  • The odd detail: A factory with a telescope — the watch company checked the stars nightly so that a whole region's clocks would agree.
  • People: Phil Shafer — 1933 winner (Buick).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elgin_National_Watch_Company ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elgin_National_Road_Races
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (The page says the races were "suspended during World War I" — in the setting the pause would be the Southern War, 1916–17.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The owner already builds plots on real skies (the 1936 eclipse); the Elgin observatory is the one place in Illinois whose log records the sky every night to the second — a Section 13 analyst checks it for a missing minute. Elgin's State Hospital (R09) is across town; Sam Cowley died in Elgin's Sherman Hospital in 1934 (R02).

Bellevue Place — the private asylum where Mary Todd Lincoln was committed — Batavia, 1867–1965 (living memory)

  • Where: Batavia, Kane County, on the Fox River; limestone Greek Revival building of 1853–54 (architect Elijah Shumway Town), first the Batavia Institute academy.
  • What happened: From 1867 the old academy was a private sanitarium, Bellevue Place. In 1875 a Chicago court ruled that Mary Todd Lincoln had behaved "irrationally" and committed her here; she stayed less than four months before release to her sister Elizabeth in Springfield. The sanitarium ran until 1965, then became "Fox Hill Home" for unwed mothers, then apartments (still private).
  • The odd detail: A president's widow was locked in a quiet Fox River town asylum — and it was still taking private patients all through the 1930s.
  • People: Mary Todd Lincoln — patient, 1875; Robert Todd Lincoln — the son who had her committed (general knowledge, not on the page read).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellevue_Place
  • Confidence: High on the building and 1875; its 1930s patients are not documented on the page.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened; still operating in the window.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A discreet private asylum for rich families' inconvenient relatives, 40 miles from the Gold Coast — where a vampire family "rests" a relative who has started to talk; the Lincoln connection ties it to the Lincoln-tomb plot (R10).

Hotel Baker and its glass dance floor — St. Charles, opened 2 June 1928

  • Where: 100 W. Main St., St. Charles, Kane County (street address from general knowledge — UNVERIFIED), on the Fox River; the Arcada Theatre (1926) across the street.
  • What happened: Col. Edward J. Baker, a St. Charles farmer, inherited a fortune from his brother-in-law John W. "Bet-a-Million" Gates (Texaco) and spent it on his home town. The hotel opened 2 June 1928 with a dinner for 301 and rooms at $2.50; its Rainbow Room had an illuminated dance floor lit from beneath by 2,620 coloured lights, and bands including Louis Armstrong and Lawrence Welk played there. Baker's niece Dellora Norris ran it until 1970.
  • The odd detail: A farm town of a few thousand had a dance floor that glowed from underneath — Depression-era couples danced on light.
  • People: Col. Edward J. Baker — benefactor; John W. Gates — the fortune; Dellora Norris — niece and heir.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotel_Baker
  • Confidence: High (dates, light count, performers). No ghost story appears on the page read.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A small-town hotel built by one man's inherited oil money is the perfect "neutral ground" for a meeting of factions away from Chicago eyes; the lit floor shows every footprint and every shadow — and something that casts none.

The Leaning Tower of Niles — a water tank disguised as Pisa — 1934

  • Where: 6280 W. Touhy Ave., Niles, Cook County (NW suburbs).
  • What happened: Industrialist Robert Ilg (Ilg Hot Air Electric Ventilating Co., Chicago) built Ilgair Park, a recreation park for his employees; its pools and artificial lake needed a big water tank and pumps, so in 1934 he hid them inside a 94-foot, half-size replica of the Leaning Tower of Pisa (architect Albert L. Farr). It contains five bells, three of them cast in Cavezzo, Italy in 1623, 1735 and 1747 — "how they came to be included in the tower is not known."
  • The odd detail: Seventeenth-century Italian church bells turned up in a Chicago fan-maker's water tower in 1934, and nobody knows how.
  • People: Robert Ilg — industrialist; Albert L. Farr — architect.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaning_Tower_of_Niles
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three bells taken from an Italian parish (by whom? sold by whom?) hang over a Protestant businessman's pool; IHS wants to know which church they came from and what they were rung against.

Part C — Lake County and the North Shore (Waukegan, Evanston, the Fox River lakes)

Mr. Electrico touches a boy with a live sword: "Live, forever!" — Waukegan, Labor Day weekend 1932

  • Where: A travelling carnival in Waukegan, Lake County (~40 miles north, on the lake); Bradbury's haunts: the Carnegie Library and the ravine that became "Green Town".
  • What happened: Ray Bradbury (born Waukegan, 22 August 1920) was twelve when, at a carnival on Labor Day weekend 1932, the sideshow performer Mr. Electrico touched him with an electrified sword and told him "Live, forever!" Bradbury credited the moment with making him a writer. The family moved to Tucson and back twice (1926–27, 1932–33) and left Waukegan for Los Angeles in 1934. His Waukegan became "Green Town, Illinois" in Dandelion Wine and Something Wicked This Way Comes — the novel of a dark carnival that comes to a lake town in October.
  • The odd detail: A carnival man with a charged blade picked one boy out of a crowd and commanded him to live forever.
  • People: Ray Bradbury — the boy; Mr. Electrico — carnival electric-man (real name not on the page read).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Bradbury ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waukegan,_Illinois
  • Confidence: High that Bradbury told it this way (it is his own account); the carnival's name and Electrico's identity are not confirmed here.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Waukegan in the setting is a lake-shore factory town (Johns-Manville asbestos from the early 1920s, the barbed-wire mill that needed the state militia in its 1919 strike, the Great Lakes sailors) with a travelling carnival every Labor Day. The owner's method writes itself: the real Mr. Electrico, the real words, and underneath — what he gave the boy.

The ex-president's house: Charles Dawes at 225 Greenwood Street — Evanston, 1909–1951

  • Where: 225 Greenwood St., Evanston (Cook County, first suburb north), a block from the lake; now the Evanston History Center.
  • What happened: A "rural French" château-style house built 1894 (architect H. Edwards Ficken of New York) for Northwestern official Robert Sheppard. Charles G. Dawes lived here from 1909 until his death in 1951; his widow stayed until 1957. The real Dawes won the 1925 Nobel Peace Prize for the German reparations plan, was Coolidge's vice-president, ambassador to Britain, and (general knowledge, not on the page — see R03) in 1932 head of the Reconstruction Finance Corporation just before its loan to his own bank. Descendant of William Dawes, who rode with Paul Revere.
  • The odd detail: The one national figure the setting makes president (1927–29) came home to a suburban house you can still tour — a president's house on an Evanston side street.
  • People: Charles Gates Dawes — banker, in the setting President 1927–29; his widow — lived on in the house until 1957 (her name is not on the page read).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Gates_Dawes_House
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — heavily. In the setting Dawes is President 1927–29 (succeeding Leonard Wood), not Coolidge's VP; there is no German reparations plan for him to win a Nobel with (Germany won — reparations run the other way). His house is therefore an ex-president's residence, presumably with a Secret Service detail on Greenwood Street through the 1930s.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Secret Service men guarding an ex-president in Evanston are the "old guard" faction's permanent foothold on the North Shore; what Dawes learned in office (the Wood death on the Boston operating table, Aug 1927) is filed in that house.

Rest Cottage — the Temperance Union's shrine and headquarters — Evanston, 1865 / HQ from 1900

  • Where: 1730 Chicago Ave., Evanston; the WCTU Administration ("Literature") Building (1910) behind it.
  • What happened: Built 1865 by Josiah Willard, the house became Frances Willard's "Rest Cottage" — where she rested between tours as president of the Woman's Christian Temperance Union. Two years after her death (1898) the WCTU made it its national headquarters; the 1910 Literature Building at the back poured out the temperance tracts behind national Prohibition. Seventeen rooms, oak and walnut floors; now a WCTU museum and National Historic Landmark.
  • The odd detail: The headquarters of the movement that made Prohibition sat in a dead woman's house kept as she left it — and Evanston stayed legally "dry" around it.
  • People: Frances E. Willard (1839–1898) — WCTU president; Josiah Willard — her father, builder.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Willard_House_(Evanston,_Illinois)
  • Confidence: High. (Evanston's dry status through the 1930s and her bicycle "Gladys" are from memory — UNVERIFIED.)
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Repeal still lands December 1933).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): December 1933, Repeal: the temperance women's shrine becomes the rallying point for the losing side. A women's society that kept a founder's rooms untouched for 35 years is a ready-made séance circle.

Fox River Grove: the ski jump and the burned ballroom — 1905 / 1923–1939

  • Where: Fox River Grove, McHenry County, on the Fox River by the Chicago & North Western line (~40 miles NW).
  • What happened: In 1905 Norwegian men from Chicago founded the Norge Ski Club here and built ski jumps — it is called the oldest continuously open ski club in the US. Eman Opatrny's Picnic Grove (1899: picnic plots, a shooting gallery, a horse track, six bars, a boathouse) burned in 1918. Louis Cernocky Sr. built the eight-sided Crystal Ballroom at "Louie's Place" in 1923 — Glenn Miller and Guy Lombardo played it, and members of the Dillinger and Barker-Karpis gangs were regulars (R02 has the gang side). In 1939 a strange incident — arson, kerosene-soaked floors, a kidnapping and planted bombs — hit the Crystal Ballroom, and it never reopened for dancing.
  • The odd detail: Ski-jumpers in the air over the Fox River in winter; gangsters at the ballroom bar in summer; and in 1939 the ballroom is soaked in kerosene and bombed.
  • People: Louis Cernocky Sr. — ballroom owner; Eman Opatrny — Picnic Grove owner; Baby Face Nelson — killed on nearby Route 14, 27 Nov 1934 (R02).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_River_Grove,_Illinois
  • Confidence: High on the club, the grove, the ballroom; the 1939 incident is on the page but its details (who, why) are not — needs a newspaper.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The 1939 attack on the ballroom is an unexplained event with kidnapping and bombs — the end of a feud that began when the gangs drank there; or something in the building someone wanted burned out.

The Chain O'Lakes resort country and the Mineola Hotel — Fox Lake, 1900s–1930s

  • Where: Fox Lake, Lake/McHenry counties (formerly Nippersink Point), on the Chain O'Lakes (15 lakes linked by the Fox River and channels); the Mineola Hotel on Fox Lake.
  • What happened: A village of a few hundred swelled each summer to about 20,000, with some 50 hotels and 2,000 cottages at its peak. The Mineola Hotel — possibly the largest wood-frame building in Illinois (NRHP 1979) — is "reported" to have been a Capone hideout, "although this has never been documented". The trumpeter Bunny Berigan was raised in Fox Lake (Wisconsin — the page result was ambiguous; UNVERIFIED which). A 1930 dam at Ingleside controls water from Long Lake into Fox Lake.
  • The odd detail: The largest wooden building in the state stands on a resort lake, and its gangster legend has no paper behind it.
  • People: Al Capone — alleged guest.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_Lake,_Illinois

Dwight: the Keeley "Gold Cure" and the women's reformatory with no real fence — 1879–1965 / opened 1930

The Lincoln Highway, the "Ideal Section" and the Boy Scouts' 2,400 markers — 1913–1928

  • Where: In Indiana through Dyer and Schererville (Lake County) and on to Valparaiso and South Bend; in Illinois through Chicago Heights (1916 routing), Aurora, Geneva, DeKalb, Dixon and Sterling; the first "Seedling Mile" near Malta, Illinois (Nov 1914).

The Insull interurbans: South Shore, North Shore, Aurora & Elgin — 1925–1932 and the crash

Part F — Northwest Indiana: "the Region" and the Dunes

Hoosier Slide: the mountain of sand that was carried away in jars — Michigan City, 1890–1920 (living memory)

  • Where: Lake Michigan shore at Michigan City, La Porte County, Indiana; the site is now NIPSCO's Michigan City Generating Station.
  • What happened: A 200-foot dune — once forested — was the great sight of the south shore, drawing about 180,000 visitors a year from Chicago to climb it and slide down its face; President McKinley visited in October 1899. From 1890 glassmakers found its iron-rich sand made blue-tinted glass: Pittsburgh Plate Glass, Ball (the famous blue canning jars) and Hemingray (electrical insulators) dug it out. It was 63 feet by 1894 and gone entirely by 1920 — 13.5 million short tons of sand. The power company built on the hole; the loss drove the later dune-protection campaigns.
  • The odd detail: A landmark hill was literally dispersed into millions of kitchen jars and telegraph-pole insulators across America — every blue Ball jar in a 1930s farm cellar holds a piece of it.
  • People: William McKinley — visitor, 1899.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoosier_Slide
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened (a 1930s adult remembers climbing it).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Something was buried in the dune, and when the dune was scattered it went with it — into jars sold in every town. A sympathetic-magic plot: the blue glass remembers the hill.

Diana of the Dunes: Alice Mabel Gray — dunes 1915; beaten 1922; death disputed (1925 or 1930); the ghost after

Around 1920–21 she took up with Paul Wilson, a fisherman and carpenter. In 1922 a man's body was found near Wren's Nest; Wilson was suspected and cleared; a deputy sheriff, Eugene Frank, accused the pair of robberies and in a confrontation Wilson was shot in the foot and Gray's skull was fractured. His wife Nell ran the campaigns from 1908. The 1930s were ruin: his housekeeper died in 1930, his daughter in 1932, and his eldest son killed himself in 1933. He died in Chicago on 6 November 1935, one week after his last sermon.
  • The odd detail: The man who preached Prohibition into law died two years after Repeal, in the city where a rescue mission on skid row had saved him fifty years earlier.
  • People: William Ashley "Billy" Sunday — evangelist; Helen "Nell" Thompson Sunday — his manager.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billy_Sunday
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The three deaths in four years around the Sunday household (1930, 1932, 1933) read, in the owner's three layers, as grief — as a curse — as a price. Winona Lake's Bible-conference grounds as the evangelicals' answer to the Theosophists at Wheaton.

The Grand Kankakee Marsh — "the Everglades of the North", drained 1882–1917 (living memory)

  • Where: The Kankakee River valley, NW Indiana and NE Illinois (~60 miles S/SE of Chicago): once 400,000 to over a million acres; English Lake and Beaver Lake (Indiana's largest lake until the 1880s).
  • What happened: The river once ran 240 miles in more than 2,000 meander bends through a vast marsh that held over 225 bird species — flocks that could "darken the sky", passenger pigeons, cranes, eagles. Between 1882 and 1917 syndicates and the Indiana legislature straightened it into "one of the largest drainage ditches in the U.S."; the loss has been put at one-fifth of America's migratory bird population, and Indiana lost 87% of its wetlands. The marsh had sheltered Native nations until the removal treaties (1821–36), and later the Underground Railroad and Black farming communities.
  • The odd detail: A 1930s Chicagoan over 40 remembered a wilderness of water an hour south of the city that no longer existed — Beaver Lake, gone from the map.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Kankakee_Marsh
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (mostly), happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Drain a marsh and whatever lived in it has to go somewhere — the towns on the old lake beds (and Kankakee's and Manteno's hospitals, R09/R13) are where it went. The Hunter Guild's hunting clubs lost their ground; what else did?

The East St. Louis massacre — 28 May and 1–3 July 1917 (living memory)

  • Where: East St. Louis, St. Clair County (across the Mississippi from St. Louis; the Eads Bridge).
  • What happened: The Black population rose from about 6,000 (1910) to 20,000 (1917) as migrants came for industrial jobs; when the Aluminum Ore Company hired Black strikebreakers, a 28 May labour meeting heard rumours of "Black men fraternizing with white women", and violence began. On 1 July white men fired into a Black neighbourhood; residents then fired on a second car — which carried police, killing a detective. Next day crowds came to see the bloodstained car, then rushed the Black districts. Congress's investigators counted at least 39 Black and 8 white dead (estimates run to 150); 6,000 were driven from their homes. On 28 July about 10,000 people walked in the Silent Parade down Fifth Avenue in New York.
  • The odd detail: The massacre was triggered by the exhibition of a bullet-riddled police car to a crowd.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_St._Louis_riots
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the page ties the labour shortage to America's Great War; in the setting it comes from the Southern War in Mexico (1916–17), which pulled men south at the same months. The event holds; its cause shifts.

Nauvoo: from the Mormons' city to the Icarians to a German Catholic wine town — 1839–1930s

  • Where: Nauvoo, Hancock County, on the Mississippi (~270 miles SW; "a day from Chicago").
  • What happened: Joseph Smith renamed Commerce "Nauvoo" in 1840; by 1844 it had about 12,000 people, "rivaling the size of Chicago"; after Smith's killing (1844) the Mormons were driven west. French utopian socialists — the Icarians of Étienne Cabet — took over 1849–1856 (peak ~500). Then Germans: Nauvoo was the largest German-speaking community in Illinois for fifty years, with wine and fruit; Prohibition ended the wine, and in the 1930s the Nauvoo Blue Cheese company began aging cheese in the old Icarian wine cellars. Baxter's Vineyards (1857) is Illinois's oldest winery.
  • The odd detail: Blue cheese maturing in the cellars dug by French socialists under a prophet's abandoned city.
  • People: Joseph Smith — founder; Étienne Cabet — Icarian leader.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nauvoo,_Illinois
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar; the town's German-speaking character BENDS in weight — in a world where Germany won, a German-speaking Illinois river town is a natural place for Berlin's cultural societies (see R23).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three cults layered in one town — Mormon, Icarian, German Catholic — and the cellars connect them. The owner's "three layers of truth" as literal geology.

Hofmann Tower and the dead "Niagara Park" — Lyons, 1908; killed by sewage and Prohibition

  • Where: 3910 Barry Point Rd., Lyons, Cook County (west suburbs), on the Des Plaines River at the old "Riverside Ford".
  • What happened: Brewer George Hofmann Jr. built a stone tower in 1908 with a horseshoe-shaped concrete dam beside it (replacing a mill dam at a natural limestone ford once used by Native travellers and settlers) as the centre of "Niagara Park" — picnics and boat rides. The park died of two things: Chicago's sewage fouling the Des Plaines, and Prohibition ruining a brewer's business. His heirs sold to the Cook County Forest Preserve in 1946; the tower is now closed as unsafe.
  • The odd detail: A brewer's pleasure-tower stood over a river turned to sewage, its park empty all through the 1930s — a stranded folly twelve miles from the Loop.
  • People: George Hofmann Jr. — brewer and builder.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hofmann_Tower
  • Confidence: High (the tower's height is not on the page).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): An abandoned tower by a dam at an ancient river ford — a crossing-place in every folklore sense — and nobody goes there because of the smell.

Argo: the world's largest corn plant and its company town — Summit, from 1907

  • Where: Argo, the south side of Summit / Bedford Park, Cook County (SW, along the canal and the Indiana Harbor Belt tracks at 59th St.; its own business street on 63rd).

Summit annexed Argo but the railway tracks, the dependence on one factory and its separate 63rd St. business district kept it a town apart. (Archer Avenue and Resurrection Mary's cemetery run just to the south-west — C06/R09.)
