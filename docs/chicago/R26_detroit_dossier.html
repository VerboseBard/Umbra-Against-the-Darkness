Overview

59 entries. The centre of gravity is the Black Legion in Detroit (17 entries), rebuilt from three period sources that the earlier lanes did not use: George Morris's August 1936 pamphlet The Black Legion Rides (a Communist Party pamphlet — partisan, but thick with names, addresses and quotations from the Detroit dailies and the confessions), and Reuters/United Press wire copy reprinted in the Hong Kong papers of May–December 1936 (Internet Archive). The alpha clue checks out twice over: "the first sixteen arrested for the murder of Poole" (Morris; R12 found the wire headline "Four of the 16 Held"), of whom twelve were arraigned on 25 May 1936 and eleven convicted — and a second sixteen arrested for the plot to kill Highland Park publisher Arthur Kingsley, including the Legion's Michigan commander and an ex-mayor then working as an investigator in the county prosecutor's own office. The "power vacuum" is real too: after nearly fifty convictions the Legion collapsed, its members "faded back into society", its commander stayed in Ohio under a syndicalism indictment, its women's auxiliary, rifle club and political front were never prosecuted as such — and within weeks a "White Legion" sent Detroit police a message in human blood. The rest of the file goes deeper than R07 on the fall of the Purple Gang and the Sicilian war that replaced it, Buckley's murder (the vanished radio engineer), the February 1933 run on the Union Guardian Trust (Reuters: Ford's $7.5 million deposit, the governor blaming Ford and retracting by evening), the aftermath of the Hunger March (wounded men chained to beds), the UAW's rise and 1938–39 civil war, the 1938 bombing season, Ford's Dearborn (Edison's last breath in 42 test tubes; the German Eagle handed over by the consul in Cleveland), radio (the Lone Ranger was born at WXYZ), the only execution in Michigan's history, and the 1940 graft indictment that caught the Legion's own prosecutor. Things that bend hardest under the 1916 break: every "Communist" frame (the Legion's enemies, the Hunger March, the UAW purge), Reuther's Gorky years, Valentiner (a Kaiser's General Staff officer running the DIA), Ford's 1938 German honour, Mooney's Japanese posting, Hamtramck's Poles, and the Canadian border.

A. The Black Legion in Detroit — the Poole case, the "sixteen", and what came after

Reconciling the alpha clue first. The alpha's newspaper line was "The Detroit paper has an arrest of 16 of the Black Legion." The real record supports it in two ways, both Detroit, both May–June 1936: (1) the first sixteen men held in the Poole murder (George Morris's August 1936 pamphlet: "the bits of information that were squeezed out of the first sixteen arrested for the murder of Poole"; R12 found the matching wire headline "Four of the 16 Held in Black Legion Slaying", Greencastle Daily Banner, 25 May 1936), of whom twelve were arraigned for murder on 25 May and eleven were finally convicted; and (2) a second sixteen, arrested for the plot to murder Highland Park publisher Arthur Kingsley — among them the Legion's Michigan commander, a former mayor who was then working as an investigator inside the county prosecutor's office, two factory policemen and a police officer (sixteen indicted, nine convicted). Either can be "the arrest of 16". The "power vacuum" is also real: see the entry on the Legion's collapse below.

The night ride of 12 May 1936 — from Findlater Temple to Dix Road

  • Where: Findlater Temple, corner of West Lafayette and Waterman avenues, southwest Detroit (the Wolverine Republican League's meeting hall, per the League's own letterhead); a dark stretch of Dix Road on the Dearborn/Detroit edge, just past the River Rouge bridge.
  • What happened: Charles A. Poole, a 32-year-old WPA organiser, was lured from home that night with the promise of a job (Michigan Public: an axle-factory job and a place on a baseball team) and driven out by Legionnaires straight from a meeting at Findlater Temple. By Morris's reconstruction from the confessions, the men "quickly formed into squads" for a "night ride"; two cars reached Dix Road ahead of the rest, which were held up when the River Rouge bridge opened. The missing car carried the black robes, hoods and rope for "a necktie party". "Colonel" Harvey Davis said "Well, I guess they must have got lost" and told Dayton Dean to "get Poole out"; Davis accused Poole of beating his wife ("She is in a hospital now with a broken rib"); Poole answered that she was in hospital having a baby. Dean fired five shots from a .45 automatic and three from a .38; a third man fired three more; Poole rolled into a ditch. The killers then "stepped into a beer garden and drank to a good night's work."
  • The odd detail: The lynching was improvised because the hoods were stuck behind a drawbridge — the robed ritual never happened; it was just a shooting on a roadside.
  • People: Charles A. Poole — WPA organiser, Catholic, murdered; Dayton Dean — triggerman; Harvey Davis — "Colonel", gave the order; Ervin D. Lee — present (Michigan Public names him with Dean).
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/TheBlackLegionRides (George Morris, The Black Legion Rides, Workers Library Publishers, New York, Aug 1936 — a Communist Party pamphlet; partisan, but built from the Detroit dailies and the confessions) ; https://www.michiganpublic.org/families-community/2017-02-28/how-a-1936-murder-exposed-and-destroyed-a-notorious-michigan-hate-group ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Legion_(political_movement)
  • Confidence: High on the killing, Dean and Davis; Medium on the drawbridge and beer-garden details (Morris only). Morris names the third shooter "Urban Lee", which looks like a conflation of Urban Lipps and Ervin Lee — UNVERIFIED which.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the WPA is Roosevelt's, 1935).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The car with the robes "got lost" at the Rouge bridge. Legend could say it was never found — the hoods, the rope and whoever was driving it; the Golden Circle has the ritual kit, and the Legion's Detroit men only ever had guns.

"The first sixteen arrested" — the Poole round-up — 22–27 May 1936 (the alpha's "arrest of 16")

  • Where: Detroit — Wayne County Jail; the Recorder's Court arraignment; Inspector John I. Navarre's Homicide Squad.
  • What happened: The story broke on 22 May 1936, ten days after the murder. Morris writes that the Legion's shape was learned "from the bits of information that were squeezed out of the first sixteen arrested for the murder of Poole". On 25 May twelve "unshaven and dishevelled" members "stood mutely on arraignment on murder warrants"; their attorneys entered not-guilty pleas and all were held without bail, while "John Doe" warrants went out for twelve more suspects (United Press, Detroit, 25 May). On 27 May their examination was adjourned six days at the prosecutor's request; defence counsel was a Mr Bernard Cruse. Homicide chief Navarre said of the prisoners: "whoever organized that outfit was no slouch. Those babies fairly cried before they talked." By August, Morris counted more than sixty members arrested (his OCR'd text reads "CO", most likely 60).
  • The odd detail: Hardened night-riders who "fairly cried before they talked" — because each carried the bullet that was his promised punishment.
  • People: John I. Navarre — Detroit Homicide Squad inspector; Bernard Cruse — defence counsel (per the wire; spelling unconfirmed); the twelve arraigned (named in the trials entry).
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/TheBlackLegionRides ; https://archive.org/details/NPTG19360526 (Hong Kong Telegraph 26 May 1936, UP wire "Detroit, May 25") ; https://archive.org/details/NPTG19360528 (Hong Kong Telegraph 28 May 1936, "Examination adjourned")
  • Confidence: High on twelve arraigned 25 May and eleven convicted; Medium on "sixteen" as the first-held count (one pamphlet + one wire headline found by R12); the counsel's name is OCR — spelling Low.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Sixteen taken, twelve charged — four walked. The alpha clue becomes a question the players can actually answer: who were the four released, and who asked for them?

The Kingsley plot — the other sixteen, the blood pact and the blackjack — 1933, exposed 1936

  • Where: Highland Park, Michigan (the auto-town enclave inside Detroit, home of Ford's old Highland Park plant).
  • What happened: Arthur Kingsley published a Highland Park community paper and ran for mayor against N. Ray Markland, a Legionnaire. Dean testified that the chosen squad met in a field and made a "blood pact": each man "dipped a toothpick in his own blood and signed his name". A "colonel" then told Dean he had been chosen to kill Kingsley; Dean said he stalked him for weeks and once chased him for miles. Michigan commander Arthur Lupp and Markland met Dean several times to hurry him; Markland said, "My whole campaign will be ruined if you don't act quickly," and handed him a blackjack: "Take this, you might need it." When Dean told the story, sixteen were arrested — Lupp, Markland, two factory policemen, a police officer and several Highland Park city employees; sixteen were indicted for conspiracy to murder, and nine were convicted (one to five years each), Markland and Lupp among them. At the time of his arrest, Markland was employed as an investigator in Prosecutor McCrea's own office — "at that very moment he was busy on a Legion flogging case."
  • The odd detail: The man investigating a Legion flogging for the county was the Legion's own ex-mayor.
  • People: Arthur Kingsley — publisher and mayoral candidate, the target; N. Ray Markland — former Highland Park mayor, Legionnaire, prosecutor's investigator; Arthur F. Lupp Sr. — Michigan commander (paroled Dec 1938, died 24 July 1980 in Indiana aged 86); Roy Hepner — named by Morris as a leader of the plot.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Legion_(political_movement) ; https://archive.org/details/TheBlackLegionRides
  • Confidence: High on sixteen indicted/nine convicted (Wikipedia); Medium on the blood pact, the blackjack and Markland's job (Morris).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Sixteen names written in their own blood on a paper that was never entered as evidence. For an occult setting, a roster signed in blood is a contract; whoever holds that sheet holds sixteen men.

The trials and the sentences — 8 October and 21 December 1936

  • Where: Wayne County Recorder's Court, Detroit.
  • What happened: Twelve men were tried for Poole's murder; one was acquitted and eleven convicted. Life for first-degree murder: Dayton Dean, "Colonel" Harvey Davis, Ervin D. Lee, Urban Lipps, Paul R. Edwards, Edgar Baldwin, John Bannerman and Lowell Rushing. Second-degree: Thomas R. Craig (10–20 years), Virgil Morrow (5–15), Albert Stevens (7½–15), John S. Vincent (3–10). Reuters reported from Detroit on 8 October that Dean "was sentenced to life imprisonment to-day", the seventh Legionnaire found guilty of first-degree murder, and that at trial he still alleged Poole had ill-treated his wife before the birth — which the widow denied; the Legion's "Colonel" Davis and six others drew life the same week. On 21 December five Legionnaires were convicted of Silas Coleman's murder — the wire called Coleman "a negro war veteran" and said his body "had 18 bullet wounds". Dean died in prison on 18 January 1960; Urban Lipps's sentence was commuted in June 1960 by Governor G. Mennen Williams; Ervin Lee's in 1966 by Governor George Romney.
  • The odd detail: The killer kept repeating the wife-beating lie in open court, months after the widow had buried it.
  • People: the eleven named above; Mrs Poole — the widow; Silas Coleman — the second murder.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Legion_(political_movement) ; https://archive.org/details/NPTG19361009 (Hong Kong Telegraph 9 Oct 1936, "Detroit, Oct. 8") ; https://archive.org/details/NPCM19361010 (China Mail 10 Oct 1936) ; https://archive.org/details/NPTG19361230 (Hong Kong Telegraph 30 Dec 1936, "Detroit, Dec. 21")
  • Confidence: High. The acquitted twelfth man is not named in the sources read — UNVERIFIED. Coleman as a "war veteran" is the Reuters wording only.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — if Coleman was a Great War veteran, in the setting America never entered the war; he would be a veteran of the Southern War in Mexico (1916–17) or of the Army before it.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Eight life sentences at the state prison — eight Legionnaires in Jackson and Marquette through the decade. A prison is a place where an oath sworn "in the name of God and the Devil" keeps being enforced.

Judge Chenot's one-man grand jury and McCrea's membership card — June 1936

  • Where: Wayne County Circuit Court, Detroit.
  • What happened: On 1 June 1936 Circuit Judge James E. Chenot was constituted a one-man grand jury "to trace the sinister influence of the Black Legion on Detroit's Government", with two judges and a prominent politician to be subpoenaed, and the question of the prosecutor's own application to be examined; the same wire said Canadian authorities had joined the inquiry. Prosecutor Duncan McCrea had been hailed as "the great hope for a clean-up" until the Hearst Detroit Times printed a photostat of a Legion application card bearing his signature. He could not flatly deny it: he "might have signed" while campaigning, was "just a joiner", and noted the cards were disguised as automobile-insurance cards. Morris adds that another McCrea investigator was a Klan official and that the man whose name appeared as McCrea's sponsor on the card was fired. On 27 May McCrea had wired Attorney General Homer S. Cummings that the Legion operated in at least fifteen states, including New York and Chicago, and hauled bodies across state lines — a federal offence — hoping to pull the federal government in.
  • The odd detail: The Legion's application form was disguised as an auto-insurance card — a man could join without knowing, or say so afterwards.
  • People: James E. Chenot — circuit judge, one-man grand jury; Duncan McCrea — Wayne County prosecutor (the spelling "McRae" in some sources is wrong); Homer S. Cummings — U.S. Attorney General.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/NPTG19360602 (Hong Kong Telegraph 2 June 1936, UP "Detroit, June 1") ; https://archive.org/details/NPTG19360528 ; https://archive.org/details/TheBlackLegionRides
  • Confidence: High on Chenot and the card story's existence; Medium on the investigator details (Morris). McCrea's later fate — widely said to have been convicted in the 1939–41 Detroit graft cases — is UNVERIFIED this session (see Leads).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar; the Attorney General under Roosevelt holds. In the setting McCrea's telegram would land on the desk of K. Edgar Hoover's FBI — or of the rival Secret Service, depending on which one the prosecutor trusted.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A membership card disguised as insurance. FBI Section 13 could have a drawer of them — every signature a man can deny — and a prosecutor who owes Section 13 for keeping his quiet; the Secret Service, IHS's old ally, would want the same drawer for the opposite reason.

"Fifty suicides" and the execution list — May 1936

  • Where: Michigan statewide; the Michigan State Police.
  • What happened: Reuters reported from Detroit on 24 May 1936 that "fifty recent sudden deaths" were now attributed to the Legion and that police were "closely examining reports that fifty deaths recently reported as suicides were really murders". State Police Captain Ira H. Marmon ordered a review of every suspicious "suicide" and mysterious death in Michigan over the past three years, said the inquiry had found "almost unbelievable facts", and said he had evidence that fifty men were on the Legion's "execution list". George Eckhardt, a former Pontiac police chief, charged that a third of the Pontiac force were members. After the Coleman confession, Dean and James Roy Lorance claimed that if the Ford-owned mill pond near the Pinckney marsh were drained, seven more bodies of "thrill" victims would be found.
  • The odd detail: A Ford mill pond said to hold seven bodies — and, as far as I found, never drained.
  • People: Ira H. Marmon — Michigan State Police captain; George Eckhardt — former Pontiac chief; James Roy Lorance — killer (died of tuberculosis in prison 3 Jan 1938).
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/NPTG19360525 (Hong Kong Telegraph 25 May 1936, Reuters "Detroit, May 24") ; https://archive.org/details/NPTG19360526 ; https://archive.org/details/TheBlackLegionRides
  • Confidence: High that the claims were made and reported; the "fifty" is a police claim never proven. Whether the pond was ever searched is UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Fifty "suicides" re-opened in 1936 — a case list for any Great Lakes investigation. The Ford pond is a place a GM can drain.

Typhoid, death needles and cyanide through a keyhole — the Lupp–McCutcheon laboratory — 1935–36

  • Where: Detroit Department of Health (milk-inspection division and the city laboratory); a basement in the home of William Guthrie.
  • What happened: Arthur Lupp, the Legion's Michigan commander, was a Detroit Board of Health milk inspector; Charles T. McCutcheon was the department's chemist and bacteriologist. Guthrie, a Legion "intelligence man", said the two came to his house to see whether typhoid could be bred in his basement, for injection into milk and cheese sold in Jewish markets (McCutcheon was asked whether germs could be preserved in cottage cheese). McCutcheon told the prosecutor that Lupp also discussed poisoned "death needles" to be jabbed into people in a crowd, a "cyanide gas" to be pumped into a room through a keyhole, and a bomb "the size of a cigarette" to blow up a car. The Health Department fired McCutcheon for keeping the plot to himself for over a year — and disclosed that he had been doing bacteriological experiments at the city laboratory that were no part of his job. He said the Legion shadowed him and would kill him if it came out.
  • The odd detail: The city's own laboratory, used after hours for experiments nobody assigned.
  • People: Arthur F. Lupp Sr. — milk inspector, commander; Charles T. McCutcheon — city bacteriologist; William Guthrie — Legion "intelligence man", informant.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Legion_(political_movement) ; https://archive.org/details/TheBlackLegionRides
  • Confidence: High on the typhoid plot (Wikipedia); Medium on the needles, keyhole gas, cigarette bomb and the lab experiments (Morris, from the prosecutor's statements).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): What was McCutcheon actually growing in the city lab? The period reading is typhoid. The occult layer: a culture that is not a germ.

The Legion's "job service" and the union wreckers — 1933–36

  • Where: Ecorse (Great Lakes Steel); the Hudson Motor Car plant (east side); Pontiac; Ford, Budd Wheel and other Detroit plants.
  • What happened: Morris argued that most men arrested or named held public jobs, and that the Legion placed members through officials such as M. Wesson Dickinson, Michigan's superintendent of private employment bureaus (who also endorsed Lupp's pistol permit), and Ecorse Village's employment director, who fed Legionnaires into Great Lakes Steel. Isaac "Peg-Leg" White, a retired one-legged Detroit policeman and former Klan official, told the Detroit News (2 June 1936) that "a few years ago" he had carried typed lists of alleged Communists to Hudson, Ford, Budd Wheel "and all the plants that had strikes or threats of strikes" — "the personnel departments… thanked us. It was merely a courtesy proposition." Hudson's A.F. of L. local (about 7,000 members) and the Pontiac local (4,000) broke away and collapsed; Pontiac union president Tice Woody told Morris he had joined the Legion thinking it would help labour, only to find Hudson local president Arthur Greer and Richard L. Byrd — Roosevelt's appointee as labour's representative on the Automobile Labor Board — already members. The Detroit Federation of Labor's Frank X. Martel set up a committee of five to root Legionnaires out of the unions.
  • The odd detail: A one-legged ex-cop walking typed blacklists into the personnel offices of Detroit's car plants as "a courtesy".
  • People: Isaac "Peg-Leg" White — ex-policeman, list-carrier; M. Wesson Dickinson — state employment official; Tice Woody, Arthur Greer, Richard L. Byrd — union men named as members; Frank X. Martel — Detroit Federation of Labor president.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/TheBlackLegionRides ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Legion_(political_movement)
  • Confidence: Medium — Morris is a Communist pamphleteer with a thesis (the Legion as the bosses' weapon); White's quoted words are from the Detroit News. Treat Greer/Byrd membership as allegation.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Morris frames every target as a "Communist"; with no Soviet Union and no Red Scare in the setting, the blacklists would name "anarchists", "Socialists", "German agitators" or simply "union men".
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A blacklist is a list of names typed by someone else and carried by a man who "never was any good at remembering names". Who typed it is the question.

The Wolverine Republican League — the Legion's respectable front — 1935–36

  • Where: Findlater Temple (West Lafayette at Waterman); the office of attorney Harry Z. Marx, the League's address; Adrian, Michigan (1935 arrest).
  • What happened: Morris calls the Wolverine Republican League (or Club) "the first Black Legion organization uncovered": five of the Poole killers were its officers, and its president, L. J. Black — clerk to Judge L. Eugene Sharp — was arrested in the Kingsley and Voisine plots. A League letter, signed by secretary Floyd Nuget, invited members to its annual meeting at Findlater Temple on 11 May (the eve of Poole's murder) and enclosed nominating petitions for former Governor Wilber M. Brucker's 1936 Senate run; Brucker gave his first campaign speech at a League meeting. Poole's killer John Bannerman told the Detroit News he "worked hard for Wilber M. Brucker and Governor Fitzgerald" and handed Brucker ten petitions; Paul Edwards said "if this organization is good enough for Mr. Brucker… it's good enough for me"; Edgar Baldwin said he "joined the night Brucker spoke". When three Legionnaires were caught near Adrian in 1935 with black hoods, pistols and rope in their car, Marx and Effinger went to the State Police to plead for their release. Judge Sharp endorsed Lupp's pistol permit. (Michigan Public, 2017, says Brucker was "associated with the organization"; he later became Secretary of the Army.)
  • The odd detail: The annual meeting was on 11 May 1936 — the killers' political club met in the same hall the night before the murder ride began there.
  • People: Wilber M. Brucker — former governor (1931–33), Senate candidate; L. J. Black — League president, judge's clerk; Harry Z. Marx — attorney, League director; L. Eugene Sharp — judge; Floyd Nuget — secretary (name as printed).
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/TheBlackLegionRides ; https://www.michiganpublic.org/families-community/2017-02-28/how-a-1936-murder-exposed-and-destroyed-a-notorious-michigan-hate-group
  • Confidence: Medium — the quotations are from the Detroit News as reprinted by Morris (who misdates them "1935"); Brucker's knowledge of the Legion is not established. The 11 May date comes from the reproduced letter.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Brucker was governor 1931–33 in the real record; nothing in the break touches Michigan's statehouse).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A political club with a lodge hall is how a cult hides in a population. The Wolverine League's membership list is the population; the Findlater Temple basement is the cult.

The Wayne County Rifle and Pistol Club and the National Guard letter — 1930s

  • Where: downtown Detroit (the club); the Michigan National Guard cavalry.
  • What happened: Morris reports that Dean and many Legion leaders took regular target practice at the Wayne County Rifle and Pistol Club, "regularly chartered", which "received its share of free ammunition from the United States War Department". Congressman Samuel Dickstein of New York told the House he could trace the Legion's origin to correspondence between Dr Samuel J. Rubley, a captain in the Michigan National Guard's cavalry, and William Dudley Pelley of the Silver Shirts, and read a letter: "…hope to have 50 Klansmen mounted in two weeks' time… I may be exceptionally blood-thirsty but I feel that the later winter snows will be tinged scarlet in the streets of Detroit." Each recruit was given a .38 bullet, and "You can't quit the organization unless you are six feet under."
  • The odd detail: Legion gunmen trained on federal ammunition, through a legal rifle club — the "armoury" was the War Department's.
  • People: Samuel Dickstein — New York congressman; Samuel J. Rubley — Guard captain and doctor (name as OCR'd); William Dudley Pelley — Silver Shirts (R10 covers him).
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/TheBlackLegionRides
  • Confidence: Medium-Low — single partisan source; the Rubley letter and the club's ammunition are UNVERIFIED elsewhere.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Pelley exists; his Silver Shirts' 1933 founding keyed to Hitler's chancellorship does not — see R10).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "The winter snows will be tinged scarlet in the streets of Detroit" is a line a cult prophet would write. Pelley was a medium; perhaps the letter is a prophecy, not a threat.

The Maurice Sugar plot and the forged "Communist" leaflet — 30 March 1935

  • Where: Northern High School, Detroit; the Workers' Camp in Oakland County.
  • What happened: Labour lawyer Maurice Sugar ran for Recorder's Court judge in spring 1935 with the Detroit Federation of Labor, independent unions and the Communists behind him, and polled about 63,000 votes. Dean confessed, after the Poole arrest, that on the night of Sugar's final rally (30 March) one Legion squad burned the Workers' Camp while another went to the school: he and League president Leslie Black were to cut the lights, six Legionnaires would set off stench bombs, and forged leaflets signed "Communist Party of America" would be thrown in the dark ("Negroes, rise against your white oppressors…"). Dean cut only the emergency wires; the janitor found them and a bundle of leaflets the next day, but the Board of Education and the Hearst press still called the leaflet genuine. When deputies searched Dean after the Poole arrest they found a clipping with Sugar's photograph: Dean said Lupp had ordered him to bomb Sugar, and records showed he had rented an apartment in Sugar's building for five months; he "got cold feet because too many people would have been killed".
  • The odd detail: The assassin had lived in the target's building for five months, and still carried his photograph.
  • People: Maurice Sugar — labour lawyer and candidate, later UAW general counsel; Dayton Dean; Leslie (L. J.) Black — League president.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/TheBlackLegionRides
  • Confidence: Medium — Morris only (Dean's confession as reported). Sugar's later UAW role is from memory — UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the real rally was a Communist-backed campaign; in the setting the forged leaflet would be signed by whatever the Legion's bogeyman is (the IWW, "anarchists", a German-paid party).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A forged leaflet that the city believed. The owner's "three layers" in one sheet of paper: the Legion wrote it, the Board of Education believed it, and the janitor kept the proof.

C. Money, towers and the 1933 collapse

The run on the Union Guardian Trust and the eight-day holiday — 14–15 February 1933

  • Where: the Guardian Building (Union Guardian Trust), 500 Griswold Street; the Detroit banks of the Guardian Detroit Union Group and the Detroit Bankers Company (First National); Lansing.

The president of Briggs Manufacturing — Ford's body supplier, whose strike had nearly shut Ford's Detroit works that winter — also sat on the Union Trust board. Forty million dollars was rushed from the Federal Reserve banks of New York and Chicago by midnight.

F. The UAW's rise and its civil war — 1935–1939

The UAW is born in Detroit — and its first president is a Baptist preacher — 26 August 1935 → 1939

  • Where: Detroit (founding convention); the Griswold Building, UAW headquarters.
  • What happened: The United Automobile Workers held its founding convention in Detroit on 26 August 1935 under an AFL charter, with 25,769 members from its constituent locals; the rank and file soon overturned the AFL's rule against electing their own officers. Martin had trained at a Baptist seminary in Kansas City and been a pastor at Goreville, Illinois, and in Kansas City before he went into the auto plants. After Flint (R07) the UAW claimed 150,000 members by mid-1937. Then it tore itself apart: in the summer of 1938 Martin had four vice-presidents — Richard Frankensteen, Wyndham Mortimer, Ed Hall and Walter Wells — tried at headquarters (26 July–6 August) on charges of Communist connections and graft, with labour lawyer Maurice Sugar defending them and accusing Martin's faction of taking orders from the ex-Communist Jay Lovestone; Frankensteen, Mortimer and Wells were expelled, and reinstated on 4 October. On 13 January 1939 some 3,000 members — a pro-Martin crowd followed by an anti-Martin one — occupied the Griswold Building, cut off the elevators and packed the stairs from the first floor to the eleventh; on 20 January the board impeached Martin, he "suspended" 15 of its 24 members, and on 23–24 January the CIO recognised R. J. Thomas instead. Martin called it the CIO's "declaration of war".

K. Deaths, hangings and the decade's scandals

The University of Islam and the truancy war — Detroit, 1934; Elijah's flight to Chicago, 1935 (deepens R05)

Detroit becomes an archdiocese — Edward Mooney, from Tokyo to Detroit — 31 May 1937

  • Where: the Cathedral of the Most Blessed Sacrament, Woodward Avenue; the chancery.
  • What happened: Coughlin's protector, Bishop Michael Gallagher, died in January 1937. Pope Pius XI raised Detroit to an archdiocese and on 31 May 1937 made Edward Mooney its first archbishop. Mooney had been the Holy See's Apostolic Delegate to the East Indies (1926–31) and to Japan (1931–33), then Bishop of Rochester (1933–37). In October 1937 he publicly rebuked Coughlin for calling Roosevelt "stupid" over the Hugo Black Supreme Court nomination, and Coughlin cancelled 26 broadcasts. In January 1939 Mooney proposed "labor schools in the parishes" to train Catholic workers to lead the unions. (In 1942 he silenced Coughlin; he died in Rome in the conclave of 1958.)
  • The odd detail: Detroit's first archbishop came to the radio priest's city straight from being the Pope's man in Japan.

Hamtramck — the Polish city inside Detroit — 1910–1940

  • Where: Hamtramck, a separate city wholly surrounded by Detroit; the Dodge Main plant; St. Florian Church.
  • What happened: A German farming village in 1901 with 3,559 people in 1910, Hamtramck exploded after the Dodge brothers built "Dodge Main" on its edge (ground broken 1910, production from 1911–14 by different sources) — to 48,615 in 1920 (a 1,266 per cent rise), 56,268 in 1930 and 49,839 in 1940. The Poles who came to work at Dodge pushed out the old German politicians; in 1922 the village incorporated as a city — to escape annexation by Detroit — with Peter C. Jezewski as first mayor. By the mid-1920s 78 per cent of residents owned or were buying their homes and 85 per cent of household heads worked in factories. Dodge Main grew to 67 acres and 35 buildings with its own doctors, fire brigade, "welfare department" and an after-hours machine shop the men called "the Playpen". St. Florian's Polish parish (1907) built Ralph Adams Cram's Gothic church in 1926–28 — dedicated 21 October 1928 before nearly 5,000, almost 200 feet tall, still the tallest building in town, seating 1,200, with an altar made in Italy; at its height the parish had seven priests and 3,000 schoolchildren.
  • The odd detail: A city that incorporated itself to stay out of Detroit, and ended up an island in it.
  • People: Peter C. Jezewski — first mayor, 1922; Ralph Adams Cram — St. Florian's architect.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamtramck,_Michigan ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St.Florian_Church(Hamtramck,_Michigan) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit/Hamtramck_Assembly ; https://detroithistorical.org/learn/encyclopedia-of-detroit/hamtramck
  • Confidence: High on the figures (the DHS gives 46,615 for 1920 against Wikipedia's 48,615). Hamtramck's 1920s corruption scandals and the 1935 housewives' meat boycott (Mary Zuk) are real leads but UNVERIFIED this session — see Leads.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in the setting Poland's fate after a German victory is different (no Versailles Poland; perhaps a German-sponsored Polish kingdom, as Berlin proclaimed in 1916). Hamtramck's Poles would read the news from home very differently: a homeland under German patronage, not a new republic.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The period's own social categories: to a 1930s Detroiter, Hamtramck was "Polish", Catholic and politically its own. A Polish city inside the city is a place where a Catholic faction (IHS) has a whole population to hide in — and where a German-patron faction has a claim on the homeland.

M. The river and the lakes around Detroit

Bob-Lo Island — the amusement park in Canadian waters — 1898 onward

The Blue Water Bridge — Port Huron to Sarnia — dedicated 8 October 1938

The Kerns Hotel fire — Lansing — 11 December 1934

O. Where Detroit's money lived and danced

The Book-Cadillac — the gangster on the top floor, and Gehrig on the staircase — 1924–1939

  • Where: Washington Boulevard at Michigan Avenue, downtown (the Book brothers' "Fifth Avenue of the West").
  • What happened: J. Burgess, Frank and Herbert Book hired Louis Kamper for a string of buildings on Washington Boulevard; the Book-Cadillac Hotel opened on 8 December 1924 as the tallest building in Detroit and the tallest hotel in the world, and their 38-storey Book Tower (1926) was briefly the city's tallest. A second Book Tower of 81 storeys — "designed to be the world's tallest building" — was cancelled in the Depression. The Purple Gang used the hotel, and Abe Bernstein kept a residence on its top floor until his death in 1968. On 2 May 1939, in the lobby, Lou Gehrig told Yankees manager Joe McCarthy to leave him out of that day's game against the Tigers, ending his 2,130-game streak; the hotel's history also has him collapsing on its grand staircase that day.
  • The odd detail: The Purples' leader lived above the city's grandest ballroom for four decades.

Gaukler Point — Edsel Ford's English village with a hidden darkroom — 1926–1930

  • Where: 1100 Lake Shore Drive, Grosse Pointe Shores, on Lake St. Clair.
  • What happened: Albert Kahn built Edsel and Eleanor Ford a house made to look like a cluster of Cotswold cottages; the family moved in in 1928 after two years of fitting antique panelling and fireplaces taken from English manor houses — the Gallery has sixteenth-century oak panelling and a chimneypiece from Wollaston Hall. Walter Dorwin Teague designed Art Deco rooms; the kitchen counters are sterling silver; a hidden darkroom sits behind a panel in Edsel's office. In 1930 a playhouse with working electricity and plumbing was built for daughter Josephine on the 87-acre grounds.
  • The odd detail: Rooms bought out of English houses and reassembled on a Michigan lakeshore — the son did with manors what the father did with Edison's laboratory.
  • People: Edsel Ford — Rivera's patron, Union Guardian director; Eleanor Clay Ford; Albert Kahn — architect.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edsel_and_Eleanor_Ford_House
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — in the setting a bankrupt Britain would be selling its manor-house interiors cheaply to American money, which makes the Fords' shopping more ordinary and more plentiful.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Rooms carry what happened in them. A Wollaston Hall chimneypiece in Grosse Pointe is a haunting imported by the ton — and a hidden darkroom is where the Ford heir kept pictures nobody else saw.
