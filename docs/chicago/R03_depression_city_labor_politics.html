R03 — Depression Chicago: money, politics, labor, protest, hunger, and the city's institutions breaking (1929–1939)

  • Lane: R03 — Depression Chicago: the money, the machine, the unpaid teachers, the tax strike, the bank runs, the evictions and hunger marches, the unions, the relief system, heat/dust/drought, and what things cost.
  • Date: 2026-09-26
  • Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent, parallel sweep)
  • Status: Complete — 53 entries, plus a Living memory list, Leads not chased, and a Top 10.

Overview

Depression Chicago went broke from the top down and fought back from the bottom up. The property-tax system collapsed in 1928–33 (a reassessment fiasco, then a 30,000-member taxpayers' strike), so the city and its school board paid their people in scrip and tax-anticipation warrants the Loop banks would not cash. The teachers — paid on schedule on only nine of 46 paydays in 1929–34 — occupied the Board, City Hall and, on 24 April 1933, five Loop banks, chanting for Charles Dawes, whose own bank had just been saved by a record $90 million federal loan (in the setting Dawes is a former President, which sharpens every scene he is in). Around that core: the June 1932 bank panic (40 Chicago banks failed that month), Insull's collapse and flight to Greece and the Ottoman shore, Binga's bank, both 1932 conventions at the Stadium, Cermak's death and the three-week acting mayor who could not sign warrants, the Kelly–Nash sewer machine, the eviction riot and the Unemployed Councils who switched the gas back on, the shelters (one in the old county jail) and the West Madison "slave market" where men lined up for work, the 1934 Stockyards fire, dust storms and record heat, and the CIO wars that climaxed in the Memorial Day Massacre (1937) and its suppressed newsreel. A final section runs the same story around the lakes: Michigan's bank holiday, Dearborn, Flint, Toledo, Minneapolis. Most entries hold on the domestic calendar; the ones that bend are those touching Dawes, Insull's Ottoman arrest, Communist organising (no Soviet Union in the setting), Taft's Chief Justiceship, and Roosevelt's "Quarantine Speech."

Part 1 — The unpaid teachers (the owner's "teachers' strike")

Short answer for the owner: it was not a walk-out strike in the modern sense. From 1929 to 1934 Chicago's school board could not meet its payroll; teachers were paid late, in part, in scrip, in tax-anticipation warrants the banks discounted, or not at all. In spring 1933 thousands of them stopped asking politely: they occupied the Board of Education and City Council meetings, then on 24 April 1933 some 5,000 of them swarmed five Loop banks, trashed lobbies, and chanted "We want Dawes!" [UNVERIFIED — V_R03: chant wording unconfirmed; the heckling and Dawes's "To hell with trouble makers" are primary-sourced] outside the bank of Charles G. Dawes — who in this setting is a former President. Their movement became the Chicago Teachers Union in 1937. The entries below split it into its pieces so a GM can use any one of them.

"Payless paydays" — the Chicago school board cannot pay its teachers — 1929–1934

  • Where: Chicago Board of Education, Builders Building, La Salle Street (board meetings); every public school in the city.
  • What happened: The finding aid of the Chicago Teachers' Federation records at the Chicago History Museum (as quoted in search results; page itself returned 403) describes the "payless paydays" of 1929–1934, "when teachers' salary checks were distributed on schedule on only nine of 46 paydays," and when "scrip or tax anticipation warrants were frequently tendered to teachers in lieu of cash." The Board began issuing scrip (IOUs) in 1931; many businesses refused it or took it only at a discount; the teachers' union won a court injunction against scrip, and the Board answered with outright payless paydays. Between January 1931 and May 1933 teachers received their monthly salary only three times. By April 1933 the city's roughly 14,000 teachers had "received only two weeks' salary in cash since last June"; the average teacher was owed six to eight months' back pay. The warrants were redeemable only when property owners paid their taxes — which, during the tax strike (see Part 2), they were not doing.
  • The odd detail: The money was not "gone" — it was locked up. Tax-anticipation warrants were a promissory note against taxes the city's richest property owners were refusing to pay, and the Loop banks, which held the city's paper and the owners' accounts, declined to buy more of it at face value. The teachers concluded, correctly, that the bankers and the tax strikers were the same people.
  • People: Margaret Haley — head of the Chicago Teachers' Federation (women elementary teachers, founded 1897), veteran of the 1900s tax fights; William J. Bogan — superintendent of schools.
  • Sources: https://chicagohistorytoday.wordpress.com/2017/03/22/irritable-teachers-3-22-1933/ ; https://www.shawlocal.com/thank-you/teachers/2025/05/14/teachers-struggled-through-great-depression/ ; https://theclio.com/entry/18174
  • Confidence: High for the overall shape; Medium for "nine of 46 paydays" (seen in the Chicago History Museum finding-aid text via search, page not loadable).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (municipal finance; the setting's different cause of the Crash doesn't change a Cook County property-tax collapse).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A teacher paid in scrip is a character who will do nearly anything for cash — the perfect cut-out for a cult or for Section 13 needing someone inside every school in the city. The scrip itself (numbered, printed by the city) is a paper trail.

The teachers take City Hall and the Board — 17–22 March 1933

  • Where: Board of Education meeting, Builders Building, La Salle Street; City Hall / City Council chamber.
  • What happened: On 17 March 1933 teachers learned that janitors had received secret raises while teacher pay was cut. On 21 March hundreds marched to the mayor's office (Chicago was between mayors — Cermak had been shot in Miami in February and died 6 March) and promised to come back every Tuesday. On 22 March more than 200 teachers descended on the Board of Education meeting carrying signs such as "Irritable Teachers Are Not Fair To Children," booed and heckled a board member who opposed pending legislation on school shutdowns, and were told by the presiding officer: "If you have no more control over your pupils than you have over yourselves, I question whether you are worthy of your calling." They then marched to City Hall chanting "We want our pay!"; Acting Mayor Frank Corr let three teachers address the Council, which agreed to try to raise cash through more tax-anticipation warrants.
  • The odd detail: The city had no elected mayor when the teachers rose — Cermak was dying of a bullet meant (or said to be meant) for Roosevelt, and an alderman, Frank J. Corr, was acting mayor for a matter of weeks.
  • People: Frank J. Corr — acting mayor of Chicago, March–April 1933; John M. Fewkes — gym teacher, leader of the Volunteer Emergency Committee.
  • Sources: https://chicagohistorytoday.wordpress.com/2017/03/22/irritable-teachers-3-22-1933/ ; https://www.dailykos.com/stories/2012/08/02/1116048/-The-Chicago-Teacher-Revolt-of-1933
  • Confidence: High (the Daily Kos piece is a secondary summary built on Lyons's Teachers and Reform; dates agree with the Chicago History Today entry).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A city with no mayor for six weeks is a city where whoever holds the Council's keys — or the Board's seal — can move things unseen. A GM can drop a crucial vote, a building permit, or a school closure into that gap.

The bank invasion — "We want Dawes!" — 24 April 1933 (and the Title & Trust battle, Wednesday 26 April ⟨fixed: V_R03⟩) (also in 09-22 archive: H05)

  • Where: The Loop, La Salle Street banking district. Named banks: First National Bank of Chicago, Harris Trust and Savings Bank, City National Bank and Trust Co. (Dawes's bank), Northern Trust, Continental Illinois; then the Chicago Title and Trust Company. Rally point: Grant Park. Continental Illinois stood at 231 South LaSalle Street (renamed Continental Illinois National Bank & Trust in 1932); the First National had absorbed the failed Foreman State Banks in 1931 (Wikipedia, First Chicago Bank). Other street addresses UNVERIFIED this session.
  • What happened: On 24 April 1933, the first day of spring break, about 5,000 teachers organised by John Fewkes's Volunteer Emergency Committee met in Grant Park, split into five columns, and marched on five of the city's largest banks — the banks that had refused to buy the school board's tax warrants. They wore arm bands showing how many months they were behind in pay [UNVERIFIED — V_R03]. Inside they chanted "Pay us! Pay us!", turned over desks, broke windows and threw ink on the walls, and some occupied the banks until police removed them. At City National Bank a crowd chanted "We want Dawes! We want Dawes!" [UNVERIFIED — V_R03: the chant wording is only in AP copy on newspapers.com; the UP, INS and Daily Worker accounts read this session do not contain it] — for Charles G. Dawes, the bank's chairman, who had just had his previous bank rescued with a $90 million federal loan (see Part 3). What the same-day United Press wire (Waterbury Evening Democrat, 24 Apr 1933, p.1; Sam Knott's UP story, Times-News, 25 Apr) and the Daily Worker (25 Apr, p.3) record: the teachers, demanding the banks advance "$29,000,000 owed them in back salaries," converged on City National, Continental-Illinois, Harris Trust and First National; "the first three closed" temporarily, First National staying open after Melvin Traylor agreed to meet a delegation. City National officials "at first denied that Dawes was in the city"; when the clamor grew, "Dawes appeared outside the bank. Boos and hisses greeted him." Taunts: "There's the man who borrowed $90,000,000 from the government for his own bank but won't lend any to the teachers!" and "He went along with Samuel Insull in cheating us." He raised a hand for silence (INS photo, Washington Times, 26 Apr), urged the crowd to get "behind our Mayor Kelly" ("Kelly isn't our Mayor. We didn't elect him," they shouted back), told a heckler whose colleague's electricity had been cut off "You'd be a damn sight better off if you had your electricity turned off," and finally boomed "To hell with trouble makers." Inside, teachers "climbed over marble topped desks, upset wastebaskets," vowed to sit "all night if necessary," and four women started a bridge game on an overturned desk; placards read "A Century of Progress — the biggest joke yet" and "We want food." ⟨fixed: V_R03⟩ Police Commissioner James Allman had 200 policemen stationed through the financial district; police, many of whom had themselves missed paydays, were reluctant to act [UNVERIFIED — V_R03: the wire says only that it was feared the 200 police "would be obliged to use their clubs"]. On Wednesday 26 April ⟨fixed: V_R03 — Lyons: "the following Wednesday"; Daily Worker dateline "CHICAGO, April 26"; the Daily Worker counts "more than 1000"⟩ more than three thousand male teachers "armed with schoolbooks" returned, invaded the Chicago Title and Trust Company (which held some $10 million of property taxes in escrow [UNVERIFIED — V_R03]), wrecked the lobby and fought mounted police who clubbed them; the protesters threw lobby furnishings back.
  • The odd detail: A contemporary account in The Nation (Aug 1933) credited the teachers for "refusing to be distracted" by Dawes's "picturesque profanity" — the former Vice President (in this setting, former President) swore at a crowd of schoolteachers in his own lobby. The invasion produced the payment of about $12 million owed for the last months of 1932.

Fewkes — 32-year-old gym teacher (the 09-22 archive places him at Tilden Technical High School), VEC leader; William A. A teacher's suicide on the day of the second raid — Paul Schneider — 27 April 1933

  • Where: Chicago (he taught at Washburne, the city's trade/manual-arts school).
  • What happened: The Nation reported that on 27 April 1933 Paul Schneider, a 44-year-old Chicago school teacher who had "not been paid for eight months," killed himself, leaving a widow with three children, unpaid grocery bills and a house losing value. The same account gave the toll among Chicago teachers: 400 in "asylums and sanitariums as a result of the strain"; and, from a national NEA questionnaire (William G. Carr, NEA Research Division — not Chicago-specific ⟨fixed: V_R03⟩), 2,278 teachers in 263 schools who had lapsed on $7,800,567 of life insurance and 759 who could not meet payments on their homes; more than 500 on charity [UNVERIFIED — V_R03: not in the article; its nearest figure is 954 unemployed eligible-list teachers supported by the Unemployment Relief Committee]; many in debt to loan sharks. The Daily Kos summary (not ISR) names Schneider as a Washburne manual-arts teacher.
  • The odd detail: The date — the day after three thousand of his colleagues fought mounted police in the Title & Trust lobby (Wednesday 26 April) ⟨fixed: V_R03⟩.
  • People: Paul Schneider — 44, manual-arts teacher, Washburne.

Grant Park rallies and the last day of school — 13 May and 9 June 1933 (also in 09-22 archive: H05, which stops at 13 May)

  • Where: Grant Park; the Loop.
  • What happened: On 13 May 1933 a mass rally in Grant Park — Clio gives 15,000 teachers — heard that four months of the nine owed would be paid. On 9 June, the last day of the school year, 5,000 protested in the Loop; the Shaw Local summary records another police confrontation in the Loop "four weeks later" than 13 May — i.e. the 9–10 June clash, not late June ⟨fixed: V_R03⟩. Earlier that spring elementary teachers staged a one-day sick-out, and high-school teachers another with a student sympathy strike; teachers also organised a boycott of businesses named as tax delinquents. Teachers themselves raised about $112,000 for poor children's clothing and breakfasts.
  • The odd detail: Unpaid teachers were feeding their pupils: one teacher, according to the Shaw summary, lived on "graham crackers and milk," and teachers bought stockings and shoes for children who came to class without them.
  • People: John Fewkes — VEC; Harry Tate — Chicago Teacher Voter Association (named by Daily Kos).

"Fads and frills" — the July 1933 school cuts — 12 July 1933 (protest mass meeting reported 31 July)

  • Where: Board of Education; mass protest at a "West Side Stadium" (per Time; likely the Chicago Stadium, 1800 W. Madison — UNVERIFIED).
  • What happened: On 12 July 1933 the Board, acting on the advice of the business-led Citizens Committee on Public Expenditures, approved a $4,000,000 cut of what it called "fads and frills": junior high schools, kindergartens, physical-education teachers and more; Crane Junior College was closed (it reopened in less than a year as Herzl Junior College). A board member, Helen Hefferan, was reportedly shut out of the meeting, and Superintendent Bogan said he had been blindsided. Time (31 July 1933) reported 20,000 parents and teachers at a mass meeting organised by a "Save Our Schools" committee claiming 40 civic organisations; Bogan appeared and said, "I am living in terror of the effect of the economies on the public schools." University of Chicago dean Charles Hubbard Judd argued the real 1933 deficit was $1,162,940, not the Board's $10,000,000. The US Commissioner of Education called the cuts "a return to the dark ages." The Board rescinded the worst cuts that October; by September 1935 all dismissed teachers had been reassigned.
  • The odd detail: The business committee that pushed the cuts admitted it had "taken charge" of city finances — the schools were being run, in effect, by an unelected committee of the Loop's richest men.
  • People: William J. Bogan — superintendent (died 1936); Charles H. Judd — U. of Chicago education dean; Helen Hefferan — board member; Edward J. Kelly — the new mayor (appointed April 1933).
  • Sources: https://time.com/archive/6753101/education-defrilled-chicago-2/ ; https://www.dailykos.com/stories/2012/08/02/1116048/-The-Chicago-Teacher-Revolt-of-1933 ; https://isreview.org/issue/86/peoples-history-chicago-teachers-union/
  • Confidence: High for the $4M/"fads and frills"/Judd/Bogan quote (Time); Medium for the 12 July date, Hefferan, and October rescission (Daily Kos synthesis). Crane → Herzl from a Wikipedia search snippet (Malcolm X College article), not opened. Number of teachers dismissed: not confirmed (a "1,400" figure from memory was NOT found — dropped).

  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A closed junior college and a dozen shuttered junior-high buildings in 1933–34 are empty public buildings with keys held by the Board — ideal for a cult's meeting room, or for a Kabbalah contracted to clean one out.

The Chicago Teachers Union is born — 1937

  • Where: Chicago Civic Opera House (founding meeting), 20 N. Wacker Drive.
  • What happened: The unrest of the early 1930s finally united Chicago's divided teacher unions. In 1937 the Men's Teachers Union, the Federation of Women High School Teachers, the Elementary Teachers Union and the Playground Teachers Union merged at a meeting at the Civic Opera House; on 28 October 1937 their four AFT charters were surrendered and the new Chicago Teachers Union received a single charter as AFT Local 1. About 3,500 teachers joined at once; by September 1938 it had over 8,500 and was the largest teachers' union in the country. John Fewkes, the VEC leader, became its first president (and later president of the AFT; Time called him "the John L. Lewis of the teaching profession" [UNVERIFIED — V_R03]). Margaret Haley's Chicago Teachers' Federation — the old women's elementary-teacher organisation — stayed out for years, fearing the CTU would be run by and for men and high-school teachers.
  • The odd detail: The union that grew out of a bank riot was chartered at Samuel Insull's opera house — the building Insull put up in 1929 just before his empire fell (see Part 3).
  • People: John M. Fewkes — first CTU president; Margaret Haley — CTF leader, held out of the merger.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Teachers_Union ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Teachers'_Federation ; https://isreview.org/issue/86/peoples-history-chicago-teachers-union/
  • Confidence: High for 1937, Local 1, Fewkes. Conflicts: ISR's "eight teachers' organizations, including the CTF, the MTU, the ETU, and the FWHST" coalesced into the earlier Teacher Welfare Organizations coalition, not the 1937 CTU itself, which Wikipedia gives as four unions ⟨fixed: V_R03⟩; Wikipedia's two articles disagree on whether 8,500 members was reached Sept 1937 or Sept 1938. The 28 Oct 1937 charter date came from a search-result summary [UNVERIFIED — V_R03: not on Wikipedia or the CTU's own history page].

Schools closed by polio, taught by radio — September 1937

  • Where: Chicago public schools; radio stations WCFL, WENR, WGN, WIND, WJJD, WLS.
  • What happened: A polio outbreak led Chicago Board of Health president Herman Bundesen to postpone indefinitely the school opening set for 7 September 1937. For two weeks from 13 September, grades 3–8 were taught by radio in 15-minute slots from 7:15 a.m. on six donated stations (science and social studies Mon/Wed/Fri, English and arithmetic Tue/Thu/Sat), with lessons, questions and assignments printed in six newspapers and a help line of sixteen teachers for parents. Superintendent William H. Johnson estimated 315,000 pupils listened. High schools reopened 16 September, elementary schools 27 September. Rochester, NY and Cleveland copied the scheme; the Board later formed the Chicago Radio Council and founded WBEZ in 1943.
  • The odd detail: The idea came from George H. Biggar of WLS — the farm-and-barn-dance station — and every morning the whole city's children sat by the wireless listening to the same teacher's voice at the same minute.
  • People: Herman N. Bundesen — Board of Health president; William H. Johnson — superintendent; Minnie Fallon — assistant superintendent; Lillian M. Tobin — curriculum; August H. Pritzlaff — announcer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance_education_in_Chicago_Public_Schools_in_1937
  • Confidence: High (Wikipedia article resting on Sept 1937 Chicago Tribune and NYT reports). Case/death counts for Chicago not given there.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): 315,000 children hearing one broadcast voice each morning, at a fixed minute, for two weeks — a summoner's dream of a mass audience. What if one lesson was not written by Miss Tobin?

Part 2 — Banks, bailouts and the June 1932 panic

The June 1932 Chicago bank panic — 20–27 June 1932

  • Where: The Loop; neighbourhood banks citywide; the main Post Office (postal-savings windows).
  • What happened: Of 49 bank failures in Illinois in June 1932, 40 were in Chicago, and 26 of those failed in the single week 20–27 June (Commercial and Financial Chronicle, 2 July 1932, cited by Calomiris & Mason). Withdrawals from the downtown banks began 22 June and peaked Friday 24 June; people "from all parts of the city seemed to converge on the Loop in hysterical fear and anxiety" (F. Cyril James, 1938). First National paid out $50 million in days and still had $125 million in cash. The only Loop bank to fail was the Chicago Bank of Commerce. Depositors carried their cash straight to federal postal savings: the Post Office raised its savings windows from the usual 25 or 30 to about 100 and took $2–3 million a day at the peak against a normal $200,000. Triggers included the Insull collapse, a bank-fraud scandal, and Congress's refusal on 22 June to lend the bankrupt city $80 million.
  • The odd detail: The safest vault in Chicago that week was the Post Office — the crowd lined up at federal windows to hand over the cash they had just pulled from the banks.
  • People: Melvin A. Traylor — president, First National Bank of Chicago, credited with stopping runs on his and other banks; he drew about 40 votes on each of the first three ballots at the Democratic convention in Chicago that same week and died of pneumonia 14 Feb 1934.
  • Sources: https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/aer/aer_1997_calomiris_contagion_bank_failures.pdf (Calomiris & Mason, AER Dec 1997, read in full text) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Alvah_Traylor ; https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/traylor-melvin-alvah
  • Confidence: High (peer-reviewed economic history quoting contemporary sources). A story that Traylor stood on a pillar/marble counter and talked a crowd down is folklore-grade — UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar; the setting's colonial-speculation crash would just give the depositors different reasons to fear (colonial bonds, German-sanctioned exporters).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Runs are crowds that move on rumour. A cult that can seed one rumour on 22 June can empty a specific bank by Friday — and a bank that closes "voluntarily" can lose its safe-deposit records in the confusion.

The Bain chain collapse — 9 June 1932

  • Where: Chicago neighbourhood banks (South Ashland and other outlying branches of the Bain chain).
  • What happened: John Bain, a real-estate developer who owned a chain of banks, became the defendant in the city's largest bank-fraud case. On 9 June 1932 all 12 banks in his chain failed to open. The "Dawes loan" — Central Republic Bank rescued by the RFC — 26–27 June 1932

  • Where: Central Republic Bank & Trust Co., the Loop; RFC, Washington (by open telephone line).
  • What happened: Charles G. Dawes resigned as president of the new Reconstruction Finance Corporation on 6 June 1932 to go back to his Chicago bank, the Central Republic Bank & Trust Co. On Sunday 26 June its directors met on an emergency that "threatened all Loop banks." Dawes's management was preparing to close the bank voluntarily; the other Chicago bankers, fearing collapse of the whole Loop, persuaded him to stay open. At about 3 a.m., with RFC officials in Washington on an open telephone wire, a $90 million "Dawes loan" was arranged (Calomiris & Mason: the plan was $80 million RFC plus $10 million from banks, with the RFC accepting the city's tax warrants — $30 million of which the Loop banks held — as collateral). It was the largest loan in the RFC's history [UNVERIFIED — V_R03: not stated by Time, Calomiris & Mason, or the RFC pages read], and cries of favouritism followed. Central Republic still withdrew from banking in October 1932 and was officially closed two years later; Dawes started a new bank, City National Bank & Trust Co., and chaired it until his death. The RFC recovered about $45 million, then in November 1934 sued some 5,000 stockholders for $14 million under double liability — and won.
  • The odd detail: The city's unpaid tax warrants — the same paper the teachers were being handed instead of salaries — were accepted as collateral to save a banker's bank.
  • People: Charles Gates Dawes — chairman, Central Republic; ex-RFC president; (in the setting) former President; lived at 225 Greenwood St., Evanston, 1909–1951.

Part 3 — The city goes broke: the tax strike, the machine, the conventions, Insull

The taxpayers' strike — the Association of Real Estate Taxpayers — 1930–1933

  • Where: Chicago and Cook County; ARET ran its own radio programme.
  • What happened: Property assessment in Cook County collapsed in chaos at the end of the 1920s, and in 1930 a group of wealthy real-estate owners founded the Association of Real Estate Taxpayers. Its leaders, John M. Pratt (executive director; a former Hearst advertising manager and co-founder of the North American Newspaper Alliance) and James E. Bistor (president), argued that the county's failure to assess personal property — furniture, cars, stocks and bonds — broke the Illinois Constitution of 1870's rule of uniform taxation. By early 1931 ARET was telling its members to withhold their real-estate taxes until the courts ruled. It worked as a cooperative law firm: $15 a year in dues bought a place among 26,000 co-litigants; its tax evidence filled "a 7,000-page, two-foot-thick book." By late 1932 it had nearly 30,000 members — mostly skilled workers and small-business owners — and a budget over $600,000. Mayor Cermak tried to break the strike by threatening criminal prosecution and cutting off city services. ARET took "a demoralizing blow" when the U.S. Supreme Court refused its case in October 1932 and collapsed in early 1933 amid coercion, legal defeats and factional fights.
  • The odd detail: The strike's members were not the idle rich but homeowners and shopkeepers — and the taxes they withheld were exactly the money that should have paid the teachers (Part 1). One side of the city's collapse was a grassroots revolt; the other side was unpaid schoolteachers. Both blamed the banks.
  • People: John M. Pratt (1886–1954) — ARET executive director, newspaperman; James E. Bistor — ARET president from 1930; Anton Cermak — mayor, strike-breaker.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_Real_Estate_Taxpayers ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_M._Pratt (both rest on David T. Beito, Taxpayers in Revolt, 1989)
  • Confidence: High for the outline and numbers (single scholarly source behind both articles). How much tax was withheld in total: not found.

  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A radio programme with 30,000 loyal listeners told to disobey the law on the say-so of one man — a recruiting pool for the Golden Circle's respectable wing, or a front a German-funded outfit would love to buy.

Congress says no — the city begs Washington — June 1932

  • Where: Washington, D.C.; Chicago City Hall.
  • What happened: In June 1932 a delegation of Chicago city officials and bankers asked Congress for $80 million in aid for the city; Congress turned them down on 22 June, two days before the bank panic peaked (Part 2). In May 1932 the Illinois Emergency Relief Commission announced office closings; the RFC lent $6.3 million to keep relief going, then $6 million in August and $5 million in September; the Emergency Relief and Construction Act of July 1932 created $300 million in loans to states.

Cermak's machine and Cermak's death — April 1931 to 6 March 1933 (also in 09-22 archive: R05, which covers only a Czech memorial)

  • Where: City Hall; Bayfront Park, Miami; Jackson Memorial Hospital, Miami; funeral at Chicago Stadium (1800 W. Madison); grave at Bohemian National Cemetery, Chicago.
  • What happened: Anton Cermak, a Bohemian-born Democrat, beat "Big Bill" Thompson in April 1931 with 58% of the vote, building his win on the Czechs, Poles and other immigrant groups — Thompson had mocked his name, and Cermak answered: "it's true I didn't come over on the Mayflower, but I came over as soon as I could." He made Patrick Nash county party chairman in 1931. On 15 February 1933, at Bayfront Park in Miami, Giuseppe Zangara fired at President-elect Roosevelt; Cermak, standing near him, was shot in the lung and died at Jackson Memorial Hospital on 6 March 1933. His funeral procession ran five miles with about 50,000 marchers; the service was held in the Chicago Stadium. 22nd Street was renamed Cermak Road.
  • The odd detail: He took nineteen days to die, and his own physician attributed the death to complications of ulcerative colitis rather than the bullet (Wikipedia, March 1933). Also wounded in Miami: two women, a man, and Roosevelt's bodyguard, policeman William Sinnott. Zangara, 32, went to the electric chair at Raiford, Florida, on 20 March 1933 — two weeks after Cermak died. The famous line "I'm glad it was me instead of you" was printed by the Chicago Tribune without attribution, and most scholars doubt he said it. The rumour that Zangara was really an Outfit hit on Cermak — revenge for the mayor's police shooting of Frank Nitti in December 1932 — was pushed by author Ronald Humble; Roosevelt biographer Jean Edward Smith found "no proof."
  • People: Anton J. Cermak — mayor 1931–33; Giuseppe Zangara — gunman, executed 20 March 1933; William Sinnott — Roosevelt's police bodyguard, wounded; Frank Nitti — Outfit leader.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Cermak ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Stadium ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Nash ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_1933 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_1933

Frank Corr — the mayor who could not sign a paycheck — 15 March to 8 April 1933

The Kelly–Nash machine — April 1933 onward

  • Where: City Hall; the Sanitary District; Nash Brothers (sewer contractors).
  • What happened: Edward J. Kelly, chief engineer of the Chicago Sanitary District through the 1920s, was hand-picked by his friend Patrick Nash and took office as mayor on 17 April 1933 (Wikipedia; the Corr article gives Corr's last day as 8 April). Nash and his brother Richard ran Nash Brothers, a sewer-building firm that earned more than $14 million from the Sanitary District — the district Kelly had engineered; in 1925 its city contracts gave Nash one of the ten highest incomes in Chicago, and he kept racehorses at Shannon Farm and on a Kentucky farm. Nash had turned down the mayoralty himself. Together they built the "Kelly–Nash Machine," one of the most powerful and most corrupt city organisations in the country — more inclusive than its predecessors, spreading jobs across European ethnic groups and drawing Black Chicagoans into the Democratic fold. Kelly was mayor through the Century of Progress, the teacher cuts, the Memorial Day Massacre, and on to 1947. Nash died 6 October 1943.
  • The odd detail: The two men who ran Depression Chicago made their fortunes underground — in the sewers.
  • People: Edward J. Kelly — mayor 1933–47; Patrick A. Nash (1863–1943) — sewer contractor, county chairman 1931–43.

The Republican convention at Chicago Stadium — 14–16 June 1932

The Democratic convention — Roosevelt flies in — 27 June–2 July 1932

  • Where: Chicago Stadium, 1800 W. Madison Street.
  • What happened: The Democrats opened on 27 June 1932 — the very day the RFC's Dawes loan was agreed and the Loop panic broke. Samuel Insull: the fall, the flight, the ship, the trial — 1932–1938

  • Where: Chicago (Civic Opera House, 20 N. Wacker — address from general knowledge, UNVERIFIED this session); Paris; Italy; Athens; Istanbul; Smyrna; Chicago federal court.

The Diana Gross eviction riot — 3 August 1931 (also in 09-22 archive: H04)

  • Where: 5016 S. Dearborn Street (per the Daily Worker; Wikipedia says "near 51st street and Dearborn"), South Side Black Belt; Washington Park.

The shelters: "the Intake" at 758 West Harrison and the breadline at 116 Green Street — 1930–1935

  • Where: 758 W. Harrison St. (the Intake, where men applied); shelters at 116 Green St. (breadline), 222 Morgan St., the Hobbie Street shelter (where drunks were sent), and 54 West Austin (the old Criminal Court building and jail — the same address number as today's Courthouse Place, 54 W. Hubbard; the Austin → Hubbard street renaming is UNVERIFIED this session).
  • What happened: As the Depression hit, men slept in the parks, under Wacker Drive and in hallways; some insurance companies refused to insure plate-glass windows or raised rates for fear of the unemployed. Governor Emmerson appointed a Commission on Unemployment and Relief in October 1930, which financed the Salvation Army, the Christian Industrial League, the Urban League and the city's Department of Public Welfare by the number of beds and meals each supplied. A Mrs. Collins got the county's old criminal-court building and jail at 54 West Austin and ran it as a ten-cent flophouse. By March 1934 about 20,000 men lived in the Chicago shelters. Rumours ran through the shelters: "A hundred men were poisoned at 116 Green Street yesterday and the administration is trying to hush it up"; "Some of the windows fell out of 54 West Austin."

Part 6 — The labour wars, 1936–1939

The Memorial Day Massacre — Republic Steel, South Chicago — 30 May 1937

  • Where: Sam's Place, a former tavern and dance hall at 113th Street and Green Bay Avenue (SWOC headquarters), then across the open prairie toward the Republic Steel mill, South Chicago.
  • What happened: Four days into the "Little Steel" strike (called 26 May 1937 against Republic, Youngstown Sheet & Tube and Inland Steel; 67,000 out within days), a holiday rally of 1,500–2,500 strikers, families, sympathisers and passers-by at Sam's Place marched across the prairie toward the Republic plant to set up a picket line. Ten demonstrators died — four on the spot, six of their wounds: Hilding Anderson, Alfred Causey, Leo Francisco, Earl Handley, Otis Jones, Sam Popovich, Kenneth Reed, Joseph Rothmund, Anthony Tagliori and Lee Tisdale. A coroner's jury ruled the killings "justifiable homicide," and no officer was prosecuted. Part 8 — The Great Lakes ring: money and labour beyond Chicago

Michigan closes its banks — the domino that toppled the country — 14 February–6 March 1933

  • Where: Detroit and all Michigan; then Illinois (withdrawals limited 2 March) and the nation.
  • What happened: With two of Detroit's largest banks about to fail, Michigan Governor William Comstock closed every bank in the state for eight days on 14 February 1933 — the first large industrial state to do so. Other governors followed; Illinois and Ohio limited withdrawals on 2 March, and by 4 March 37 of the 48 states had closed their banks or limited withdrawals. Roosevelt's national bank holiday began at 1 a.m. on Monday 6 March 1933; Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act on 9 March; Federal Reserve city banks reopened 13 March, and by 15 March banks with 90% of the country's resources were open again.
  • The odd detail: Cermak was shot the day after Michigan shut its banks — Chicago's crisis of spring 1933 (a dying mayor, a closed banking system, unpaid teachers, an acting mayor who could not sign warrants) all landed in the same three weeks.
  • People: William Comstock — Michigan governor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_1933 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_1933 ; https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/bank-holiday-of-1933
  • Confidence: High. The Ford/Union Guardian Trust quarrel behind the Detroit failure was NOT confirmed this session.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the setting's crash cause changes why the banks were weak, not the dates).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Eight days when no bank in Michigan opened: safe-deposit boxes unreachable, cash only, and anyone who needed to move something valuable across the state line had to carry it by hand.

The Ford Hunger March — Dearborn — 7 March 1932

  • Where: From Detroit down Miller Road to the gates of Ford's River Rouge plant, Dearborn, Michigan; funeral to Woodmere Cemetery.
  • What happened: Between 3,000 and 5,000 unemployed auto workers, organised by the Unemployed Councils and the Auto, Aircraft and Vehicle Workers, marched about a mile to the Rouge plant with fourteen demands (rehiring, medical care, winter fuel, an end to racial discrimination in hiring and to company spies).   • People: Albert Goetz — Detroit Unemployed Council; John Schmies — Communist mayoral candidate; Harry Bennett — Ford security chief (named elsewhere; role that day per this article: "Ford security officers").

The Battle of the Overpass — Dearborn — 26 May 1937

  • Where: Pedestrian overpass over Miller Road, Gate 4, River Rouge plant, Dearborn.
  • What happened: UAW organisers came to hand out leaflets at shift change and posed for Detroit News photographer James R. The Flint sit-down — 30 December 1936–11 February 1937

  • Where: General Motors' Fisher Body No. 1 and No. 2 and Chevrolet No. 4, Flint, Michigan.
  • What happened: For 44 days UAW members occupied the Fisher Body plants that made the dies for GM's car bodies. On 11 January 1937 police tried to retake Fisher Body 2; strikers drove them off with hinges, bottles and bolts — 14 were wounded by gunfire — and named it "the Battle of the Running Bulls." Genora Johnson (later Dollinger) organised the all-female Emergency Brigade. Governor Frank Murphy refused to use force against the strikers and sent the National Guard to protect them from police and company guards. GM recognised the UAW; its membership grew from 30,000 to 500,000 within a year.
  • The odd detail: A governor who deployed the Guard to protect strikers — the opposite of Toledo 1934 and Chicago 1937.
  • People: Genora Johnson Dollinger — Emergency Brigade; Frank Murphy — Michigan governor.

  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.

The Battle of Toledo — Auto-Lite — 12 April–3 June 1934 (riot 23–24 May)

  • Where: Electric Auto-Lite plant, Toledo, Ohio.
  • What happened: A strike begun 12 April 1934 was kept alive by the Lucas County Unemployed League (Ted Selander, Sam Pollock), an arm of A. J. Muste's American Workers Party, whose Louis Budenz told them to defy Judge Roy Stuart's injunction limiting pickets to 25 a gate. On 23 May nearly 10,000 people fought sheriff's deputies with stones, bricks and bottles; on 24 May the Ohio National Guard (eight rifle companies, three machine-gun companies) arrived, fought 6,000 people, and fired into the crowd, killing Frank Hubay (27) and Steve Cyigon (20) — neither an Auto-Lite worker. The union won recognition, a 5% raise and a 35-cent minimum on 2–3 June.
  • The odd detail: The strike was won by the unemployed — men with no jobs at the plant picketing for those who had them.
  • People: A. J. Muste — Dutch-born minister, American Workers Party; Louis F. Budenz — its executive secretary; Frank Hubay, Steve Cyigon — killed.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auto-Lite_strike ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/April_1934 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_1934
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS (lightly) — Muste's American Workers Party (founded Dec 1933) was an independent Marxist party seeking "an American approach" and was not Trotskyist during the strike; it merged with the Trotskyist Communist League of America only in December 1934 ⟨fixed: V_R03⟩. Minneapolis Teamsters and "Bloody Friday" — 16 May–21 August 1934

  • Where: Minneapolis market district (central market); the city under martial law.
  • What happened: Teamsters Local 574, led by Farrell Dobbs, the Dunne brothers and Carl Skoglund, fought the employers' Citizens Alliance. Part 9 — Water, concrete, and price tags

The lake, the sewers, and the Supreme Court — 1929–1930s

  • Where: The Sanitary District of Chicago (created 29 May 1889); the Chicago Sanitary and Ship Canal; the sewage-treatment works at Stickney (today the largest water-reclamation plant in the U.S., 1.44 billion gallons a day).
  • What happened: Chicago flushed its sewage away from the lake by reversing the river and pulling Lake Michigan water down the Sanitary and Ship Canal. Wisconsin and the other lake states sued, saying the diversion was lowering the lakes and hurting their harbours. On 14 January 1929, in Wisconsin v. Illinois, a unanimous Supreme Court held that federal equity could force a state to take positive action to stop harming others, and a special master set limits on the diversion — the decree that pushed the Sanitary District into its great treatment-plant building programme of the Depression years. The District had committed itself to building treatment plants by a 1919 ordinance and finished its first plant in 1922; Edward Kelly was its chief engineer in the 1920s, and Pat Nash's firm earned $14 million from it (see Kelly–Nash).
  • The odd detail: The city's great 1930s political machine grew out of the one agency that controlled where Chicago's filth went — and a federal court was watching how much lake water it drank.
  • People: William Howard Taft — wrote the 1929 opinion; Edward J. Kelly — District chief engineer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin_v._Illinois ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stickney_Water_Reclamation_Plant ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Joseph_Kelly
  • Confidence: Medium-High (flow limits, dates of the 1930 decree and 1930s plant openings NOT confirmed this session).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Taft was Chief Justice only because President Harding appointed him in 1921; in the setting Harding never became President, so the 1929 Court's Chief Justice is whoever President Leonard Wood chose. The lawsuit and the diversion hold.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The owner asked for "loss of water": a court decree that cuts how much of the lake the city may take, a machine that owns the sewers, and new treatment plants rising on the prairie at Stickney — the underground is where the setting's summoners would build.

The New Deal pours concrete — Lake Shore Drive north, Midway, the WPA — 1933–1939

  • Where: Lake Shore Drive from Belmont (3200 N) to Foster (5200 N); the Outer Drive Bridge; Chicago Municipal Airport (Midway).

The southern lakefront road was then named Leif Ericson Drive (1927; renamed Lake Shore Drive in 1946). The Outer Drive Bridge opened in 1937 (see Part 6), its approach crossing the Illinois Central yards and turning sharply onto the bridge. The Works Progress Administration, created 6 May 1935, peaked nationally in November 1938 with 3,334,594 workers at about $52.50 a month; among its signature airport jobs was Chicago's Municipal Airport (Midway), 1935–39.
  • The odd detail: The new lakefront drive was built on land that did not exist a few years before — the city's Depression work relief literally made new ground in the lake.

What a gun and a car cost — 1921–1934

  • Where: National prices, relevant to Chicago.
  • What happened: The Thompson submachine gun sold for $200 in 1921; in 1926 the plain M1921 was cut to $175 (the Cutts-compensator model stayed $200); by 1928 the list price was $225, with 50-round drums at $5 and 20-round magazines at $3. Hardware stores stocked them. After the National Firearms Act of 1934 (already in the setting) every transfer carried a $200 tax — as much as the gun. The 1932 Ford ran from $490 (coupé) and $495 (roadster) to $650 (convertible sedan), and the new V8 cost only $10 more than the four-cylinder Model B; Clyde Barrow favoured stolen Ford V8s and died in a 1934 V8 sedan on 23 May 1934.
  • The odd detail: In 1932 a gangster's Tommy gun ($225) cost about half as much as a new Ford ($490) — and the NFA's $200 tax made the paperwork as expensive as the weapon.
