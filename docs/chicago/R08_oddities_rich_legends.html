Overview

This lane is the strange furniture of 1930s Chicago: the rich and their scandals (the McCormick–Rockefeller circle — Jung's patron who claimed a pharaoh's marriage, her husband's gland operation and his diva, the daughters who married old men, the son who married his roommate's mother; Insull's throne and subway-stair death; the Colonel's mutilated flag; the Palmer castle sold to an inventor), the museums that opened almost all at once (Adler 1930, Shedd 1930, Field's Hall of Man 1933, MSI's coal mine 1933, Brookfield's barless zoo 1934 and its 1937 panda, the first blood bank 1937), the hidden city (60 miles of freight tunnel under the Loop, Lower Wacker, the Couch tomb on the old cemetery, Holmes's castle standing until 1938, Dunning's 38,000, Manteno's typhoid), the haunted road (Resurrection Mary and the twice-burned ballroom, Bachelor's Grove, Sag Bridge, the Congress Hotel, Death Alley, Showmen's Rest, the Confederate Mound with the Golden Circle's 1864 plot under it, the whale in the Bahá'í temple), the fringe (Bughouse Square, the Dil Pickle's last winter, Reitman's Hobo College, Riverview, the Green Mill's trapdoor), the real rooms where the rich partied (Pump Room 1938, the Drake and the Edgewater Beach, the Empire Room and the bankrupt Stevens, Chez Paree, the Aragon), and trivia with teeth (the Lindbergh Beacon, the Eastland's hulk sinking a U-boat, Chinatown's Norwegian-built temple, Yellow Kid Weil, Bathhouse John, Cap Streeter's "District", Dick Tracy's birth, Bubbly Creek, 1936's cold-and-heat, and — for the setting — the ex-president Dawes living in Evanston). The largest divergence finds: Dawes's real Nobel is for German reparations that run the other way in the setting; the U-boat on the lake bottom, Cantigny, the 1939 Langer suicide (no Nazis), the blood bank's Russian sources and the Adler's German projector all need re-grounding. The side task (which YouTuber) is answered at the top: most likely Adam Selzer's walking "virtual tours"/daily streams; alternatively Dilla Thomas's You Don't Know Chi.

2. The rich and their scandals

Edith Rockefeller McCormick — Jung's patron, "Tutankhamun's first wife", dead at the Drake — 1913–1932 (also in 09-22 archive: W10, W11)

  • Where: 1000 N. Lake Shore Drive (her mansion); Villa Turicum, Lake Forest; died in Chicago (the Drake Hotel per her biographers — UNVERIFIED this session, Wikipedia says only "Chicago")
  • What happened: John D. Rockefeller's daughter, wife (1895–1921) of reaper heir Harold F. McCormick. In 1913 she went to Zürich for depression, was analysed by C. G. Jung, stayed eight years, became a lay Jungian analyst with 50-plus patients, paid to have Jung translated into English and bankrolled the Zürich Psychological Club. She paid James Joyce a monthly stipend Feb 1918–Oct 1919 while he wrote Ulysses, then cut him off. Divorced December 1921 (the hearing took fifty minutes). Back in Chicago she ran a rigid salon, sponsored the Woman's World's Fairs (1925–28), and in February 1923 told the press she was the reincarnation of Tutankhamun's child bride: "I married King Tutankhamen when I was only sixteen years old. I was his first wife." She celebrated Christmas on December 15. In 1919 she gave the wedding-gift land that became Brookfield Zoo. Her fortune, moved into real estate through the Edith Rockefeller McCormick Trust (1923), was wrecked by the crash; she died of cancer on August 25, 1932, aged 59, and was buried at Graceland.
  • The odd detail: A Rockefeller heiress, a trained analyst of the unconscious, who publicly claimed a pharaoh's marriage bed — and set her own date for Christmas.
  • People: Edith Rockefeller McCormick — heiress, analyst, reincarnationist; C. G. Jung — her analyst and beneficiary; Harold F. McCormick — husband, see next entry; James Joyce — her briefly funded writer
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edith_Rockefeller_McCormick ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brookfield_Zoo
  • Confidence: High on dates/quotes above. UNVERIFIED: the "Edithton" planned resort town north of Kenosha (developers Krenn & Dato, mid-1920s, collapsed after 1929) — remembered, not confirmed from a page this session; the Drake as place of death; astrology as a daily practice.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Zürich analysis 1913–21 straddles the Great War; in the setting Switzerland is still neutral, but a Rockefeller heiress living in a German-victorious Europe 1916–21 is a colour change, not a break).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The salon at 1000 Lake Shore Drive is where high society's vampires would be introduced. Her reincarnation claim is either a delusion or a true memory; her Trust's real-estate empire, dying with her in 1932, leaves title to strange parcels of land (a zoo, a ghost resort) for someone to inherit.

Harold F. McCormick — the gland operation and the diva — 1922–1931

  • Where: Chicago (International Harvester), Paris (wedding), Villa Turicum, Lake Forest
  • What happened: After the 1921 divorce and before remarrying, McCormick "sought to fortify himself" with a rejuvenation gland transplant — Wikipedia attributes it to Serge Voronoff, the Paris surgeon famous for grafting monkey glands into ageing men; the older Chicago-press version (a young donor, a Chicago hospital, Dr. Victor Lespinasse) could not be confirmed this session. On August 11, 1922, in Paris, he married the Polish soprano Ganna Walska, whose singing his money could not rescue: at her 1920 Chicago Opera engagement in Zazà she quarrelled with the conductor at dress rehearsal and stormed out before ever appearing; in Havana an audience pelted her with vegetables; Frances Alda said "work as I did, I could not teach Ganna Walska to sing." She bought the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées and vowed never to sing there until she had earned it. They divorced in 1931. Orson Welles borrowed the pair for Citizen Kane's Susan Alexander. McCormick died of a cerebral haemorrhage in Beverly Hills on October 16, 1941.
  • The odd detail: The city's biggest industrial heir, rumoured to be carrying a stranger's (or an ape's) glands, buying an opera house for a wife who would not sing in it.
  • People: Harold F. McCormick — heir, patient, patron; Ganna Walska — soprano, six-times married, later of Lotusland; Serge Voronoff — gland surgeon (per Wikipedia); Orson Welles — the borrower
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Fowler_McCormick ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganna_Walska
  • Confidence: Medium. UNVERIFIED: which surgeon, which hospital, the donor story; McCormick's amateur whistling recordings (remembered, not confirmed).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Gland transplants" are the period's own word for what a vampire's patronage does to an old man. A rich Chicagoan who got young in 1922 and whose wife could not sing but always got the stage is a Golden Circle or high-society vampire thread hiding in plain sight in the society pages.

Samuel Insull's "Throne" and his fall — Nov 4, 1929 – July 16, 1938 (also in 09-22 archive: W08)

  • Where: Civic Opera House, 20 N. Wacker Drive; Insull's empire ran from the Edison Building, 72 W. Adams
  • What happened: Insull, the English-born utilities king who electrified Chicago and the Midwest, built the 45-storey Civic Opera Building (Graham, Anderson, Probst & White; Indiana limestone; 3,563 seats in its day, 3,276 today ⟨fixed: V_R08⟩) for his Chicago Civic Opera; it opened November 4, 1929, six days after the crash. Its shape — a tower with two wings, a tall-backed armchair — earned it "Insull's Throne" (and "Insull's Folly"); the chair "faces west", read by wags as turning its back on New York's Metropolitan. The legend that he built it for a singer wife the Met had snubbed is false. His pyramid of holding companies controlled about $500 million on $27 million of real equity; it collapsed in 1932, wiping out about 600,000 investors. He fled to France, then to Greece (no extradition treaty), was arrested in Turkey and extradited in 1934, tried for mail fraud with 16 co-defendants and acquitted after a seven-week trial and roughly two hours of jury deliberation. He died of a heart attack on July 16, 1938, on the stairs of the Place de la Concorde Métro in Paris, with about 30 francs on him; his estate was about $1,000 against $14 million of debts.
  • The odd detail: An opera house shaped like a throne, opened the week the world ended, whose king died on a subway staircase with pocket change.
  • People: Samuel Insull — utilities magnate, opera patron; Insull's Chicago Civic Opera folded 1932
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civic_Opera_House_(Chicago) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Insull
  • Confidence: High. (Sources differ on the coins in his pocket — 30 francs here; other accounts say less.)
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Greece/Turkey as refuges are unchanged; the setting's Europe is German-led, which may make an American fugitive's extradition a German-brokered favour).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Throne as a literal seat of something; the 600,000 ruined small investors are the population, the men who built the pyramid are the cult. Insull's flight through a German-run Europe is a Section 13 courier story.

The Tribune Tower's stolen world — 1922–1925 onward

  • Where: 435 N. Michigan Avenue
  • What happened: The Tribune's 1922 international design competition ($100,000 in prizes, $50,000 to the winner, 260-plus entries) was won by Howells & Hood's neo-Gothic tower, completed 1925, 463 feet, 36 floors; Eliel Saarinen's second-place entry changed skyscraper design more than the winner. Before construction, Col. Robert R. McCormick had Tribune correspondents bring back rocks and bricks from historic sites, and the practice continued for decades: 149 stones are now embedded at street level (Great Pyramid, Taj Mahal, Great Wall, Parthenon, Edinburgh Castle), plus, from 1999 to 2011, a moon rock on loan. The lobby's Hall of Inscriptions carries quotations on press freedom. Sculptor René Chambellan hid the architects in the entrance: a Robin Hood (Hood) and a howling dog (Howells).
  • The odd detail: A newspaper that sent its reporters out to chip pieces off the world's holy places and mortar them into its wall — in a 1930s setting, some of those stones have been there less than a decade.
  • People: Col. Robert R. McCormick — publisher, collector-by-proxy; John Mead Howells and Raymond Hood — architects; René Paul Chambellan — sculptor
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribune_Tower
  • Confidence: High (the count of 149 is today's; the 1930s count was smaller — UNVERIFIED how many by 1936).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (stones from the Ottoman and German-held East would have been collected under different flags).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A wall of relics from every temple on earth, assembled by a man who thinks in empires — the obvious ward, or the obvious summoning circle, depending on who told the correspondents what to take.

The Colonel: simplified spelling, "Chicagoland", and the flag with a star cut out — 1930s

  • Where: Tribune Tower; Cantigny estate, Wheaton
  • What happened: Robert R. McCormick, a 25% owner of WGN (named for the Tribune's motto "World's Greatest Newspaper"), put "Chicagoland" into common use (first documented on the Tribune's front page, July 27, 1926) ⟨fixed: V_R08⟩, named his Wheaton estate after the 1918 battle he had served in, and used the paper to impose his own simplified spelling (the crusade was "quixotic" and mocked by rivals). He compared the New Deal to Communism. For a period in 1935, to protest Rhode Island's Democratic judiciary, he displayed a 47-star flag outside the Tribune building with Rhode Island's star removed ⟨fixed: V_R08⟩ — until told it was illegal. His wife Amy died in 1939 and he became a near-recluse until remarrying in 1944.
  • The odd detail: The most powerful publisher in the Midwest altered the national flag outside his own building to punish a state ⟨fixed: V_R08⟩.
  • People: Col. Robert R. McCormick — publisher; Amy Irwin Adams McCormick — wife (d. 1939)
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_R._McCormick
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Cantigny is a battle of an American Expeditionary Force that never sailed; the Colonel's war record, his estate's name, and his anti-Roosevelt/anti-Communist line (no Soviet Union) need re-grounding — in the setting his war would be the Southern War in Mexico.

The Palmer "castle" sold to an inventor — 1350 N. Lake Shore Drive — 1928–1950

  • Where: 1350 N. Lake Shore Drive, Gold Coast
  • What happened: Potter and Bertha Palmer's turreted mansion (built 1882–85; budgeted at $90,000, cost over $1 million) was designed without exterior locks or doorknobs — you entered only if someone inside let you in. Bertha, the 1893 fair's grande dame, turned Potter's $8 million into about $15 million in Florida land before she died in 1918. In July 1928 her sons sold the castle for $3 million to Vincent Bendix, the South Bend brake-and-starter magnate, who renamed it "The Bendix Galleries" and hung Rembrandts in it; it came back to Potter Palmer Jr. in 1933 for $2 million after Bendix's Depression troubles. It stood largely empty until demolition in 1950; the art went to the Art Institute.
  • The odd detail: A castle with no way in from the street, and for the whole Depression decade, an inventor's private gallery that nobody entered.
  • People: Bertha Honoré Palmer — society queen (d. 1918); Potter Palmer Jr. — son; Vincent Bendix — inventor-industrialist of South Bend, Indiana
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palmer_Mansion
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 built; holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A great house that admits only those invited from within is the vampire's own rule written in stone; the 1933 repossession is a quiet change of masters.

Julius Rosenwald dies; the Sears man's museum and his grandson the "intended victim" — Jan 6, 1932

  • Where: Highland Park (home); Museum of Science and Industry, Jackson Park; Michigan Boulevard Garden Apartments, 47th & Michigan
  • What happened: Sears president Julius Rosenwald died at home in Highland Park on January 6, 1932, aged 69. After a 1911 visit to Munich's Deutsches Museum he pledged $3 million (eventually over $5 million) to convert the 1893 fair's Palace of Fine Arts into a museum of industry; it opened in stages from 1933, during the Century of Progress, with a working coal mine built from Old Ben No. 17 at Johnston City, Illinois (closed 1923). His Rosenwald Fund (1917) was designed to spend itself to nothing (gone by 1948). In 1929 he built the 421-unit Michigan Boulevard Garden Apartments for Black tenants, taking 2.4% on $2.7 million. His grandson Armand Deutsch believed he had been Leopold and Loeb's intended 1924 victim.
  • The odd detail: The museum's underground coal mine is a real mine's machinery re-buried under a fair palace — a mine shaft in a museum, open through the whole decade.
  • People: Julius Rosenwald — philanthropist; Armand Deutsch — grandson, later Hollywood producer; Ernest Grunsfeld — nephew, architect (also of the Adler)
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Rosenwald ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Science_and_Industry_(Chicago)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — the museum is modelled on a German museum and Germany is now the world's victor; German exhibits and scientific loans would be prestige goods, not curiosities.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A coal mine under a museum under a fair; a fund built to die; a grandson marked by the century's most famous murderers. The IHS would want to know what else Rosenwald moved from Munich.

William Wrigley Jr. dies; the gum king's lodge for the unemployed — 1930–1932

  • Where: Wrigley Building, 400 N. Michigan; Wrigley Lodge (ex-factory, Salvation Army); Catalina Island; died Phoenix
  • What happened: Wrigley died January 26, 1932, in Phoenix, aged 70, of "acute indigestion, complicated by a heart attack and apoplexy". In 1930–31 he had given a six-storey factory building to the Salvation Army as a lodging house for the unemployed (Wrigley Lodge). He had bought control of Catalina Island in 1919, finished its Avalon Casino in 1929, and held the Cubs (majority from 1921; the park renamed for him 1926). He was entombed in a custom sarcophagus in the Wrigley Memorial tower on Catalina until 1947, when the body was moved to Forest Lawn so the gardens could open to the public. Son Philip K. Wrigley ran the company after him.
  • The odd detail: The gum king lay for fifteen years in a tower on his own island; his Chicago factory became a flophouse.
  • People: William Wrigley Jr. — magnate (d. 1932); Philip K. Wrigley — heir
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wrigley_Jr.
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.

Frances Glessner Lee: the Prairie Avenue heiress who bought forensic medicine — 1931

  • Where: Glessner House, 1800 S. Prairie Avenue (her childhood home); Harvard; New Hampshire
  • What happened: Born in Chicago March 25, 1878, daughter of International Harvester's John J. Glessner, she inherited a fortune and in 1931 endowed Harvard's Department of Legal Medicine — the first in the country — and its George Burgess Magrath Library. The freight tunnels: 60 miles of railway forty feet under the Loop — 1899–1959

The Confederate Mound and the 1864 Chicago Conspiracy — Oak Woods, 1895 / the memory of the Golden Circle

6. Bohemia and the fringe

Bughouse Square and the Newberry across the street — peak 1920s–30s

  • Where: Washington Square Park, Clark & Walton, Near North; Newberry Library opposite
  • What happened: Chicago's oldest small park (1842) had been "Bughouse Square" since the 1890s (after the mental hospitals nearby). The Dil Pickle Club grew out of this scene. The Newberry Library's later Bughouse Square Debates (1986–2021) revived it.
  • The odd detail: The city's free-speech forum sat opposite its rarest-books library, one street apart, with the mental hospitals that named it a few blocks away.
  • People: the orators — unnamed here; Jack Jones — Dil Pickle founder (below)

The Dil Pickle Club closes — 18 Tooker Alley, 1917–1933

  • Where: 18 Tooker Place (Tooker Alley), off Dearborn between Chicago and Delaware, Near North
  • What happened: Ex-Wobbly Jack Jones turned a barn into a club (c. 1914–17, incorporated 1917) whose door read "Step High, Stoop Low and Leave Your Dignity Outside"; regulars included Ben Reitman, Sherwood Anderson, Carl Sandburg, Emma Goldman, Clarence Darrow, professors, hoboes, artists and radicals. It closed in 1933 after "150 citations in a single winter" for Prohibition violations, under mob pressure to let bootleggers in, with the literary crowd gone to New York and Hollywood. Jones died in poverty in 1940; a 1944 reopening failed when the building was condemned. Franklin Rosemont called it the most influential little gathering place in the city's history.

  • What happened: The Eastland — the excursion steamer that rolled over at the Clark Street dock on July 24, 1915, killing 844 — was raised, bought by the Navy and commissioned in 1918 as the gunboat/training ship USS Wilmette. In 1921 her guns sank the surrendered German submarine UC-97, which had sailed to the Great Lakes in 1919 under Lt. Charles A. Lockwood for a Liberty Bond exhibition tour of lake ports ⟨fixed: V_R08⟩, as a target off Chicago; the gunners included the men who had fired America's first cannon and torpedo shots of the Great War. Through the 1930s Wilmette took naval reservists on Great Lakes training cruises; in August 1943 she carried Roosevelt on a ten-day fishing trip to McGregor Bay (Ontario, Lake Huron) and Whitefish Bay ⟨fixed: V_R08⟩; scrapped 1947.
  • The odd detail: The city's worst death-ship spent the whole 1930s as a Navy schoolship on the lake, and a German U-boat lies on the bottom of Lake Michigan where she put it.
  • People: UC-97 — the submarine; the Eastland/Wilmette — the ship
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Wilmette
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS hard — no American entry into the war means no surrendered U-boat, no "first shots", no Liberty Loan tour. The Eastland's salvage and Navy purchase can still happen (1915 is pre-break); the U-boat on the lake bottom must become something else — a German gift, a bought boat, a wreck of another kind.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): 844 dead in her hull, then twenty years of Navy boys sleeping in it. The one ship in the setting that is a haunted house and a warship at once; the Great Lakes Naval Training Station is Section 13's obvious lakeside partner.

Chinatown's "city hall": the On Leong Merchants Association Building — May 2, 1928

  • Where: 2216 S. Wentworth Ave. at Cermak Rd. (then 22nd St.)
  • What happened: Built 1926–27 for $1 million by the Norwegian-American architects Michaelsen & Rognstad, with glazed Teco terra cotta from Crystal Lake, Illinois standing in for Chinese liuli tile, it opened May 2, 1928 with meeting halls, a school, a shrine and the On Leong association's offices — informally Chinatown's city hall and immigrant-aid centre. On April 20, 1988 federal agents found a casino hidden on the second floor and $350,000 in a vault; the government seized the building under racketeering law; a church bought it in 1993 as the Pui Tak Center.

  • Where: Chicago (born July 1, 1875; died Feb 26, 1976, aged 100)
  • What happened: Joseph Weil's cons — fake banks staffed with hired "customers", oil land he did not own, a Colorado silver mine sold to Andrew Mellon's brother's circle for a reported $500,000, "talking" dogs, rigged prize fights, and (by his own claim) $2 million from Mussolini — added up, per biographer W. Cap Streeter's "District of Lake Michigan" — the claim dies in 1928

  • Where: Streeterville, the made land east of Michigan Ave. north of the river
  • What happened: On July 10, 1886, George Wellington Streeter ran his steamboat Reutan aground off Superior Street (he blamed a storm the weather records don't show), got N. K. Fairbank's leave to stay, refused to leave, forged federal titles, invited contractors to dump rubble around the boat, and claimed the growing 186 acres as the "United States District of Lake Michigan", outside Illinois law — a 1902 witness quoted him: "We'll get a million out of it." He met evictions with shotguns and boiling water, staged squatter "invasions", was convicted of manslaughter in 1902 and sent to prison (the acquittal story is the legend version) ⟨fixed: V_R08⟩, and died of pneumonia January 22, 1921, aged 84, the mayor at his funeral. His heirs kept suing until April 1928 ⟨fixed: V_R08⟩, when the courts ruled for Chicago Title and Trust. The Hancock tower stands where his shanty was.
  • The odd detail: The Gold Coast's newest luxury district was, within living memory, a self-declared independent territory defended with a shotgun.
  • People: George W. Streeter — squatter-sovereign; "Ma" Streeter — widow; N. K. Fairbank — the landowner who let him stay
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Streeter
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened; holds (the 1928 ruling holds).

The ex-president's house in Evanston — Charles G. Dawes at 225 Greenwood St. (BENDS: he is a former president in the setting)

  • The odd detail: In the setting, the man in this house was President of the United States 1927–29; the Nobel Prize he holds in the real record was for helping Germany pay — in the setting, Germany is paid.
  • People: Charles G. Dawes — banker, (setting) president; Caro Dawes — wife
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Gates_Dawes_House ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosehill_Cemetery

Dick Tracy is born in the Tribune's syndicate — October 4, 1931

  • Where: Chicago Tribune–New York News Syndicate, Tribune Tower; Chester Gould's studio (later Woodstock, Illinois)
  • What happened: Chester Gould drew "Plainclothes Tracy"; Joseph Medill Patterson of the Tribune syndicate renamed him Dick Tracy and supplied the origin (his sweetheart's father murdered by robbers); the strip debuted October 4, 1931 in the Detroit Mirror and spread nationwide. Tracy was modelled on Eliot Ness; his first great villain, "Big Boy", was Capone. By April 1937 it was America's third-favourite strip (Fortune poll), behind Little Orphan Annie and Popeye, and already attacked for its violence.

Bubbly Creek — the river that caught fire — 1900s–1930s

  • Where: South Fork of the South Branch of the Chicago River, Bridgeport/McKinley Park, behind the Union Stock Yards
  • What happened: The packers dumped blood and entrails into the two-mile fork for decades; decomposition sent up methane and hydrogen sulphide — "Bubbles of carbonic gas will rise to the surface and burst, and make rings two or three feet wide" (Sinclair, 1906) — and the grease crust was thick enough to walk on and sometimes burned. The bubbling continued through the 1930s (the yards ran until 1971); remediation was proposed in 2007 ($15.4 million) and stalled in 2015 over contaminated sediment.
  • The odd detail: A creek of animal blood, crusted like lava, that bubbled and burned in the middle of the city all decade.
  • People: Upton Sinclair — witness; the packers — polluters
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubbly_Creek
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 origin; holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Every ritual needs a river of blood; Chicago has a real one. What was fed into it besides cattle.

The Atwood Sphere — the planetarium before the planetarium — 1913

  • Where: Chicago Academy of Sciences, Lincoln Park (Laflin Building) in the 1930s; now at the Adler
  • What happened: A 15-foot sheet-metal globe with 692 holes drilled for the brightest stars, built in 1913 for the Academy; visitors sat inside while it turned around them — "the world's oldest operating immersive sky simulator". It had to be lowered through the Adler's ceiling when it moved (date not given on the page; remembered as 1995).
  • The odd detail: Before the Adler's German projector (1930), Chicago's sky was a tin ball you climbed inside.
  • People: Wallace W. Atwood — designer (remembered; not named on the page); Chicago Academy of Sciences — owner
  • Sources: https://www.adlerplanetarium.org/explore/atwood-sphere/
  • Confidence: Medium.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened; holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Two skies in one city: the German machine on the lake, and the old tin sky in Lincoln Park. They do not show the same stars.

Rosehill: Boyington's gate, the Tiffany mausoleum, and a president in the ground — 1864 / 1914 / 1951

  • Where: Rosehill Cemetery, 5800 N. Ravenswood Ave.
  • What happened: William Boyington's 1864 castellated Lemont-limestone gate is the twin of his Water Tower (1869); the name is either pioneer Hiram Roe's hill misspelled or the wild white roses that grew there. Sidney Lovell's 1914 community mausoleum — Chicago's largest — has 30-plus stained-glass windows, several by Tiffany, and holds both Aaron Montgomery Ward and Richard Warren Sears, the rival mail-order kings. Also here: 16 Union generals, Julius Rosenwald (1932), Charles Hull (Hull House's landlord), Oscar Mayer, victims of the Iroquois fire and the Eastland, and Vice President Charles G. Dawes (1951). Legends of Lulu Fellows and Frances Pearce are folklore (not on the page).
  • The odd detail: Sears and Ward, who fought for forty years, spend eternity in the same marble corridor; the gate to it is the Water Tower's sister.
  • People: W. W. Boyington — architect; Sidney Lovell — mausoleum architect; Sears, Ward, Rosenwald, Dawes — the interred
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosehill_Cemetery
  • Confidence: High; ghost legends folklore (labelled).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Dawes is a former president in the setting (see the Evanston entry).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A mausoleum lit through Tiffany glass where the men who invented selling-by-mail lie a corridor apart: the perfect meeting place for the two people in the city who must never be seen together.

Bohemian National Cemetery: founded on a refused burial; Cermak's tomb — 1877 / 1933 (touches 09-22 archive: R05)

  • Where: 5255 N. Pulaski Rd., North Park
  • What happened: Founded in 1877 after Marie Silhanek, a Czech Catholic, was refused burial by several Catholic cemeteries for not making her Easter Duty; the Czech community bought its own ground. Mayor Anton Cermak, shot beside Roosevelt in Miami in February 1933 and dead March 6, lies in a mausoleum in Section 21. The cemetery holds Eastland victims (many of the dead were Czech Western Electric families) and is known for photograph-and-memento columbarium niches; in 2009 a Cubs fan built "Beyond the Vines", a Wrigley-wall columbarium with 288 niches and real stadium seats.
  • The odd detail: The mayor of Chicago is buried in a cemetery that exists because the Church would not bury a woman.
  • People: Marie Silhanek — the refused; Anton Cermak — mayor (d. 1933)
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemian_National_Cemetery_(Chicago)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Cermak's death at Roosevelt's side is in the real record; the brief keeps the presidents' calendar from 1933).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A secular Czech cemetery is ground the IHS does not hold; the Easter-Duty refusal is the kind of Church rule a Kabbalah contract is written around.

Jane Addams dies; the funeral in the Hull House courtyard — May 21, 1935 (also in 09-22 archive: R03)

  • Where: Passavant Hospital (death); Hull House, 800 S. Halsted (funeral); Cedarville, Illinois (burial)
  • What happened: Addams died at 74 after surgery for an abdominal obstruction; doctors had found cancer in 1931 and never told her. First American woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize (1931, shared with Nicholas Murray Butler; she gave her share to the Women's International League for Peace and Freedom). The DAR had expelled her for pacifism after 1917. The 1913 "Devil Baby" legend — crowds besieging Hull House for weeks to see a horned, cloven-hoofed infant said to be hidden there, which Addams wrote up as a study in immigrant women's memory — is remembered, not on the page read.
  • The odd detail (deeper than R03): She was carried out of a house that, twenty-two years earlier, thousands of neighbours were certain held the Devil's child.
  • People: Jane Addams — settlement founder (d. 1935); Nicholas Murray Butler — co-laureate
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Addams ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hull_House
  • Confidence: High on death/Nobel; Devil Baby UNVERIFIED this session (well-known; Addams's own 1916 essay "The Devil Baby at Hull-House").
  • Divergence check: BENDS — her DAR expulsion was for opposing American entry into the Great War; with no American entry, her pacifism has no war to oppose at home — she would have opposed the Southern War instead.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Devil Baby is the owner's three layers in one building: the crowd's belief, the folklore Addams recorded, and whatever was in the upstairs room.

The Chicago Historical Society moves onto the graveyard — 1932

  • Where: Clark St. & North Ave., Lincoln Park (Graham, Anderson, Probst & White, Federal style); old building 632 N. Dearborn (1896, now the Excalibur nightclub)
  • What happened: The Society's new museum opened in 1932 on the south edge of Lincoln Park — the former City Cemetery — after 36 years at Dearborn & Ontario. Its treasures came largely from Charles F. Gunther's estate (bought 1920 for $150,000): Lincoln's deathbed and the furniture of the room he died in, clothing said to be worn by the Lincolns at Ford's Theatre, the table on which Lee surrendered; the Society's first collection, including Lincoln's final draft of the Emancipation Proclamation, burned in 1871. The 1896 Dearborn building's ghost stories (legend says Eastland bodies were laid out there — folklore, not on the page) belong to the old house.
  • The odd detail: In 1932 the city put Lincoln's deathbed in a new building on top of its first cemetery, a few yards from the Couch tomb.
  • People: Charles F. Gunther — candy-maker and relic collector; Graham, Anderson, Probst & White — architects
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_History_Museum ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ira_Couch
  • Confidence: High (the page's "built by the WPA" for a 1932 opening is chronologically doubtful — the WPA dates from 1935; treat the opening year as the fact).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Gunther's relics were bought wholesale and some are doubtful; a museum of possibly-false holy objects on a graveyard is where a Section 13 archivist would keep the true ones.
