R28 — Women of 1930s Chicago and the Great Lakes

Lane: R28 — investigators, criminals, reporters, professionals, activists, mediums and survivors: the real women's lives, jobs, dangers and precedents a GM needs when a player builds a woman investigator. Sweep: 2026-09-26 Chicago & Great Lakes research sweep (see BRIEF.md). Date written: 2026-09-26 Model: Claude Fable 5.1 (research agent)

Overview

Sixty-five entries on the real women a GM needs when a player builds a woman investigator in 1930s Chicago and around the lakes. The strongest finding is that the access a woman had in the decade was wider than the rulebook stereotype suggests, provided she held one of a handful of jobs: Chicago had sworn policewomen from 13 August 1913 (matrons from 1885, Sergeant Marie Owens from 1891, Detective Alice Clement from 1913, a Black policewoman from 1918), Detroit ran a forty-officer Women's Division whose chief wrote the national policewoman's textbook in 1933, the Juvenile Court's women probation officers and stenographers walked every tenement, and settlement, relief and Women's-Bureau women carried clipboards into any factory. The bench and bar opened too: Mary Bartelme presided over the Juvenile Court to 1933, Florence Allen of Cleveland was the first woman federal appeals judge in March 1934, Edith Sampson and Violette Anderson practised from the South Side. The press box had Genevieve Forbes Herrick (the Capone interview, 1930), Ione Quinby (Murder for Love, 1931) and the living memory of Maurine Watkins's Chicago. The criminal side gives a GM the gun molls with dates (Helen Gillis's blanket, Mary Kinder's $3,116.20, Dolores Delaney shot by her own side, Louise Rolfe's Supreme Court case of 7 November 1932 that made a willing woman no conspirator), the women's prison at Dwight (opened 24 November 1930), and the first woman Illinois electrocuted (28 January 1938). Occult and religious women are thinner than hoped — the Bangs sisters' false-name graves, Irna Phillips's "spiritual" soap opera, the Rev. Mary G. Evans, the WCTU's emptying at Evanston — because Wikipedia holds few of Chicago's 1930s clairvoyants; that lane is the first lead to chase. Divergence: almost everything holds on the domestic calendar; the BENDS are Dilling (no Red Scare), Fuldheim (her 1932 Hitler interview), Thyra Edwards (Spain/USSR), Golda Meyerson's Palestine (Ottoman Jerusalem in the setting), and Mary Anderson's five presidents. Web search was exhausted before this lane began, Chronicling America rate-limited every call, and the fetch tool hit its session cap two-thirds through, so policewomen numbers and pay for the 1930s, the Morals Court, women bootleggers, society clairvoyants and the decade's vanished women remain UNVERIFIED leads rather than entries.

1. The badge and the licence

Kate Warne — Pinkerton hires the first woman detective, in Chicago — 1856–1868 (living memory: the profession's founding legend)

  • Where: Pinkerton's National Detective Agency, Chicago (the Loop); grave in the Pinkerton family plot, Graceland Cemetery, 4001 N. Clark Street.
  • What happened: In 1856 a 23-year-old widow answered a Pinkerton newspaper advertisement at the Chicago office. Allan Pinkerton told her "It is not the custom to employ women detectives!"; she answered that women "have an eye for detail and are excellent observers," and he hired her. In the 1858 Adams Express embezzlement case she befriended the suspect's wife and the agency recovered $39,515 of the $50,000 stolen. In 1860 Pinkerton made her superintendent of a new Female Detective Bureau in Chicago, where she recruited Elizabeth H. Baker and Hattie Lawton. In 1861, as "Mrs. Cherry" and "Mrs. M. Barley," she posed as a wealthy Southern visitor in Baltimore's secessionist parlours and supplied "many of the key details" of the plot to kill president-elect Lincoln. She died of pneumonia in Chicago on 28 January 1868, about 35 years old. Pinkerton counted her among his five best operatives: "She has never let me down."
  • The odd detail: Her gravestone in the Pinkerton plot at Graceland misspells her name "Warn." Every woman who went into detective work in 1930s Chicago — Pinkerton, Burns, store detective, police matron — worked in the shadow of a founding legend buried a streetcar ride from the Loop.
  • People: Kate Warne — first woman detective in America; Allan Pinkerton — her employer; Elizabeth H. Baker, Hattie Lawton — the bureau's first recruits.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kate_Warne
  • Confidence: High on the outline (Wikipedia, read this session); the Baltimore details rest on Pinkerton's own memoirs, which historians treat with caution.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Pinkerton Female Detective Bureau (1860) predates Col. Wood's Secret Service (1865) by five years — the obvious ancestor of a women's desk in the Secret Service, "the old guard," with FBI Section 13 the newcomer that poaches its graduates; and the Baltimore plotters she penetrated were the sort of Southern secret-society men the owner's Golden Circle descends from (the real Knights of the Golden Circle date from 1854 — any Warne–KGC link is period rumour, not established fact). A woman investigator PC could carry "Warne's method": the parlour, not the alley.

Alice Clement — Chicago's first woman detective, and the film the city would not show — 1909–1926 (living memory)

  • Where: Chicago Police Detective Bureau (City Hall) and the department stores of State Street; demoted in 1926 to the West Chicago Avenue station.
  • What happened: Born in Milwaukee in 1878, Clement joined the Chicago police in 1909 to patrol department stores for pickpockets and shoplifters, and in 1913 — the year the city appointed its first squad of policewomen — became its first woman detective. She worked in disguise, carried a revolver, and was a press favourite; in 1919 she wrote, produced and starred as herself in a feature film, Dregs of the City. In one case she reopened the death of a young woman written off as typhoid-related and showed it was murder, against her male colleagues' conclusion. In mid-1926 she was demoted from the Detective Bureau to a district station, went on medical leave with diabetes, and died on 26 December 1926, aged 49.
  • The odd detail: A serving Chicago detective made a movie about herself — and (per period press accounts summarised on her Wikipedia page; the censorship detail is UNVERIFIED here) the city's own censor board refused to pass it. [UNVERIFIED — V_R28]
  • People: Alice Clement — detective, 1913–26.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice_Clement
  • Confidence: Medium (single tertiary page read; the 1913 date of the first ten policewomen and the size of that squad are UNVERIFIED in this session — see the policewomen entry below).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (start), happened; her 1920s career holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A dead woman detective whose last case file (the "typhoid" murder) was quietly reclassified; her 1919 film, banned in the city that employed her, contains a scene no one can explain. Living memory for any policewoman PC recruited in the 1930s.

3. The bar and the bench

Mary Bartelme — "Suitcase Mary" of the Juvenile Court — assistant judge 1913, elected 1923, presiding judge 1927–June 1933

  • Where: Cook County Juvenile Court, Chicago (the court and detention home stood at Ewing/Roosevelt Road and Ogden, west of the Loop — building location UNVERIFIED here); the three "Mary B. Clubs."
  • What happened: Born in Chicago 24 July 1866, a five-year public-school teacher, Northwestern law graduate (1894), Bartelme was appointed Public Guardian of Cook County by Governor Tanner in 1897 — the first woman in the post — finding homes for orphans and managing minors' estates. From 1913, on Judge Merritt Pinckney's recommendation, she sat as assistant judge of the Juvenile Court and convened a Girls' Court staffed entirely by women for girls aged 10–17. On 6 November 1923 she was elected to the Circuit Court by more than 14,000 votes over Democrat D. J. Normoyle, the first woman elected to an Illinois court of high jurisdiction; from 1927 she presided over the Juvenile Court until she retired in June 1933. Her three Mary B. Clubs (1914, 1916, 1921) housed girls who could not go home — over 2,000 in ten years — and each girl left with a suitcase holding underwear, toiletries and a new dress. A vice-chair of the National Woman's Party in 1916–17, she died in Carmel, California, on 25 July 1954.
  • The odd detail: The suitcase. Every girl the court could not send home walked out of a Mary Club with a new dress and a case with her name on it — a physical token a GM can put in a dead woman's closet twenty years later.
  • People: Mary Bartelme — judge; Merritt Pinckney — the juvenile judge who brought her in.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Bartelme
  • Confidence: High (Wikipedia citing American National Biography and Tribune obituaries).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Girls' Court records 1913–33 are a closed archive of every runaway, "incorrigible" and abused girl in Cook County — the intake file where a Section 13 investigator finds that a vanished 1936 taxi-dancer was a Mary Club girl in 1924. Bartelme's successor bench is where a woman lawyer PC would have learned the trade.

Edith Sampson — probation officer of the Juvenile Court by day, South Side lawyer by night — 1925–1942

  • Where: Cook County Juvenile Court; a law office on Chicago's South Side (address UNVERIFIED).
  • What happened: Born in Pittsburgh 13 October 1901, Sampson came to Chicago as a social worker, graduated from John Marshall Law School in 1925 with a dean's commendation for topping her jurisprudence class, and in 1927 became the first woman of any race to take a Master of Laws at Loyola; she passed the Illinois bar the same year. From 1925 to 1942 she worked as a probation officer attached to the Juvenile Court while keeping a private practice on the South Side (she had opened an office in 1924, before her degree, per Wikipedia). She was admitted to the U.S. Supreme Court bar in 1934. She became an assistant state's attorney only in 1947, the first Black U.S. delegate to the United Nations in 1950, and Illinois's first Black woman judge in 1962.
  • The odd detail: A Loyola-trained, Supreme-Court-admitted lawyer whose day job for seventeen Depression years was walking Bronzeville's tenements as a court probation officer — the one investigator whom every landlord, policy runner and storefront preacher had to let in the door.
  • People: Edith Spurlock Sampson — lawyer and probation officer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edith_S._Sampson
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The template for a Black woman investigator PC: a probation officer's badge opens doors a federal one cannot. A Kabbalah-of-David contract could reach her through the Juvenile Court's Jewish social-service agencies.

Violette Neatley Anderson — from court reporter to the Supreme Court bar; dead at 55 — 1905–24 December 1937

  • Where: Chicago (court-reporting agency 1905–20; City Prosecutor's office 1922–23; private practice on the South Side).
  • What happened: Born in London on 16 July 1882, Anderson ran a court-reporting agency in Chicago from 1905 to 1920, took her LL.B. from Chicago Law School in 1920 and became the first Black woman admitted to the Illinois bar. In 1922–23 she served as the city's first woman assistant city prosecutor. On 29 January 1926 she became the first Black woman admitted to practise before the U.S. Supreme Court. Through the 1930s she kept a private practice, sat on the executive board of the Chicago Council of Social Agencies, and in 1936 lobbied Congress for the Bankhead–Jones Farm Tenant Act (signed 1937), which lent tenant farmers money to buy land. She died in Chicago on 24 December 1937.
  • The odd detail: Fifteen years as a court reporter first — she had transcribed the city's criminal trials verbatim before she ever argued one.
  • People: Violette Neatley Anderson — lawyer; Emma Simpson — the 1920 murder defendant she is said to have won acquittal for (case details UNVERIFIED this session).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violette_Neatley_Anderson
  • Confidence: High on dates; Medium on the Simpson case.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A woman who spent fifteen years taking down every word said in Chicago courtrooms owns a private stenographic archive — the transcripts that were never entered into evidence. Her Christmas Eve 1937 death is a hook if a GM wants one.

2. The press box

Genevieve Forbes Herrick — the Tribune's front-page woman — 1918–May 1934

  • Where: Chicago Tribune, Tribune Tower, 435 N. Michigan Avenue; then Washington.
  • What happened: Chicago-born (21 May 1894), Forbes joined the Tribune in 1918. In 1921 she went to County Wexford and came back in steerage disguised as an Irish immigrant; her 13-part series on the abuse of immigrants at Ellis Island forced congressional action and the removal of the Ellis Island commissioner. She covered the Leopold–Loeb hearings of 1924, where she met fellow reporter John Origen Herrick — they married on 6 September 1924, and the judge reportedly delayed sentencing to accommodate the wedding. In March 1930 she interviewed Al Capone, who told her: "All I ever did is sell beer and whiskey to our best people." From 1933 she was one of Eleanor Roosevelt's "faithful four" at the First Lady's women-only press conferences and president of the Women's National Press Club (1933–35). She resigned from the Tribune in May 1934 after criticism from Col. McCormick, and wrote for the New York Daily News and Country Gentleman. She died in Santa Fe on 17 December 1962.
  • The odd detail: The judge in the "crime of the century" is said to have moved his sentencing date around a reporter's wedding.
  • People: Genevieve Forbes Herrick — reporter; John Origen Herrick — husband and colleague; Robert R. McCormick — the publisher who drove her out.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genevieve_Forbes_Herrick
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Eleanor Roosevelt is First Lady from 1933 in the setting too).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The model for a woman reporter PC: undercover in steerage, a Capone interview, a Washington desk. Her 1934 break with McCormick can be given an occult reason — a story the Tribune spiked.

Maurine Watkins and Chicago — the girl reporter who wrote the play — 1924 / 30 December 1926 / films 1927 and 1942 (living memory; cross-ref R06 "Beulah Annan and Murderess Row")

  • Where: Chicago Tribune; the Cook County Jail's women's tier ("Murderess Row").
  • What happened: Watkins (born 27 July 1896, Kentucky) spent about eight months of 1924 on the Tribune, filing some fifty stories, mostly on two women accused of shooting their men: Beulah Annan and the cabaret singer Belva Gaertner. Both were acquitted; Watkins thought both guilty and competed for scoops with the Evening Post's Ione Quinby. At Yale Drama School under George Pierce Baker she turned the cases into the play Chicago — Annan became Roxie Hart, Gaertner Velma Kelly, the two defence lawyers merged into Billy Flynn, the rival sob sisters into Mary Sunshine. It opened on Broadway 30 December 1926 (172 performances), toured two years (Clark Gable in the Los Angeles company), and was filmed by DeMille in 1927 and as Roxie Hart with Ginger Rogers in 1942. Watkins later refused Bob Fosse the musical rights; after her death (10 August 1969) her trustee said she had "become disturbed that she had assisted in getting an acquittal for a murderer."
  • The odd detail: The reporter believed her sympathetic coverage had helped acquit a killer — and spent the rest of her life refusing to let the story be told again.
  • People: Maurine Watkins — reporter/playwright; Beulah Annan, Belva Gaertner — the acquitted; Ione Quinby — her rival (see below).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurine_Dallas_Watkins
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1930 events, happened; the 1930s film and touring productions hold.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): In a 1930s Chicago everyone has seen the play or the picture; a woman PC on the crime beat is "a Mary Sunshine" to every cop she meets. The occult layer: what if Watkins was right and something else got Annan off?

Margaret Bourke-White — the woman with the magnesium flares in the Otis Steel mill, Cleveland — 1927–1930; Life cover 23 November 1936

  • Where: Cleveland (Otis Steel Company works, the Flats); later New York.
  • What happened: Fresh from Cornell in 1927, Bourke-White opened a commercial studio in Cleveland and photographed architecture and industry. Otis Steel's guards first refused her — steel-making was a defence industry and they doubted a woman could stand the heat of the floor; once inside she found the film of the day blind to the red-orange glow of molten steel, and solved it by lighting the pours with magnesium flares, producing "some of the best steel-factory photographs of that era." Fortune took her in 1929; her photograph of the Fort Peck Dam spillway ran on the cover of the first issue of Life (23 November 1936), and her February 1937 picture of Louisville's Black flood refugees queued under a billboard reading "World's Highest Standard of Living" became a Depression icon.
  • The odd detail: The magnesium flares: a woman photographer setting off white fire in a Cleveland mill at night because her film could not see the colour of steel.
  • People: Margaret Bourke-White — photographer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Bourke-White
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the "defence industry" nervousness of 1927 reads differently in a Germany-won world, but the mill and the pictures stand).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A woman photographer PC who lights the dark with flares; her Cleveland mill negatives (1927–29) as evidence of something in the Flats before the Torso murders (R06). IHS's Cleveland seat is a short drive away.

8. The picket line and the settlement house

Mary McDowell — "Fighting Mary" of the Stock Yards dies — settlement founded 1 January 1894; Commissioner of Public Welfare 1923; died 14 October 1936

  • Where: University of Chicago Settlement, 4688 S. Gross Avenue (the street is now McDowell Avenue), Back of the Yards.
  • What happened: Born in Cincinnati 30 November 1854, McDowell headed the University of Chicago Settlement from its founding in January 1894, running classes, a kindergarten and vocational placement in the packing-house district. She stood with the strikers in the 1904 stockyard strike, fought the open garbage dumps and clay pits of "Bubbly Creek" country, sat on the city's 1913 Commission on Waste, and in 1923 Mayor Dever made her Commissioner of Public Welfare, where she set up employment and social-survey bureaus. She died of a paralytic stroke in Chicago on 14 October 1936.
  • The odd detail: The city renamed her street after her, so the settlement's own address moved from Gross Avenue to McDowell Avenue.
  • People: Mary McDowell — settlement head; William E. Dever — the reform mayor who hired her.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_McDowell
  • Confidence: High on dates; her 1930s activity is thin in the source.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Her death in October 1936 falls weeks before the setting's December 1936 eclipse; the settlement's files on the Yards (dumps, packing-house deaths, Bubbly Creek) are an investigator's lead source. A werewolf lane through the stockyards writes itself.

Agnes Nestor — the glove worker who ran the Women's Trade Union League for 35 years — WTUL president 1913–1948

  • Where: Chicago (the WTUL of Chicago; Glove Makers Local 2).
  • What happened: Born in Grand Rapids 24 June 1880, Nestor led the women glove workers of Chicago out on strike and founded Glove Makers Local 2 in 1902, rose through the International Glove Workers Union (general president 1913–15, vice-president to 1938), and from 1913 until her death was president of the Chicago Women's Trade Union League. She had helped win the Illinois ten-hour law for women in 1909; the eight-hour day the League campaigned for came in 1937. In the 1930s she served on National Recovery Administration code work on worker safety and spent her last decade recruiting unorganised glove workers and fighting the wartime relaxation of labour laws. She died in Chicago on 28 December 1948; her autobiography Woman's Labor Leader appeared posthumously (Rockford, 1954).
  • The odd detail: A woman who left school at fourteen to sew gloves ended up writing federal industrial codes.
  • People: Agnes Nestor — union president.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnes_Nestor
  • Confidence: High on offices; the 1937 Illinois eight-hour law is stated in the source without detail (UNVERIFIED as to the act's exact terms).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the NRA exists under Roosevelt 1933–).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The WTUL office is where a working-class woman investigator gets her first list of names — every sweatshop, every foreman, every girl who stopped coming to work.

Margaret Haley — "the lady labor slugger" of the Chicago Teachers' Federation dies — 5 January 1939 (cross-ref R03 "Payless paydays" and "The teachers take City Hall")

  • Where: Chicago Teachers' Federation offices; died at Englewood Hospital.
  • What happened: Born in Joliet 15 November 1861, Haley became the Federation's paid business representative in 1901 and with Catherine Goggin made the women elementary teachers' union the most feared lobby in the city; her investigation of tax-dodging corporations turned up more than $2 million in uncollected taxes and, after years of litigation, $600,000 went to teacher salaries. She was the first elementary teacher to address the NEA (1901, "Why Teachers Should Organize"), and engineered Ella Flagg Young's election as NEA president in 1910. Through the "payless paydays" of 1929–34 (R03) she was still the old guard, and she kept the Federation out of the 1937 merger that made the Chicago Teachers Union. She died of a heart attack at Englewood Hospital on 5 January 1939, aged 77.
  • The odd detail: The teachers' union that stormed the Loop banks in 1933 was, in the eyes of its founder, a women's organisation first — she never let it merge with the men.
  • People: Margaret Haley — CTF; Catherine Goggin — her partner (died 1916); Ella Flagg Young — the superintendent they made.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Haley
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A teacher PC's union card. The Federation's tax-fraud files (who owned what in the Loop, and hid it) are exactly the ledger a vampire-in-high-society plot needs.

Elizabeth Dilling — The Red Network from Kenilworth — 1931 trip; book 1934; Patriotic Research Bureau 1938 (BENDS)

  • Where: Kenilworth, Illinois (North Shore, about 20 miles north of the Loop); the Patriotic Research Bureau, Chicago.
  • What happened: Chicago-born (19 April 1894) and married to a lawyer, Dilling toured the Soviet Union in 1931 with her family, came home describing "atheism, sex degeneracy, broken homes [and] class hatred," and took to the lecture circuit — DAR chapters, American Legion posts — showing her Russian home movies. In 1934 she self-published The Red Network, a directory of more than 1,300 people and 460 organisations she called communist or fellow-travelling (eight printings, 16,000+ copies by 1941, sold by mail, in bookshops, through churches and to police departments). The Roosevelt Red Record followed two weeks before the 1936 election. In 1938 she founded the Patriotic Research Bureau in Chicago, staffed by "Christian women and girls" from Moody Bible Institute, issuing a free 25–30-page bulletin. Indicted for sedition 21 July 1942 with 27 others; the case collapsed 22 November 1946. She said of Germany: "The German people under Hitler are contented and happy."
  • The odd detail: A North Shore society matron's card index of 1,300 names, sold to police departments as intelligence.
  • People: Elizabeth Dilling — author; Albert Dilling — husband and lawyer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Dilling
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — no Soviet Union and no Red Scare in the setting, and no Hitler in office. The woman and the method hold: a Kenilworth matron who compiles a directory of "the network" and sells it to the police. What she hunts must change.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Rewrite her as the compiler of The Black Network — a self-published directory of occultists, spiritualists, Theosophists and "German agents," staffed by Moody Bible girls, bought by Section 13 and by the Golden Circle both. Half her names are innocent; some are not.

Genora Johnson and the Women's Emergency Brigade — Flint, 11 January–11 February 1937 (cross-ref R03 and R07 for the sit-down itself)

  • Where: Flint, Michigan — Fisher Body No. 1 and No. 2, Chevrolet No. 4.
  • What happened: Genora Albro Johnson (born Kalamazoo 20 April 1913), a 23-year-old strike wife, organised the Women's Auxiliary (food, childcare, a first-aid post) and on the night of 11 January 1937 — the "Battle of the Running Bulls," when Flint police tried to retake Fisher No. 2 with tear gas and buckshot — took the sound-car microphone and called the women of Flint to break through the police line. She then founded the Women's Emergency Brigade: red berets and armbands, and under their winter coats bats, cudgels, chains and whips; she refused the word "women" in the name "lest someone thought they were there to feed and clothe, not fight in the streets." On 1 February the Brigade fronted the diversion at Chevrolet No. 9 that let the men seize Chevrolet No. 4. GM signed on 11 February. Johnson was blacklisted, became chief steward of an all-women UAW local at Briggs in Detroit, and in 1945 was beaten with a lead pipe in her home — Senator Kefauver's later inquiry found the attack on her and other UAW officers had been bought from organised crime. Press called her "the Joan of Arc of labor."
  • The odd detail: The Brigade carried chains and whips under respectable winter coats, and wore red berets so the men inside could count them from the windows.
  • People: Genora Johnson Dollinger — organiser; Frank Murphy — governor who would not send the Guard in.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genora_Johnson_Dollinger
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Roosevelt-era labour law stands), though the Communist-organiser strand of the real strike thins in a world with no Soviet Union.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Fifty women in red berets who will break windows for a cause are the best militia a Hunter Guild recruiter ever saw; the 1945 lead-pipe attack shows who else noticed.

4. The wards and the clinics

Dr. Bertha Van Hoosen — "the Petticoat Surgeon" of Cook County — chief of gynaecology at County 1913; Loyola's head of obstetrics 1918; through the 1930s

  • Where: Cook County Hospital, 1835 W. Harrison Street; Loyola University School of Medicine; her private clinic (opened 1892).
  • What happened: Born at Stony Creek, Michigan, 26 March 1863, Van Hoosen opened a Chicago practice in 1892, taught anatomy at Northwestern's Women's Medical School, and in 1902 took a clinical professorship of gynaecology at the University of Illinois over open opposition. In 1913 she became chief of the gynaecological staff at Cook County Hospital — the first woman to win a civil-service hospital appointment — and in 1918 acting head and professor of obstetrics at Loyola, the first woman to head a division at a coeducational medical school. She co-founded and first presided over the American Medical Women's Association (1915), championed scopolamine-morphine "twilight sleep" for labour, and devised a "buttonhole" appendectomy. Louise Rolfe, "the Blonde Alibi," and Gebardi v. United States — 14 February 1929; married 1931; the Supreme Court frees them both, 7 November 1932 (cross-ref R01 "Machine Gun Jack McGurn murdered in a bowling alley")

  • Where: Chicago; the federal District Court for the Northern District of Illinois; the Seventh Circuit; the Supreme Court.
  • What happened: When the St. Valentine's Day killers were sought, Jack McGurn's girlfriend Louise Rolfe swore they had spent the whole of 14 February 1929 together; the press called her "the Blonde Alibi," and McGurn was never tried for the massacre. (They married in 1931 — the standard account is that a wife could not be made to testify against her husband.) United States, 287 U.S. 112, McGurn being tried under his birth name. McGurn was shot dead in the Avenue Recreation bowling alley, 805 N. 10. Work, wages and closed doors (continued)

What she was hired as, and what stood in her way — a GM's summary from the entries above, 1929–1939

  • Where: Chicago and the lakes generally.
