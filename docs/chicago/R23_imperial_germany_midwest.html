R23 — Imperial Germany in Chicago and the Midwest, 1871–1917: the template for a victor's presence in the 1930s

  • Lane: R23 — the Kaiser's Germany as it really stood in Chicago, Milwaukee and Cincinnati before America's 1917 break: the diplomats, the societies, the press, the brewers, the churches, the monuments, the intrigues of 1914–17 — and everything that was renamed, banned or seized after April 1917. In the setting none of that erasure happened; the pre-1917 record is what a victorious Germany's Chicago looks like in the 1930s.
  • Date: 2026-09-26
  • Model: Claude Fable 5.1 (research subagent)
  • Cross-references: R04 (Germany at the 1893 and 1933 fairs; the Glory of Germania tiles; the Bismarck renamings), R10 (Teutonia, the Bund, Camp Hindenburg, Clan na Gael and the Welland Canal 1900, Pfaus, Kappe, Kaltschmidt's Detroit ring), R15 (the German club directory of 1935, the Hindenburg memorial of 1934, the Bismarck Gardens/Hotel/Germania Club renamings, "Kaiser Bill" Thompson, German in the schools 1929). Overview

54 entries. German Chicago before 1917 was not a minority but the city's largest nationality — "one-third of Chicago's population," Mayor Harrison said at the Goethe unveiling in June 1914 — with two dailies, its own hospital, a Sullivan skyscraper built for German opera (with the Imperial consulate upstairs), Turner halls, a governor of Illinois, a mayor, three of the ten richest men in town (brewers), and a consulate that had been handing out the Kaiser's decorations and delivering the Kaiser's gifts since at least 1876. It paraded the Franco-Prussian victory in 1871, put Schiller, Humboldt, Reuter, Beethoven and Goethe in the parks, welcomed the Kaiser's brother with 3,000 torches in 1902, and kept the Kaiser's birthday every 27 January with the Chicago Symphony playing. Between August 1914 and April 1917 it registered 4,000 reservists in five days, filled the Bush Temple and Medinah Temple for the arms embargo and for Dernburg, hammered $1,800 of dimes into a wooden Hindenburg, and — through Consul von Reiswitz — sent three Chicago Germans to Siam to train Indian revolutionaries (the jeweller among them came out of Leavenworth as America's first fingerprint-forgery expert). Then in eighteen months the whole thing was renamed, registered, interned, seized and silenced, by a volunteer spy league invented by a Chicago advertising man. Every entry ends with what survives into the setting's 1930s, where none of that erasure happened — the strongest single finding is how continuous the cast is: Rubens, Girten, Kruetgen, Scherger and the consulate's card index run unbroken from 1902 to 1934 in the record, and would run prouder still in a Chicago whose old country won.

1. The Kaiser's own days in Chicago

Prince Henry of Prussia comes to Chicago — Monday 3 March 1902 (and Milwaukee the next afternoon)

  • Where: Union Depot (arrival 6:30 p.m.) → the Auditorium Hotel (Michigan Ave. & Congress St.) → the First Regiment Armory (Michigan Ave. & 16th St. — address from general knowledge, UNVERIFIED) → back to the Auditorium for banquet and ball; departed 2:00 p.m. Tuesday 4 March for Milwaukee (see §10)
  • What happened: Kaiser Wilhelm II sent his brother, Admiral Prince Heinrich, on a goodwill tour of the United States in February–March 1902 (Freedom of the City of New York 25 Feb; Harvard LL.D. 6 March; Philadelphia 10 March), run on a special train by the Pennsylvania Railroad, whose printed itinerary survives. Chicago's turn came on the evening of Monday 3 March 1902 (the Abendpost of 4 March reports "last night"): arrival at Union Depot 6:30 p.m., "reception by the Mayor, the German Consul, and a Reception Committee," a drive with military escort to the Auditorium, dinner at 7, the choral festival at the Armory at 9, the ball at 10:30 with supper after. Mayor Carter Harrison Jr., A. J. Eddy, Consul Dr. Walther Wever and the council's reception committee boarded the Prince's private car; Harrison said "I bid you Chicago's welcome!" and the Prince said "Thank you." The streets from the depot to the Auditorium were packed; about 3,000 members of the German clubs marched with torches behind an honorary cavalry squadron and several bands. At the First Regiment Armory the Prince "invoked three cheers for his Imperial brother," Thies J. Lefens gave the German-language address, and the German and American singing societies sang. A banquet for 160 followed at the Auditorium Hotel with Harrison as toastmaster and the Prince replying "in English"; the night ended with a festival ball — "the most elegant affair this city had ever seen" — in which the Prince led the grand march with Mrs. Carter Harrison. The Abendpost (3 March) framed the whole tour as the Kaiser's answer to "British incitements" in the American press; Chicago's Germans, who had held a protest meeting against that press, claimed they "started the ball rolling" for the welcome.
  • The odd detail: the Kaiser's brother opened the ball with the mayor's wife, and the German clubs marched by torchlight through the Loop — in 1902 that was civic pride, not a security problem.
  • People: Prince Heinrich of Prussia (1862–1929) — the Kaiser's brother, admiral; Carter Harrison Jr. — mayor; Dr. Walther Wever — Imperial German consul at Chicago; Thies J. Lefens — German-language orator, banker; A. J. Eddy — reception committee (the Eddys were decorated for it, see next entry)
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_11_0942 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_11_0944 ; https://archive.org/stream/tourhisroyalhig00railgoog/tourhisroyalhig00railgoog_djvu.txt (the 1902 Pennsylvania Railroad itinerary book) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Henry_of_Prussia_(1862%E2%80%931929)
  • Confidence: High for the Chicago programme (contemporary German press plus the printed itinerary); the Armory address is general knowledge.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. What survives into the setting's 1930s: in our history this was the last Hohenzollern welcomed in Chicago; in the setting it is the first of a series — a Chicago that never broke with Berlin would plausibly have hosted a Crown Prince or the Kaiser's sons in the 1920s (proposal). The 1902 guest list is still alive and socially dominant in 1936.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the 160 at the banquet and the couples in the grand march are a ready-made society register. One of the women who danced that night is photographed at a Germania Club ball in 1936 and has not aged. Section 13 has the 1902 seating plan.

The Kaiser's thanks — Red Eagles and Crown Orders handed out at the consulate in the Schiller Building — 29 August 1902

  • Where: the Imperial German Consulate, Schiller Building, 64 W. Randolph St. (Adler & Sullivan's 1891–92 tower, built for the German Opera House Company — see §4)
  • What happened: Acting Consul Dr. Zoepffel distributed, "by the wish of his majesty, the German Emperor," the decorations Prince Henry had ordered for those who had served his Chicago visit. Order of the Red Eagle, 3rd class: Arthur T. Eddy and Augustus N. Eddy. Order of the Crown, 3rd class: Consul Wever, Wilhelm Vocke, Harry Rubens. Crown, 4th class: the consulate secretaries Reichel, Schinger and Noculak; C. F. L. Gauss; Wilhelm Schmidt. Gold pins to the two coachmen, John Mooney and Robert Cook; "artistic pins" to Police Chief O'Neill, architect James Gamble Rogers, the painter Heinz Meixner, several aldermen, Frank Amberg and Gustav Ehrhorn; cigarette cases with the donor's name in diamonds to Frank H. Jones and E. G. Halle; signed photographs of the Prince to Mayor Harrison, Governor Richard Yates and seventeen others ⟨fixed: V_R23⟩; large signed portraits to the Germania Club of Chicago and to the Milwaukee Art Institute. A fortnight earlier (11 Aug) the Abendpost had reported that Dr. G. A. Zimmermann, former superintendent of German instruction in the Chicago public schools, was to get the Red Eagle for "the cultivation of the German language and the German culture" — and asked, sharply, whether Consul Wever had toured Chicago schools with Zimmermann without permission: "the consul of a foreign land is not anybody"; Superintendent Cooley said he had no report of it and the paper left the question open.
  • The odd detail: the Kaiser decorated Chicago's Irish police chief (O'Neill — the Francis O'Neill who collected Irish folk music, if the identification is right: he was chief 1901–05) and two coachmen; and a German paper publicly scolded the German consul for meddling in the public schools.
  • People: Dr. Zoepffel — acting consul, Aug 1902; Dr. Walther Wever — consul; Wilhelm Vocke — lawyer, German community leader; Harry Rubens — lawyer, Germania Club figure; Dr. G. A. Zimmermann — ex-superintendent of German instruction; Edwin G. Cooley — schools superintendent; James Gamble Rogers — architect
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_11_0938 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_11_0940
  • Confidence: High (names as printed); the O'Neill identification is Medium.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Survives: the Red Eagle and Crown Order ribbons on Chicago lapels were put away in 1917 in our history; in the setting they are worn to the Germania Club in 1936, and thirty more years of Berlin decorations have been handed out from the consulate — a visible caste of "the Kaiser's friends" in Chicago society.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a consular decoration list is a list of assets. Section 13 keeps a card index of every Chicagoan who has accepted an Order of the Crown; the Golden Circle reads the same list as a shopping list.

The Kaiser's birthday at the Germania Club — 27 January 1915 (celebrated 27/28 Jan)

  • Where: Germania Club, 108 W. Germania Place (Clark St. & Germania Pl.), Near North Side
  • What happened: With Germany five months into the war, "hundreds of German-Americans from various professions and social classes" met at the Germania Club for a "solemn gathering." Club president H. O. Lang opened; the main speaker was Prof. Eugen Kühnemann of the University of Breslau, then touring America for the German cause: "Never, in all the world's history, has a responsibility such as Kaiser Wilhelm II has to bear been placed on the shoulders of one man"; the war "has united the people." The Chicago Symphony Orchestra under Albert Ulrich played Weber's Jubilee Overture and the Radetzky March; the Germania Male Chorus led the hall in a Reformation hymn that "made a particularly deep impression." The banker Fritz von Frantzius presented a life-size portrait of the Kaiser in uniform, painted in Berlin ten years earlier by the Chicago artist Frank A. Werner. Mayor Carter Harrison declined to attend; his rival Robert M. Sweitzer (German-American, county clerk, running for mayor that spring) was there. The paper's line: "The Germans, in making the Kaiser's birthday a national holiday, do not honor a man; they honor themselves."
  • The odd detail: the city's symphony orchestra played the Kaiser's birthday party in wartime, and the mayor's absence was news.
  • People: H. O. Lang — Germania Club president 1915; Prof. Eugen Kühnemann — Breslau Germanist, Berlin's cultural envoy; Albert Ulrich — CSO conductor for the evening; Fritz von Frantzius — banker (see §8); Frank A. Werner — portraitist; Robert M. Sweitzer — county clerk, mayoral candidate 1915
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_10_1147
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Survives: in the setting 27 January is still kept in Chicago every year through the 1930s — and after 1918 it is the birthday of the man who won. The CSO still plays it; the mayor now attends.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Werner portrait hangs in the Germania Club through the 1930s. Something is painted under it. The night of the Kaiser's birthday, when the club is full and the lights are low for the tableau, is when the vampire "conspiring in high society" holds its own court upstairs.

The Kaiser's birthday, 1916 — two halls, and the police close the doors — 27 January 1916

  • Where: Germania Club (108 W. Germania Pl.) and the North Side Turnhall (Clark St. near Chicago Ave., UNVERIFIED address)
  • What happened: Two celebrations ran the same night. At the Germania Club, president Ernest J. Kruetgen affirmed that German-Americans were "good American citizens," and Prof. Kühnemann spoke again: "Here it is different. We assert our pride because we are of German origin, and are willing to make sacrifices in this new land." He asked the hall to fund the rebuilding of Ragnit, the East Prussian border town wrecked in the Russian invasion. Across the river the Teutonia — here the German-Austro-Hungarian Alliance, not the 1924 Nazi club of the same name (see R10) — filled the North Side Turnhall so far past capacity that police shut the entrance under the fire code; inside: Ballmann's orchestra, a comedy about army life, and a tableau in honour of the Emperor.
  • The odd detail: a Chicago police fire-code closure of a Kaiser's-birthday party, eighteen months before Chicago was told the Kaiser was the enemy.
  • People: Ernest J. Kruetgen — Germania Club president (later a Chicago postmaster and the 1933 World's Fair German-group chair, see R15); Eugen Kühnemann — speaker; Ballmann — bandleader
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_5_0617
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — the 1916 birthday is the last one before the setting's break; in the setting the next one (Jan 1917) is still celebrated, and 1919's is a victory party. Kruetgen's career (postmaster, fair chairman) holds either way.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Ragnit reconstruction" is the model for how the setting's consulate moves money: an appeal read from the Germania Club stage, collected in the Turnhall, wired through a LaSalle Street bank — and some of it never reaching East Prussia.

The German Peace Festival — the city's Germans parade the Franco-Prussian victory — 29 May 1871 (living memory for the old; the founding myth for the clubs)

  • Where: planning at Concordia Hall (1 May 1871); parade through the city, 29 May 1871
  • What happened: After Sedan (2 Sept 1870) and the peace of Frankfurt (May 1871), Chicago's Germans organised a Friedensfest — a peace festival — for Monday 29 May 1871. A meeting at Concordia Hall on 1 May chose A. Schoninger to lead it; the School Board was asked to close the schools for it (17 May); Dr. Chronik moved that the clergy "preach a sermon" for it (25 May); the Swiss Grütli Society voted to march. The Illinois Staats-Zeitung had greeted Sedan as "the most glorious feat of arms recorded in modern history" (8 Sept 1870), and in January 1871 the German community held a mass meeting against the "dirty arms trade" — American arms sales to France. Five months after the festival the city burned (8–10 Oct 1871). The festival was remembered: a 25-year memorial in May 1896, and a historical tableau "from the wonderful peace festival in Chicago in the Spring of 1871" at German Day, October 1907.
  • The odd detail: Chicago's Germans lobbied to shut the public schools for a foreign victory parade — and the Chicago Germans' first mass protest against American arms sales to a belligerent was 1871, not 1914.
  • People: A. Schoninger — festival president; Dr. Chronik — proposer of the sermons
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_10_0949 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_8_0257 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_8_0254 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_5_1370 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_10_0319 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_10_0808 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_7_1520
  • Confidence: High for the items as printed (details of the parade itself not read — see Leads).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Survives: Sedan Day (2 Sept) and the 1871 memory were dropped in 1917; in the setting, a German Chicago that celebrated 1871 for 45 years simply adds a second victory day after 1918 — and the "arms trade" protest of 1871 is the template for the 1914 one, which in the setting won.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): two victory festivals fifty years apart, same club banners. The Kriegerverein's oldest members marched in both. What the Germans carried at the head of the 1871 parade (a lead to chase) is carried again in the 1930s.

The Emperor's gift to Chicago's German veterans, and the Kriegerverein — 1874 / 4 December 1876 / 1904 / 1914

  • Where: North Side halls; the veterans' plots at Graceland and Waldheim (UNVERIFIED)
  • What happened: The Deutscher Kriegerverein (German Veterans' Society) of Chicago — men who had fought for Prussia and the German states in 1864, 1866 and 1870–71 — was founded in 1874 (its "thirtieth anniversary" jubilee was reported 18 July 1904). In 1876 the society, "lacking funds for a flag," wrote to Kaiser Wilhelm I and asked for one. About two months later a package reached the German consul in Chicago, H. Clausenius: a banner of "heavily corded white silk, artistically embroidered with a wreath, in the center of which is the German eagle," with the Emperor's name and the society's. Consul Clausenius presented it to president Winckler at the North Side Turner Hall before a large crowd after "a grand vocal and instrumental concert" (the Tribune reported it 4 Dec 1876); Winckler "spoke very feelingly and referred to the sympathy of the Germans in this country with their countrymen," and the door-money went to the society. In May 1895 the society was recruiting its "old and grey" veterans for a cut-price excursion to march in Berlin's Sedan Day parade (2 Sept). The society marched in the city's mourning for President Garfield (24–26 Sept 1881). On 31 July 1914 its president Joseph Schlenker spoke at the "mass demonstration by local Germans" as the war began; on 3 Aug 1914 it voted $250 toward the reservists. It still existed in 1935, alongside a Kriegerbund and a Society of German World War Veterans, in the Abendpost's club directory (see R15), and stood colour guard at the 1934 Hindenburg memorial.
  • The odd detail: a Chicago veterans' club wrote to the German Emperor for a flag and got one, delivered through the consulate — a direct line from the Berlin palace to a North Side Turner hall, forty years before anyone called it foreign influence.
  • People: H. Clausenius — Imperial German consul at Chicago, 1876; Winckler — Kriegerverein president 1876; Joseph Schlenker — president 1914; Heinrich Michelsen — society officer (1926 funeral)
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_11_1032 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_10_0764 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_7_1582 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_5_1264 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_5_1242 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_10_0903
  • Confidence: High for the flag and the 1895 excursion (items read); Medium for the founding year (inferred from the 1904 "thirtieth anniversary").
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Survives: in the setting the Kriegerverein is joined after 1918 by a much larger victors' veterans' society of 1914–18 men who emigrated to Chicago in the 1920s, all of them still in touch with a Berlin that won — the Kyffhäuser-style league R10 proposes.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Emperor's 1876 gift is still in the society's hall in 1936. It is not a flag.

2. The consulate and the intrigues of 1914–17

The consulate on the night the war came — 1–6 August 1914

  • Where: the Imperial German Consulate-General, Chicago (in 1902 it was in the Schiller Building, 64 W. Randolph; its 1914 address not confirmed this session); the Austro-Hungarian consulate; the German neighbourhoods of the North Side
  • What happened: On the night of 1–2 August 1914 the consulate had still had no official word of Germany's declaration of war on Russia; the Consul-General, Geissler, was away on leave and Vice-Consul Baron Kurt von Reiswitz was in charge. He told the Sonntagpost that "as soon as he receives official notice of the declaration of war, he will issue the call to service." Reservists gathered in halls across German Chicago to discuss "ways and means of getting back to Europe," some proposing to go by way of Holland because "German reservists will very likely be halted by the English or French." On 3 Aug the Abendpost reckoned fewer than 1,000 men in the city actually liable to serve; Reiswitz said he regretted missing his own regiment. By 6 Aug the Tribune reported about 4,000 men registered at the German and Austrian consulates together — and that both consuls, Reiswitz and the Austrian Hugo Sylvestin, had given up: Reiswitz "discourag[ed] German reservists from making futile efforts to reach their native country" since the British would stop the ships. Reiswitz remained in charge of the Chicago consulate through the neutrality years and was named in the 1917 Chicago "Hindu conspiracy" indictment (see next entries).
  • The odd detail: four thousand Chicagoans signed up at the German and Austro-Hungarian consulates together to fight for the Kaiser and the Emperor-King in five days, and were sent home by their own consuls because the Royal Navy was in the way ⟨fixed: V_R23⟩.
  • People: Baron Kurt von Reiswitz — vice-consul, then consul, Chicago, 1914–17; Geissler — consul-general, on leave Aug 1914; Hugo Sylvestin — Austro-Hungarian consul
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_5_1248 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_11_0900 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_5_1242
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Survives: the 4,000 who registered are the setting's core "Kaiser's men" in Chicago — in the setting, some got through via Holland and came back in 1919 as veterans of the winning side; the consulate's reservist register of August 1914 is, in the 1930s, still the best list of who in Chicago would answer a call from Berlin.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Section 13 has a photostat of the 1914 reservist register. So does the Golden Circle. Names on it keep turning up at the scenes of things.

The Bush Temple mass meeting against the "dirty arms trade" — 1 December 1914

  • Where: Bush Temple of Music, N. Clark St. & W. Chicago Ave. (NE corner — general knowledge, UNVERIFIED)
  • What happened: Four months into the war, Chicago's German and Irish organisations packed the Bush Temple "before 7:30 p.m." — police closed the doors and an overflow crowd stood outside — to demand that America stop selling munitions to the Allies. The chairman was an Irishman, James T. Clarke, who praised the Germans for keeping America out of Britain's arms; Judge Michael F. Girten said "If we stop selling munitions, the battles will soon stop also"; Congressman Henry Vollmer of Iowa denounced Allied propaganda and called for an export ban; John S. Leahy of St. Louis quoted Jefferson's "peace, commerce, and friendship with all nations"; the veteran Missouri congressman Richard Bartholdt — author of the arms-embargo bills in Congress — got "prolonged applause"; Judge Joseph Ryan read the resolutions. German male choruses sang; telegrams from U.S. senators were read. The Denní Hlasatel (13 June 1915) later described what the Czechs saw across the table: Germans "taxed ten per cent of their wages" for the cause, 1,200 German periodicals in America, Count Dernburg's propaganda bureau, wireless at Sayville, passport frauds — and "the German Embassy has been keeping an exact directory of all German nationals, their businesses, their financial status."
  • The odd detail: the German-Irish alliance was already running Chicago's anti-war mass meetings in 1914 under an Irish chairman; the Czechs' complaint was not that the Germans were disloyal but that they were better organised.
  • People: James T. Clarke — chairman, Irish; Judge Michael F. Girten — Chicago judge; Henry Vollmer — congressman, Davenport, Iowa; Richard Bartholdt — congressman, St. Louis, German-born, arms-embargo sponsor; John S. Leahy — St. Louis lawyer; Judge Joseph Ryan
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_5_0897 ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418478_4_0320
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — this is the movement that lost in our history and, in the setting, effectively won (America never went in). Survives: the German-Irish embargo coalition of 1914 is, in the 1930s, the setting's most experienced political machine in Chicago — its judges, congressmen and choruses twenty years older and vindicated; "peace, commerce and friendship with all nations" is the line the German consulate's American friends still quote.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Bush Temple meeting's resolutions committee is the seed of a standing German-Irish "Neutrality League" in the setting — the respectable front through which Berlin, "patron of other people's revolutions," funds Irish and Mexican causes in Chicago. The Golden Circle wants its mailing list.

The Wooden Knight — Chicago's Hindenburg nail-figure, and where it ended up — autumn 1914 to 1917

  • Where: a charity bazaar; Bismarck Gardens (Grace & Halsted); the Abendpost office; finally the Turnlager (Turner camp) at Cary, Illinois
  • What happened: German cities in 1914–16 raised war-relief money with Nagelfiguren — big wooden statues into which donors hammered nails at so much a nail until the figure was "iron." Chicago's Germans built one: a large wooden warrior, "probably Hindenburg," exhibited in autumn 1914 at a charity bazaar and at Bismarck Gardens, then at the Abendpost office and elsewhere. Nails cost ten cents each; the object was "to stud the statue with large-headed nails … until it became an Iron Knight." By 21 January 1916 the nails had raised $1,800.40. The plan was to send the finished knight to Berlin or Vienna by public vote; America's entry into the war made that impossible and the knight "became a local trip to the Turnlager at Cary, Illinois," where — per the WPA translator's later note appended to the 1916 item, not the 1916 paper itself — it "reposes unperturbed and serene" ⟨fixed: V_R23⟩.
  • The odd detail: an iron-studded Hindenburg, hammered full of Chicago dimes, standing in a Turner summer camp in McHenry County.
  • People: the sculptor — unnamed in the source
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_9_0670
  • Confidence: High for what the paper said; the knight's fate after 1917 is the paper's own account.

3. The German-American National Alliance and the politics of the hyphen

The German-American National Alliance — the hyphen's lobby — 6 October 1901 to 1918

  • Where: founded Philadelphia; Chicago branch meetings at the Northside Turner Hall, the Auditorium, the Bush Temple
  • What happened: Founded 6 Oct 1901 in Philadelphia with Dr. Charles J. 10. Milwaukee and Cincinnati, briefly

Prince Henry in Milwaukee — six hours, the Pfister, the united singers and the German Theatre — Tuesday 4 March 1902

  • Where: the station; the Exposition Hall; Hotel Pfister; the (Pabst) German Theatre
  • What happened: From Chicago the special train reached Milwaukee at 4:00 p.m. on 4 March 1902: "reception by the Mayor at the station. Military escort to the Exposition Hall. Greeting by the United Singing Clubs"; dinner at the Hotel Pfister; "if time allows, gala representation at the German Theatre"; departure 10:00 p.m. The itinerary book called Milwaukee (285,315 people) a city whose "principal industry" was "brewing of beer." In August 1902 the Prince sent the Milwaukee Art Institute a large signed portrait, like the Germania Club's.
  • The odd detail: the Kaiser's brother got six hours in the most German city in America, and the programme's only doubt was whether there would be time for the German Theatre.
  • People: David S. Rose — mayor of Milwaukee 1898–1906 (general knowledge, UNVERIFIED); Frederick Pabst — the theatre's owner
  • Sources: https://archive.org/stream/tourhisroyalhig00railgoog/tourhisroyalhig00railgoog_djvu.txt ; https://flps.newberry.org/id/5418474_11_0938 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pabst_Theater
  • Confidence: High for the itinerary (the printed plan; the actual day not separately confirmed).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Survives: as for Chicago — the first, not the last, Hohenzollern in Milwaukee.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): what the United Singing Clubs sang at the Exposition Hall was chosen by the Kaiser's chamberlain in advance; the same programme is sung at Milwaukee's Kaiser's-birthday every year after, and one verse is not in any hymnal.

Deutsch-Athen — Milwaukee's German half, and what 1918 did to it: the Deutscher Club, the Germania statue, the Pabst Theater, the Bennett Law

  • Where: the Deutscher Club in Alexander Mitchell's mansion, 900 W. Wisconsin Ave. (opposite the courthouse); the Germania Building, 135 W. Wells St.; the Pabst Theater, 144 E. Wells St.
  • What happened: By the twentieth century half of Milwaukee's people claimed German ancestry, the city was "Deutsch-Athen," and its German press (Milwaukee's Germania claimed the largest German circulation in America in 1909) was housed in George Brumder's Germania Building (1896; Schnetzky & Liebert; eight storeys, 117,752 sq ft, the largest office building in the city, copper domes, and a three-ton bronze Germania over the door). The Deutscher Club (1891) had the banker Alexander Mitchell's mansion; the Pabst Theater (1895, Otto Strack, after the 1895 fire of Pabst's Neues Deutsches Stadt-Theater) "exclusively booked German language performances until 1918." Wisconsin's 1889 Bennett Law (English in all schools) had already cost the Republicans the state in 1890 — Archbishop Katzer and the Missouri and Wisconsin Synods against it; the Catholic Citizen called it "all the sectarian, bigoted, fanatical and crazy impurities" of the party. In 1918: the Deutscher Club became the Wisconsin Club; the Germania Building became the Brumder Building and its statue was "removed discreetly in the night" — stored for a while by the sculptor Cyril Colnik, then lost: one theory says it was melted for scrap in World War II, another that it went to the Smithsonian ⟨fixed: V_R23⟩; nobody knows; the Pabst Theater began booking English acts "to remain viable." (The name Germania returned to the building only in 1981.)
  • The odd detail: a three-ton Germania vanished from a Milwaukee doorway one night in 1918 and has never been found.
  • People: George Brumder — publisher; Frederick Pabst — brewer; Otto Strack — architect; Alexander Mitchell — banker, railroad president (d. 1887); Frederick Katzer — archbishop
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germania_Building ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Mitchell_(Wisconsin_politician) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pabst_Theater ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bennett_Law ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Milwaukee ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language_in_the_United_States
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in the setting the Deutscher Club is still the Deutscher Club, Germania still stands over Wells Street, and the Pabst is still a German-language house in the 1930s, in a city that is half German and whose old country won.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Germania statue that was never taken down: in the setting she is still there — and R22's lake folklore says the bronze figure faces the lake and turns her head on the night of the Schiller wreck's anniversary (legend, proposal).

Victor Berger — Milwaukee's Socialist, twenty years from Judge Landis, and the House that would not seat him — 1910–1929

  • Where: Milwaukee; Judge Kenesaw Mountain Landis's federal court, Chicago (Feb 1919)
  • What happened: Austrian-born (Nieder-Rehbach, 1860; in Milwaukee from 1881), editor of Vorwärts! and the Milwaukee Leader, founder of the Social Democratic Party (1898) and the Socialist Party (1901), Berger was the first Socialist in Congress (1911–13). Indicted under the Espionage Act for his anti-war paper, he was convicted on 20 Feb 1919 and sentenced by Landis in Chicago to twenty years; Milwaukee re-elected him anyway (1918), the House refused to seat him twice (Nov 1919, Jan 1920), the Supreme Court reversed the conviction on 31 Jan 1921 (Landis should have recused), and he served 1923–29 and chaired the party (1926). He was killed by a streetcar in Milwaukee on 7 Aug 1929. Milwaukee's Socialist mayors — Emil Seidel (1910–12) and Daniel Hoan (1916–40) — ran "sewer socialism" through the whole of the setting's decade.
  • The odd detail: the judge who gave a German-American congressman twenty years for opposing the war was the same Landis who, two years later, became the first Commissioner of Baseball.
  • People: Victor L. Berger (1860–1929); Kenesaw Mountain Landis; Daniel Hoan — mayor 1916–40
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_L._Berger ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Milwaukee
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — no Espionage Act prosecution, no exclusion; Berger sits in Congress through the 1920s unbroken and still dies under a streetcar in 1929 (holds). Hoan's Milwaukee holds. Note for the setting: the BRIEF says "no Soviet Union, no Red Scare" — Milwaukee's German socialism is then a respectable, un-persecuted municipal tradition with a Berlin that (in the setting) never had a revolution either; how Berlin's SPD fared under a victorious Kaiser is a question for the owner.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a German-language socialist daily in Milwaukee that nobody ever suppressed is the one paper in the Midwest that prints what the consulate does not want printed.

Milwaukee's brewers — Pabst, Schlitz, Blatz, Miller: the money behind Deutsch-Athen — 1855–1933

  • Where: Milwaukee; Schlitz's 57 Chicago tied houses
  • What happened: Pabst (Frederick Pabst, a lake-steamer captain who married into the Best family and bought half the brewery in 1863) was "the nation's largest brewer" by 1874 and in 1893 the first American brewer to sell over a million barrels in a year [UNVERIFIED — V_R23]; in Prohibition it made 8 million pounds of Pabst-ett cheese. Schlitz (Joseph Schlitz took August Krug's brewery in 1856, married his widow, drowned in the Schiller in 1875 — 335 dead; the Uihleins took over) was America's largest brewer in 1902, pioneered brown bottles in 1912, built the Schlitz Palm Garden and Schlitz Park in Milwaukee and tied houses across Chicago. Blatz (Valentin Blatz, 1850–52, merged with Johann Braun's City Brewery; 16,000 barrels by 1868) was the first Milwaukee brewer to bottle and ship nationally (1875), sold part to an English syndicate in 1891, and was the city's third brewer in the 1900s. Miller — Frederick Miller came from Hohenzollern in 1854 with "a unique brewer's yeast" and bought the little Plank Road Brewery in 1855 for $2,300; his family held it until 1966 — was the fourth. The political weight is in §3 above: the USBA, the Alliance, the anti-Prohibition fight.
  • The odd detail: Milwaukee's second brewer died on a ship named Schiller; Chicago's German consulate sat in a building named Schiller; the Schwabenverein's first statue was Schiller. The name is a thread through the whole German Midwest.
  • People: Frederick Pabst (1836–1904); Joseph Schlitz (1831–1875); the Uihlein brothers; Valentin Blatz (1826–1894); Frederick Miller (1824–1888)
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pabst_Brewing_Company ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Schlitz_Brewing_Company ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentin_Blatz_Brewing_Company ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_Brewing_Company
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — Prohibition's calendar; BENDS in standing (no 1918 disgrace). Survives: four German brewing dynasties, un-humiliated, back in business Dec 1933.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Schlitz Palm Garden — a Milwaukee winter garden under glass — is where the setting's Milwaukee consul receives; the Uihleins' Chicago agent (Uihlein, §5) links the two cities' German money.

Cincinnati — Over-the-Rhine, the interned conductor, the library's purge, and German Street becoming English Street — 1917–18

  • Where: Over-the-Rhine (north of the Miami & Erie Canal — "the Rhine"); Music Hall; the Public Library; the renamed streets
  • What happened: Cincinnati's Germans settled Over-the-Rhine from the 1840s (Christian Moerlein's brewery, 1853, became the city's largest); the city's Turnverein of 1848 is the oldest in America (general knowledge, UNVERIFIED here), and Cincinnati stood with Chicago and New York as a brewing capital. In 1917–18, with 25,000 Cincinnatians in uniform: the Cincinnati Symphony's Austrian conductor Dr. Ernst Kunwald (1912–17; he had given America its first Mahler Third) was arrested by federal marshals on 8 Dec 1917, the day after the declaration of war on Austria-Hungary, after the Daughters of the American Revolution demanded he be kept off the podium; interned without trial at Fort Oglethorpe from 12 Jan 1918 (Karl Muck of the Boston Symphony joined him there in March), the evidence including that he conducted European classics and was loyal to his emperor; deported in 1919; he ended in Königsberg and Berlin and died in Vienna in 1939. The concertmaster Emil Heerman was arrested and released into the Conservatory's custody; "the city's library removed pro-German books and the public schools discontinued German language classes"; and the council renamed the streets — German Street became English Street, Berlin Street became Woodward (Wikipedia's Anti-German sentiment; the fuller list — Bremen to Republic, Hamburg to Stonewall, and the rest — is general knowledge, UNVERIFIED).
  • The odd detail: a city that named its canal "the Rhine" renamed German Street "English Street" and locked up its orchestra's conductor.
  • People: Ernst Kunwald — conductor 1912–17; Emil Heerman — concertmaster; Christian Moerlein — brewer
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Cincinnati ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-German_sentiment ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over-the-Rhine
  • Confidence: Medium-High (the street renamings beyond the two named are unverified).
  • Divergence check: BENDS entirely — Over-the-Rhine keeps its German Street, its German schools, its conductor and its books. Survives: the whole quarter as the Ohio River's German capital in the 1930s, brewing again from 1933.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): in the setting the Cincinnati library never purged its German shelves — which means the occult German titles the Chicago library never had are still on the Ohio.
