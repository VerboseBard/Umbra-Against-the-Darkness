R18 — The investigator's toolkit: how things actually worked in 1930s Chicago, with real numbers

  • Lane: R18 — procedures and prices for investigators: death and the law, police and courts, federal agencies, private eyes, records, communication and travel, prices and wages, gear.
  • Date: 2026-09-26
  • Model: Opus 5.5
  • Format note: many entries here are "how it worked" entries rather than events; the What happened field carries "How it worked".

Overview: 52 entries. The richest single source was the 1929 Illinois Crime Survey (public domain on archive.org, read through its search-inside): it gives the working machinery a GM needs — how a Cook County inquest ran (six men at $1 a hearing, 447 inquests in January 1928, professional jurors called "Fridays"), how autopsies were done (and how badly), how the homicide squad and its "chaser" worked, the "fixer", the bondsmen, habeas-corpus releases, suburban gun permits, and the bankers' 3,200 shotgun deputies. The 1939 WPA Illinois guide and the 1933 fair's Official Guide Book supplied hard numbers for getting around and staying over (streetcar 8¢, "L" 12¢, cab 20¢ a mile; first-class hotel $1.50–$5, rooming house $1; hotel meal 50¢–$1). The federal frame is here too — agents unable to carry guns or arrest until mid-1934, the kidnapping law's presumption clock (seven days from May 1934 — the 24-hour rule is a 1956 amendment ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩), the Prohibition Bureau's road to the Alcohol Tax Unit, the Narcotics Bureau, and the real Secret Service chief who feuded with J. Edgar Hoover. Gear: the Colt Monitor (the Treasury owned 11 of ~125), the Chicago-made Dunrite vest that drove the .357 Magnum, the first $30 Motorola, flash powder to flashbulbs, Kodachrome by mail, and the fact that magnetic wire recorders were made only in tiny numbers (American Telegraphone Co.) and barely used in 1930s America — the practical machine's inventor was a Chicago student ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩. Gaps: the Chicago police force's size and pay, pistol prices, telegram rates and several wages were NOT confirmed — Chronicling America and Google Books rate-limited the session and the Encyclopedia of Chicago did not resolve; all are listed under Leads.

1. Death and the law — coroner, inquest, morgue, autopsy

Source note for this section: the Illinois Crime Survey (Illinois Association for Criminal Justice, 1929; the Chicago data mostly 1926–28), full scan at https://archive.org/details/illinoiscrimesur00illi — page numbers below are the book's pages, read via the archive's search-inside. The coroners' names and terms (Wolff, Bundesen, Walsh) are in R13 and R09; not repeated here.

How a Cook County inquest actually ran — the coroner's jury — 1926–1928 (the system unchanged into the 1930s)

  • Where: Cook County — inquests held by deputy coroners wherever the body was (the Survey's figures are county-wide).
  • What happened (how it worked): Illinois law required the coroner to hold an inquest on every sudden or violent death and on every felonious homicide, before a jury of "six good and lawful men of the neighborhood". In practice the Survey found the phrase read as "such men as the deputy coroners may select, so long as they reside in Cook County". The jurors were paid one dollar each per hearing. In January 1928 thirteen deputy coroners held 447 inquests in the county and used 918 jurors in all. The State's Attorney of Cook County sent no one to inquests "except at an occasional inquest of unusual publicity value". The verdict could find a death accidental, leave it "open" (person or persons unknown), or order a named person held to the grand jury — for manslaughter or murder.
  • The odd detail: 447 inquests in one winter month — fifteen dead a day passing before six men paid a dollar apiece.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/illinoiscrimesur00illi (pp. 309, 633–634, 673)
  • Confidence: High (primary survey, figures quoted). The exact verdict wording used in the 1930s is not quoted in what I read — "open verdict" is the Survey's own phrase.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The inquest is the first public document on any death the party touches. A GM can hand players the transcript: six names, a deputy coroner, a verdict — and one juror who sat on every inquest the thing ever caused.

The "Fridays" — Chicago's professional coroner's jurors — January 1928

  • Where: Cook County inquest rooms and undertaking parlours (inquest locations not itemised in what I read).
  • What happened (how it worked): The Survey found the deputy coroners used "regulars, or 'Fridays' as they are termed" — the same men, paid a dollar a hearing, seated again and again. In January 1928 one man sat as a coroner's juror 60 to 69 times in the month; others sat 40–49, 30–39 and 20–29 times. Jurors were swapped between hearings so freely that "frequently not one juror will remain sitting throughout all of the hearings in one inquest." The Survey recommended picking jurors case by case from "the neighborhood where the body is found".
  • The odd detail: a man who sat on some sixty inquests in thirty-one days — quite possibly the most experienced death-panel juror in America, working for a dollar a sitting.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/illinoiscrimesur00illi (pp. 634, 676)
  • Confidence: High on the figures. The nickname's origin (Robinson Crusoe's "Man Friday"?) is my guess — UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A "Friday" has looked at more bodies than any policeman in the county. He is the witness nobody thinks to ask: have you seen one like this before? He has — three, in 1931.

The coroner's physician and the autopsy — 1889 to 1928

  • Where: Cook County Hospital (post-mortems) and the coroner's office.
  • What happened (how it worked): The coroner decided which bodies were opened; the cutting was done by a coroner's physician, a post created in 1889 under Coroner Henry L. Hertz — "a pathologist appointed with the rank of deputy coroner" (before 1889 the county physician did it). By 1919 there were six. Autopsies at County Hospital averaged 60 to 80 a month. Under Coroner Oscar Wolff (1922–28) Dr. E. R. LeCount, "a pathologist of international reputation", was discharged after thirteen years; by June 1928 "no member of the staff of coroner's physicians holds a position as hospital pathologist." Dr. Hatton's review of their reports found external and internal findings run together so "it is difficult to tell whether or not the body was opened."
  • The odd detail: the Survey's pathologists could not tell from Cook County autopsy reports whether the body had actually been cut open.
  • People: Henry L. Hertz — coroner, 1889; Dr. E. R. LeCount — county pathologist, fired in the 1920s; Oscar Wolff — coroner 1922–28 (see R13); Dr. Edward H. Hatton — author of the 1926 National Research Council (Committee on Medicolegal Problems) report on the Cook County coroner's office, which the Survey quotes ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/illinoiscrimesur00illi (pp. 413–420)
  • Confidence: High for the quoted findings. Who could demand an autopsy besides the coroner (family, State's Attorney) is not in what I read — UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (pre-1916 and domestic).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A report that does not say whether the body was opened is a gift: the party can find out it wasn't, and ask why the coroner's physician signed for organs he never weighed.

Death at the wheel: what an inquest's "held for murder" was worth — 1926–1927

  • Where: Cook County (inquest statistics).
  • What happened (how it worked): The Survey followed every motor-vehicle death inquest in 1926 and 1927 — 1,876 cases. The juries called 1,344 of them accidents and left 236 open with no prosecution; 330 drivers were arrested and all later exonerated; 203 were charged with manslaughter (179 prosecuted); 49 were ordered held for murder — and not one was convicted of murder.
  • The odd detail: a coroner's jury saying "murder" in Cook County carried a conviction rate of zero out of forty-nine.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/illinoiscrimesur00illi (p. 671)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A hit-and-run is the cheapest way to kill someone in Chicago and the law knows it. A cult that needs a death "by misadventure" drives a Packard.

The homicide squad, the "criminologist", and the "chaser" — late 1920s

  • Where: Detective Bureau, police headquarters (the 1928 building at 1121 S. State — see R09).
  • What happened (how it worked): "When 'homicide' is flashed through the police switchboard, it is relayed to the detective bureau, and the homicide squad, accompanied by the criminologist, is immediately dispatched to the scene." At the scene: "Measurements are made, the body examined, pictures taken, and, when possible, finger-prints are observed." If no arrest followed at once, the case was counted as uncleared against the district where the death occurred, and that district's commanding officer inherited it. "About three or four times a year a 'chaser' is sent out from headquarters to every station that has uncleared homicide cases." The Municipal Court's Harrison Street branch (625 S. Clark St) — which took every case arising in police districts 1 and 1A and from the Traffic and Detective Bureaus — disposed of 2,345 felony cases in 1927 ⟨fixed: V_R18 — the Survey attaches the figure to the branch court, not to the Detective Bureau⟩; Capt. John Stege (reinstated August 1928 and made Deputy Commissioner in charge of the Detective Bureau) had, as chief deputy of the Bureau under Chief Morgan Collins, compiled a list of about 18,000 criminals in four years.
  • The odd detail: an unsolved murder was an entry in a district captain's ledger, and headquarters sent a "chaser" round three or four times a year to nag him about it.
  • People: John Stege — Detective Bureau chief, deputy commissioner from Aug 1928.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/illinoiscrimesur00illi (pp. 631–632 and the Stege references)
  • Confidence: High for procedure as described c. 1928. After 1930 the radio Complaint Room (see R13) changed how the flash went out.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Uncleared deaths stay on a district's books. A police captain in a district with too many open files is a man who will take help from anyone — including Section 13, if it quietly closes them.

2. Police, prosecutors and courts

The "fixer" — how a case went away — described 1929

  • Where: Cook County — mostly at the preliminary hearing in the Municipal Court.
  • What happened (how it worked): The Survey defined him plainly: "The 'fixer' is just what the word indicates. As to qualifications, he has none, except that he may be a person of some small political influence." His work was done early: the underworld preferred to kill a case at the preliminary hearing, and a fixer who let a case get past that stage was counted a failure. Where that failed, fixers worked through intermediaries to have murder charges reduced to manslaughter. The Survey also names a First Ward Levee "overlord" who ran a new tenderloin district by "settling with the people down town."
  • The odd detail: the Survey's single-sentence job description — no qualifications but "some small political influence".
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/illinoiscrimesur00illi (pp. 343, 507, 888)
  • Confidence: High on the quotations; the mechanics beyond them are general.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The party will meet a fixer before they meet a judge. The occult version: the fixer's "small political influence" is a favour owed to something that does not vote.

Bail, bondsmen and the forfeit-as-continuance — 1918–1928

  • Where: The branch police courts of the Municipal Court; the Criminal Court.
  • What happened (how it worked): The Survey found each branch court run on "some sort of understanding among the attorneys, the lock-up keeper, the police, the bailiffs, and the professional bondsmen"; a new lawyer had to "be acceptable to the bondsmen who operated in that court." In Chicago a defendant could win a delay "by the simple expedient of failing to appear at the trial and forfeiting the bail bond" — and delay, the Survey showed, killed prosecutions. The court kept a list of 1,800–1,900 disqualified bondsmen — every surety with a forfeiture from 1918 to 24 December 1927. Named professional bondsmen included William Skidmore and Ike Roderick, both tied to gambling and to the Torrio–Capone organisation.
  • The odd detail: skipping your own trial was a standard legal tactic — you lost the bond, bought time, and the witnesses drifted away.
  • People: William "Billy" Skidmore — bondsman and gambling boss; Ike Roderick — bondsman.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/illinoiscrimesur00illi (pp. 252, 441, 445, 447, 891, 923)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Bail for a suspect the party caught is posted within the hour by a man none of them has seen before, from a bonding house that has been closed since 1927.

The Municipal Court of Chicago — 1906–1964

  • Where: Sat in City Hall from 1911 [UNVERIFIED — V_R18: the court left 116 S. Michigan Ave in 1911 (confirmed); the City Hall destination was not on any page read] (first housed at 116 S. Michigan Ave, 1906–11); branch courts in police stations; later branches at police headquarters (see R09's Central Police Station: Boys' Court, Speeders' Court, Domestic Relations).
  • What happened (how it worked): Created by an act signed 18 May 1905 and opened 3 December 1906, it replaced the city's justices of the peace: one chief justice and 27 associate judges, elected. It tried civil claims and all non-felony criminal cases, and in Chicago it held the preliminary hearings in felony cases — the stage at which (see "the fixer") most cases died. It was seen as the first court of its kind in America.
  • The odd detail: a whole city's petty crime, divorce-and-desertion, boys' cases and felony prelims ran through 28 elected judges — every one a party nominee.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_Court_of_Chicago ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipal_Courts_Building_(Chicago) ; https://archive.org/details/illinoiscrimesur00illi (preliminary hearings)
  • Confidence: High on structure. The court's Psychopathic Laboratory (tied to the eugenicist Harry H. Laughlin in the Wikipedia search result) was not read this session — see Leads.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A player arrested for breaking into a house will see a Municipal Court judge within a day. The GM decides whose nominee he is.

3. The federal presence

R02 covers the May–June 1934 crime bills and the Bureau's Chicago office (Bankers Building, 105 W. Adams); R01 covers Frank J. Wilson's Treasury Intelligence Unit and the Untouchables. These entries give the procedural frame an investigator needs.

What a federal agent could and could not do — before and after May–June 1934

  • Where: Nationwide; the Bureau's Chicago office under Melvin Purvis from 1932.
  • What happened (how it worked): Before 1934, Bureau of Investigation agents "did not have authority to carry firearms (although some agents reportedly did) and make arrests (they could make a 'citizen's arrest', then call a U.S. Marshal or local law officer)." After the Kansas City Massacre (17 June 1933) Congress gave the Bureau "statutory authority to carry guns and make arrests (in May and June 1934)"; the Bureau then bought Thompson submachine guns and Winchester rifles, later modified Remington Model 81 semi-automatics. The name ran Bureau of Investigation (1908) → Division of Investigation (1933, when it was linked to the Prohibition Bureau) → Federal Bureau of Investigation (1935). Purvis took the Chicago office in 1932 and resigned in 1935.
  • The odd detail: until mid-1934 a federal agent who caught a bank robber had to make a citizen's arrest and phone a marshal.
  • People: Melvin Purvis — Chicago special agent in charge 1932–35.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas_City_massacre ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Purvis
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar, with one setting note: the Bureau here is K. Edgar Hoover's FBI, whose supernatural division is Section 13; the Secret Service is a separate, rival Treasury faction, not under Hoover. The 1934 powers go to the Bureau (and so to Section 13) exactly as in the record; whether the setting's Secret Service already carried arrest powers is the owner's call. ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Before mid-1934 any federal player-character is legally a civilian with a badge. That is the best era for a Section 13 team: they have to borrow a Chicago cop to make every arrest — and the cop sees everything.

The Federal Kidnapping Act (the "Lindbergh Law") and the seven-day rule — 22 June 1932 / 18 May 1934 ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩

  • Where: Federal law; Chicago's kidnap wave (the Factor and Hamm cases — see R01, R02).
  • What happened (how it worked): Signed by President Herbert Hoover on 22 June 1932 after the Lindbergh baby's body was found, the act made it a federal crime to carry a kidnapped person across a state or national line. The original act carried no presumption at all; the 18 May 1934 amendment added a rebuttable presumption that a victim not released within seven days had been carried across a state line — letting federal agents in on day eight. The 24-hour presumption in today's statute dates only from 6 August 1956 (after the Peter Weinberger kidnapping), so it does not exist in the 1930s ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩. The 1934 amendments also exempted parents taking their own children and allowed the death penalty when the victim was not released unharmed. The first major prosecution was the July 1933 Urschel kidnapping (Machine Gun Kelly — life sentences).
  • The odd detail: federal jurisdiction arrived by the calendar: from May 1934 a kidnapping was presumed federal at the end of the seventh day, whether or not anyone had crossed a line ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩.
  • People: Herbert Hoover — president (holds in the setting, 1929–33); Charles F. Urschel — Oklahoma oilman kidnapped July 1933.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Kidnapping_Act ; https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1201 (amendment notes: "Act Aug. 6, 1956, substituted 'twenty-four hours' for 'seven days'") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidnapping_of_Peter_Weinberger
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Hoover is president 1929–33 in the setting). The enforcing agency is the Bureau — K. Edgar Hoover's FBI, with Section 13 for the cases that turn strange; the Secret Service is a separate faction and does not enforce it ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A GM's clock for any abduction scenario: on day eight (from May 1934) the feds arrive, and the local players lose the case unless they solve it first — or unless Section 13 wants it.

From dry agents to the Alcohol Tax Unit — 1920 to 1934

  • Where: Nationwide; the Chicago Prohibition Office (northwest district under administrator Malachi Harney).
  • What happened (how it worked): The Prohibition Unit was formed inside the Bureau of Internal Revenue on 16 January 1920 with about 960 dry agents; it became the separate Bureau of Prohibition on 1 April 1927, peaked at about 2,300 agents, and moved from Treasury to the Justice Department on 1 July 1930. In 1933 it was absorbed into the Bureau of Investigation as the Alcohol Beverage Unit, and with repeal in December 1933 it went back to Treasury as the Alcohol Tax Unit of the Bureau of Internal Revenue (the Bureau of Prohibition itself was formally dissolved on 2 March 1934) ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩. After repeal (5 Dec 1933) the ATU chased untaxed liquor — moonshine and bootleg stills — not drinkers. Federal firearms-law enforcement was given to the ATU only in 1942.
  • The odd detail: the same men who raided speakeasies in 1932 were, by 1934, tax collectors hunting stills — and for a year (1933–34) they had worked for the Justice Department's Bureau.
  • People: Malachi Harney — Chicago-based Prohibition district administrator; Eliot Ness — agent (see R01).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Prohibition ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Alcohol,_Tobacco,_Firearms_and_Explosives
  • Confidence: High on dates (both pages agree once read together: ATU created December 1933; 2 March 1934 is the Bureau of Prohibition's dissolution date) ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (repeal in Dec 1933 is in the setting); the 1933 merger into the Bureau BENDS with K. Edgar Hoover.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A laid-off dry agent in 1934 knows every still, cellar and tunnel in Cook County — the best guide a party can hire to Chicago's underside.

The Federal Bureau of Narcotics — 14 June 1930

  • Where: Treasury Department; nationwide.
  • What happened (how it worked): Created 14 June 1930 in the Treasury, with Harry J. Anslinger appointed first Commissioner of Narcotics by Treasury Secretary Andrew Mellon. It enforced the Harrison Act's tax-stamp system on opium, morphine and cocaine, and later the Marihuana Tax Act of 1937. Its overseas special agents never numbered more than 17 at once.
  • The odd detail: narcotics, like machine guns after 1934 and liquor after repeal, were policed as a tax matter — Treasury again.
  • People: Harry J. Anslinger — Commissioner of Narcotics from 1930.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Narcotics
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Mellon's continuity as Treasury Secretary under presidents Dawes/Hoover is plausible but UNVERIFIED for the setting).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Every Treasury tax — liquor, guns, dope — is a Treasury paper trail, and the Secret Service is Treasury. The Secret Service does not need warrants; it reads the stamp books — and Section 13, over in Justice, has to ask the old guard for a look ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩.

The Secret Service under Chief William H. Moran — 1917–1936

  • Where: Treasury Department, Washington; field offices (the Chicago field office's 1930s address was not found this session).
  • What happened (how it worked): William H. Moran — a counterfeiting expert with 54 years in the Service — was Chief from 1917 to 1936, the longest-serving chief. Under him the Service added official protection of the President's family, created the White House Police Force (1922), handled the 1928 switch to smaller-size currency, and investigated Teapot Dome. He "was often at odds with FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover over issues of jurisdiction." His successor was Frank J. Wilson (1937–46), the Treasury man who built the Capone tax case.
  • The odd detail: the real Secret Service chief spent the 1930s in turf fights with J. Edgar Hoover — which is exactly the rivalry the setting keeps: the Secret Service ("the old guard") against K. Edgar Hoover's FBI and its Section 13 ("the newcomer") ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩.
  • People: William H. Moran — chief 1917–36; Frank J. Wilson — chief 1937–46.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_H._Moran ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Secret_Service
  • Confidence: High on Moran's dates and duties; the Chicago field office location UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. The setting's Secret Service is a separate faction and is NOT run by K. Edgar Hoover (he runs the FBI); Moran (1917–36) and Wilson (1937–46) can stand as its chiefs unchanged, and their real jurisdiction fights with Hoover map straight onto the Secret Service–Section 13 rivalry ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Moran's turf war with Hoover is the setting's turf war: the old guard's chief and the newcomer's director fighting over who owns a case — and Section 13 lives in the gap between them ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩.

4. Private detectives and reporters

The labor-spy business — Pinkerton, Burns and the La Follette count — 1933–1937

  • Where: Nationwide; the big Chicago-area employers (steel, packing, farm machinery) among the clients.
  • What happened (how it worked): The Senate's La Follette Civil Liberties Committee (1936–41) investigated the five largest agencies — Pinkerton's National Detective Agency, William J. Burns International Detective Agency, National Corporation Service, Railway Audit and Inspection Company and Corporations Auxiliary Company — and counted 3,871 labor spies at work in 1933–37. Pinkerton alone had operatives in almost every union: 55 in the United Auto Workers, 17 in the textile workers, 9 in the rubber workers, 5 in the mine workers. General Motors spent $839,764.41 on detective services in 1934–36. Federal Laboratories' president John W. Young testified in March 1937 about machine guns and tear gas sold to employers. Pinkerton dropped labor spying after the 1937 hearings.
  • The odd detail: a union local in 1935 could have a Pinkerton on its executive board — the committee found spies who had risen to union office.
  • People: Robert M. La Follette Jr. — senator; John W. Young — Federal Laboratories president.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Follette_Civil_Liberties_Committee ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinkerton_(detective_agency)
  • Confidence: High on numbers. The "spies in union office" line is a well-known committee finding but was not in the text I read — treat as Medium. Pinkerton's 1930s Chicago office address not found — see Leads. Illinois private-detective licensing law and fees NOT confirmed this session.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The easiest cover for a player investigator in a Chicago plant is the true one: a detective-agency operative. The Hunter Guild, founded by T. R., would run its own agency — and sell the same spies to the steel companies.

The City News Bureau — "If your mother says she loves you, check it out" — 1890 onward

  • Where: Chicago; reporters posted at police headquarters, the coroner's office, City Hall, the County Building and the Criminal Courts.
  • What happened (how it worked): Founded in 1890 as one of America's first cooperative news agencies, owned jointly by the city's papers, City News (or "City Press") ran around the clock with about ten police reporters on duty at any one time, and documented every death reported to the coroner's office, plus courts, meetings and precinct-by-precinct election returns. It was the city's training ground: young reporters phoned facts to rewrite men who drilled them in its motto. Alumni included the playwright Charles MacArthur.
  • The odd detail: every death reported to the Cook County Coroner went onto a City News slip — a cub reporter somewhere knew about each body before any paper printed it.
  • People: Charles MacArthur — City News alumnus, co-author of The Front Page.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_News_Bureau_of_Chicago
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A City News cub is the cheapest informant in Chicago — a dollar and a cup of coffee buys the day's coroner list. He is also the first person a cult would want to own.

The press room at the Criminal Courts — The Front Page — 1928 (living memory for the 1930s)

  • Where: The press room of the old Criminal Courts Building (54 W. Hubbard St — see R09), overlooking the gallows behind Cook County Jail.
  • What happened (how it worked): Ben Hecht and Charles MacArthur, both ex-Chicago reporters, set The Front Page (opened Broadway 14 Aug 1928; film 1931 by Lewis Milestone) entirely in that "dingy press room". Their papers were modelled on City News and the Chicago Daily News; the escaped condemned man Earl Williams was loosely based on "Terrible" Tommy O'Connor (who escaped in December 1921, four days before his scheduled hanging, and was never found ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩), and the managing editor Walter Burns on the Hearst editor Walter Howey. The reporters played cards, took calls from their desks, and hid a fugitive in a roll-top desk.
  • The odd detail: Chicago's crime reporters worked inside the courthouse, next to the gallows, with their own telephones.
  • People: Ben Hecht, Charles MacArthur — authors; "Terrible" Tommy O'Connor — model for Earl Williams; Walter Howey — model for Walter Burns.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Front_Page

The telephone: letters, digits and a nickel — 1930s

  • Where: Chicago (Illinois Bell).

The Colt Monitor — the police BAR, $300 — 1931–1936

  • Where: Colt, Hartford; sold to the Bureau, Treasury, prisons, banks and police.
  • What happened (how it worked): Colt introduced the Monitor (R80) in 1931: a Browning Automatic Rifle with an 18-inch barrel and Cutts compensator, 13.2 lb, for law enforcement and prisons. Beating the vest: soft armour, the .38 Super and the .357 Magnum — late 1920s to 1935

  • Where: Nationwide; the Dunrite vest was made in Chicago.
  • What happened (how it worked): From the late 1920s gangs wore cheap vests of "thick layers of cotton padding and cloth" that stopped common handgun rounds — .22, .25, .32, .380, .38 Special and .45 ACP — travelling at up to about 980 ft/s. The Dunrite Bulletproof Vest, made by the Detective Publishing Company of Chicago, was wool with about 15 lb of metal; one was found in the back of Bonnie and Clyde's car. Police answered with the faster .38 Super (Colt, 1929) and then the .357 Magnum (1935, Elmer Keith, Phillip Sharpe and Douglas Wesson of S&W and Winchester), whose first gun was the S&W "Registered Magnum" (1935), sold with a certificate of authenticity and back-ordered for its four years despite being "extremely expensive".
  • The odd detail: a Chicago police-supply publisher sold the vest that ended up in Bonnie and Clyde's car.
  • People: Elmer Keith, Phillip B. Sharpe, Douglas B. Wesson — developers of the .357.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulletproof_vest ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/.357_Magnum ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith_%26_Wesson_Model_27
  • Confidence: High; the Registered Magnum's price and the "first one to J. Edgar Hoover" story NOT confirmed (the page did not say).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the "Hoover got No. 1" story, if the owner wants it, BENDS to K. Edgar).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A standard .38 police revolver will not stop a man in a Dunrite vest. A GM can use this as the realistic version of "the bullets don't work" — before the party learns the unrealistic one.

The press camera and the flash — Speed Graphic, flash powder, flashbulbs — 1912–1930s

  • Where: Every Chicago newspaper's photo desk.
  • What happened (how it worked): The Graflex Speed Graphic (Rochester, from 1912; the "Pre-Anniversary" model 1928–39) was the press camera: usually 4×5-inch sheet film, focal-plane shutter to 1/1000 second. Through the 1920s flash meant "sprinkling powder into the trough of a T-shaped flash lamp" — dangerous, "especially if the flash powder was damp" (flash powder dates from 1887). Manufactured flashbulbs were first produced commercially in Germany in 1929 and spread to American newsrooms in the early 1930s.
  • The odd detail: a crime-scene photograph in 1930 Chicago might still have been lit by an open pan of exploding powder — the smoke hanging over the body.
  • People: Arthur "Weegee" Fellig — the New York Speed Graphic crime photographer (not Chicago, but the type).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_Graphic ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flashbulb_(photography)
  • Confidence: High; the date flashbulbs reached Chicago papers (the US "Sashalite", c. 1930) UNVERIFIED this session.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — the flashbulb is a German invention (1929); in a world where Germany won the war, German photo technology (bulbs, the Leica, the Contax) would be more prestigious, perhaps pricier under the sanctions regime. The owner's call.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A flash-powder photograph fogs where the smoke was. A crime-scene plate shows a shape in the smoke that the photographer swears was not there.

Colour film arrives: Kodachrome — 1935 (16mm), 1936 (35mm slides)

  • Where: Eastman Kodak, Rochester; processing only by Kodak.

Recording a voice: dictograph yes, wire recorder no — 1910s–1930s

  • Where: Nationwide; the wire recorder's inventor was a Chicagoan.
  • What happened (how it worked): An investigator in the 1930s could listen remotely with a dictograph — Kelley M. Turner's office-dictation device, "a microphone in one location and a remote listening post with a speaker", whose output could be recorded on a phonograph; it was among the earliest covert listening devices in American criminal work. Magnetic wire recorders for dictation and telephone recording were made through the 1920s–30s (mainly by the American Telegraphone Company) but "use of this new technology was extremely limited". The practical wire recorder came from Marvin Camras, born in Chicago on 1 January 1916, who built his first one in the 1930s for a cousin, an aspiring opera singer, and developed it at the Armour Research Foundation in Chicago; his patent (US 2,351,004) was granted only in June 1944. Wiretaps were legal federally until Nardone (1937) — see R13.
  • The odd detail: the man who made wire recording work was a Chicago student recording his cousin's arias — in the decade the players live in the technology was all but absent from the market (a handful of American Telegraphone dictation machines aside; wax-cylinder Dictaphones and Ediphones did the real work) ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩.
  • People: Kelley M. Turner — dictograph inventor; Marvin Camras — wire-recorder inventor, Armour Institute.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictograph ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wire_recording ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvin_Camras
  • Confidence: High; the year of Camras's first machine (commonly given as c. 1937–38) UNVERIFIED this session.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The only magnetic recorder in 1930s Chicago that works is a student's prototype in an Armour Institute lab. Section 13 wants it; so does whatever is speaking in the recordings of his cousin's singing.

The Magnetophon — Germany's tape recorder — Berlin, 1935 (BENDS)

  • Where: AEG and BASF, Germany; debut at the 1935 Berlin Radio Show.
  • What happened (how it worked): AEG engineers, using Fritz Pfleumer's magnetic-tape invention, showed "the world's first practical tape recorder, the K1" at the 1935 Berlin Radio Show. In 1936 Sir Thomas Beecham's London Philharmonic, on tour in Germany, was recorded (Mozart's 39th) on an AEG K2 — the players disliked the noisy playback. AC bias (1941) made tape sound live; Americans only got hold of the machine in 1945, when Jack Mullin took two from a German radio station at Bad Nauheim.
  • The odd detail: in the real record, America did not see a working tape recorder until it captured one.
  • People: Fritz Pfleumer — tape inventor; Sir Thomas Beecham — conductor; Jack Mullin — U.S. Army Signal Corps.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetophon
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in a victorious, un-Nazi Germany the Magnetophon is still German, but there is no 1945 capture; tape may reach America through trade (under sanctions) or never. A GM can make a German tape recorder in 1936 Chicago a sign of German patronage.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Germany as the patron of other people's revolutions: the German consulate's gift to a Chicago cell is a Magnetophon — the only machine in the city that can record a séance for an hour.

The third degree — the Wickersham Commission — 1929–1931

  • Where: Nationwide, Chicago included.
  • What happened (how it worked): President Herbert Hoover set up the National Commission on Law Observance and Enforcement on 20 May 1929; its 14-volume report came out in 1931 (final report 7 Jan 1931). The volume Lawlessness in Law Enforcement found "the inflicting of pain, physical or mental, to extract confessions or statements ... is widespread throughout the country"; the police volume (August Vollmer its principal author) condemned the police's "general failure ... to detect and arrest criminals guilty of the many murders, spectacular bank, payroll and other holdups".
  • The odd detail: a federal commission put in print, in 1931, that American police routinely tortured suspects — and nothing changed in the station houses.
  • People: George W. Wickersham — chairman; August Vollmer — Berkeley police chief, principal author on police.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wickersham_Commission
  • Confidence: High on the national findings; the Chicago-specific cases in the report NOT read this session (Leads).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Hoover is president 1929–33 in the setting).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A player arrested in Chicago in 1932 can expect the back room. A GM can let the players see the third degree applied to a suspect who, under the rubber hose, speaks in a voice not his own.

The writ: habeas corpus as a gangster's key — 1920s–1930s

  • Where: Cook County courts; the Criminal Courts at 26th & California from 1929.
  • What happened (how it worked): Chicago police often arrested gangsters simply for being gangsters — and the gangs' lawyers had them out on writs of habeas corpus, forcing the police to charge or release. The Survey: men like Joseph Klenze, "arrested merely because they were Capone gangsters, ... were released on habeas corpus petitions presented by Capone's lawyers"; after one round-up "a battle over writs of habeas corpus followed and these were granted to all except the 'Big Three' and John Miller"; the courts "had to release the principal witnesses on habeas corpus because neither the police nor the prosecutors could make them talk." It lists "the habeas corpus which forestalls the securing of information" among the underworld's tools, with "lawyers retained at large fees, often crooked, using political influence and bribery."
  • The odd detail: the writ that protects the innocent from secret detention was, in Chicago, the Outfit's standard exit — often within hours.
  • People: Joseph Klenze — Capone gangster (named in the Survey).
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/illinoiscrimesur00illi (pp. 874, 877, 971, 1002)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): If the party grabs a cultist and hands him to the police, a writ will spring him by morning. That is why Section 13 does not hand people to the police.

Gun-toting permits from a suburban justice of the peace — 1923 inquiry

  • Where: Greyhound Corporation headquarters, Chicago (from 1930); bus depots in the Loop.
