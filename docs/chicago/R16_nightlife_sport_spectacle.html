R16 — Nightlife, vice, sport, spectacle and entertainment in 1930s Chicago (and the Great Lakes)

  • Lane: R16 — where the city went at night and what it crowded in to watch: nightclubs, hotel rooms, ballrooms, speakeasies and repeal, taxi-dance halls, burlesque, movie palaces, vaudeville's end, baseball, football, boxing, wrestling, racing, roller derby, air races, pageants, stunts.
  • Date: 2026-09-26
  • Model: Claude Fable 5.1 (claude-fable-5-1), research agent in a 16-agent sweep
  • Brief: BRIEF.md in this folder (format, sourcing, divergence checks, hooks-as-proposals).

Overview

Sixty-three entries. The night city in the Depression decade splits cleanly into rooms: the hotel rooms where society drank (the Drake, the Blackstone, the Congress, the Stevens, the Palmer House, the Sherman's College Inn, the Pump Room from 1938) and the clubs with bedrooms upstairs; the ballrooms with chaperones and radio wires (the Aragon, the Trianon, the Edgewater's Beach Walk); the mob's supper clubs (the Chez Paree with its key-locked gambling room [UNVERIFIED — V2_R16], the Green Mill with three businesses under one roof ⟨fixed: V_R16⟩ and a bankrupt owner, the Grand Terrace with Capone's quarter); the dime economy of taxi-dance halls, Skid Row burlesque and 25-cent walkathons; and the queer city — Towertown's cafés and Bronzeville's drag balls — which the Kelly machine padlocked in the winter of 1934–35. Sport was Arch Ward's decade: the Golden Gloves, the first All-Star Game (1933), the College All-Star Game (1934), all Tribune inventions; the Bears won titles on circus dirt (1932) and for $210 a man (1933); Joe Louis turned pro on the South Side for $59 and took the title at Comiskey in 1937; the Black Hawks won a Cup with a goalie from a bar; the Cubs won 21 straight and a homer nobody saw land [UNVERIFIED — V2_R16]. The tracks and the Stadium are where the mob and the machine met the public — Edward O'Hare's dog track, Paddy Harmon's arena, Norris and Wirtz. Prices and hours are in the entries where a page gave them (dime dances, 25-cent marathons, $2 corkage, $2.40 a case of beer, 35-cent movies). The divergence check bites hardest on anything touching the Great War (Soldier Field's own name, Tunney the Marine, the Blackhawks' battalion, Harding's smoke-filled room — which in the setting made Leonard Wood) and on Germany's champions (Schmeling, the air races). Four entries ⟨fixed: V2_R16⟩ deepen the 09-22 archive (W03 Sally Rand, W07 Medinah, W08 the opera house, R02 Finnie's Ball). The Encyclopedia of Chicago's host would not resolve all session and the web-search budget ran out early, so most sourcing is Wikipedia, chicagology's Tribune transcriptions, Robert Loerzel's archival blog and bar/theatre history sites — read, not searched; gaps are marked UNVERIFIED and listed in the leads.

1. Nightclubs, hotel rooms and ballrooms

The Green Mill, 4802 N. Broadway (Uptown) — the McGurn years to the 1930s tavern

  • Where: 4802 N. Broadway at Lawrence Avenue, Uptown, Chicago (the tavern; 4804 was the Green Mill Restaurant, 4806 the Green Mill Ballroom — three separate businesses in one building).
  • What happened: Tom Chamales built the original Green Mill Gardens roadhouse-nightclub (he and his brothers Bill and George owned the 1930s tavern) ⟨fixed: V2_R16⟩; Tom kept a real-estate office upstairs. In 1927 "Machine Gun" Jack McGurn (Vincent Gebardi) was reportedly a part-owner of the then-larger nightclub. On November 8, 1927 singer Joe E. Lewis, who had left the Green Mill ($600/week) for the rival New Rendezvous ($1,000/week), was attacked in his room at the Commonwealth Hotel by three men; his throat was cut and his tongue partly severed (twelve cuts, mostly face and head); he spent six weeks in hospital and Capone reportedly gave him $10,000 toward recovery. The building was damaged by fire in 1933 and rebuilt; the "Green Mill Tavern" first appears in the Chicago yellow pages in February 1935 and got its first state liquor licence on August 26, 1935 (RM Corporation, William Chamales president). In 1936 Helene Chamales filed for bankruptcy listing $2,934,962.42 in debts, including $354,750 of unpaid Green Mill property loans; in 1938 a public auction was ordered and John L. Patten paid $210,335.57 for the property; the Green Mill Ballroom (the Solovy brothers) reopened the same year. McGurn himself was shot dead on February 15, 1936 — seven years and a day after the St. Valentine's Day Massacre of February 14, 1929 ⟨fixed: V_R16⟩ — while bowling at the second-floor Avenue Recreation bowling alley, 805 N. Milwaukee Avenue; the killers left a mocking comic valentine on the body.
  • The odd detail: Dr. Harold B. Cassidy — one of the two physicians who pleaded guilty in the case of the plastic surgery on John Dillinger and Homer Van Meter — was among the upstairs tenants of the Green Mill building in 1937 ⟨fixed: V_R16⟩. And the famous "trapdoor behind the bar / tunnels to the street" story is legend as far as the 1930s record goes: the tavern's own longtime bartender Steve Brend said McGurn "used to come in here for a drink, even after Prohibition."
  • People: Tom, Bill and George Chamales — Greek-American owners; Jack McGurn — Outfit gunman, part-owner 1927; Joe E. Lewis — singer turned comic after the cutting; Steve Brend — bartender from c.1938 who kept the legends alive; Dr. Harold B. Cassidy — Dillinger's surgeon, upstairs tenant 1937.
  • Sources: https://www.robertloerzel.com/2024/11/11/the-lounge-lived-on-the-green-mill-after-prohibition/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_McGurn ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_E._Lewis
  • Confidence: High on dates/figures from Loerzel (who works from the Tribune and court records); the tunnel legend is folklore (label it so); the identity of Lewis's attackers (Wikipedia names Giancana and "Needles" Gianola) is UNVERIFIED beyond that page.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A nightclub with three businesses under one roof ⟨fixed: V_R16⟩, a bankrupt owner, a mob "silent partner" and a Dillinger surgeon upstairs is a ready-made hive: a Section 13 informant who tends bar, and a "trapdoor" that really does go somewhere.

The Chez Paree, 610 N. Fairbanks Court — opened December 1932

  • Where: Third floor of a 1917 Marshall & Fox loft building (the old Chateau Theater building), 610 N. Fairbanks Court, Streeterville, Near North Side.
  • What happened: Mike Fritzel ("Dean of Chicago nightclub proprietors") and Joe Jacobson opened the Chez Paree in December 1932 — in the pit of the Depression, before repeal — as a Parisian-style supper club. Its chorus line were the "Chez Paree Adorables" (long-legged showgirls in skimpy costumes and exaggerated headdresses); cigarette girls doubled as photographers; there were "dice girls" for a game at the tables. Behind the bandstand was the Key Club, a back room that took an actual key to enter — the club's private gambling room [UNVERIFIED — V_R16]. On repeal night, December 5, 1933, the Chez Paree charged a $2-a-quart corkage on outside liquor brought to the tables (see the repeal entry below). It ran until 1960 and booked the biggest acts of the era (Sophie Tucker and Joe E. Lewis among the 1930s regulars [UNVERIFIED — V2_R16] — Lewis's Chez Paree run is widely stated but UNVERIFIED from a page read this session).
  • The odd detail: The second floor of the same building housed László Moholy-Nagy's School of Design (the "New Bauhaus" successor) — the Bauhaus below, the mob's showgirls above.
  • People: Mike Fritzel — proprietor, ex-Friars Inn [UNVERIFIED — V2_R16]; Joe Jacobson — partner; the Adorables — chorus line.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chez_Paree ; https://joannelyeck.com/2019/12/18/chicagos-chez-paree/ ; https://www.historycompany.com/blogs/storytelling/chez-paree-crystal-cocktail-coupe-set
  • Confidence: High on address/opening/founders; Medium on the Key Club (one secondary page); the Outfit's later hidden ownership is widely asserted but UNVERIFIED here.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Key Club is the perfect "vampires conspire in high society" room: the key is the invitation, the dice girls are the doorkeepers, and the Golden Circle's bagman sits at the same table as the alderman.

The Pump Room, Ambassador East — opened October 1, 1938

  • Where: Ambassador East Hotel, northeast corner of State Parkway and Goethe Street (1301 N. State Parkway), Gold Coast.
  • What happened: Hotelier Ernie Byfield (1889–1950; Hotel Sherman Co., Ambassador East and West, the College Inn) opened the Pump Room on October 1, 1938, naming it after the 18th-century Bath assembly room in Booth Tarkington's Monsieur Beaucaire "where royalty mixed with commoners." [UNVERIFIED — V_R16] Interiors by Samuel A. Marx. The signature was food served flambé on skewers — Byfield's own line: "we serve almost everything flambé in these rooms, occasionally even a waiter's thumb." Booth One, a cream-leather corner banquette, was held empty for celebrities even with a queue at the door and had its own telephone. The room lived on the Hollywood-to-New York rail traffic: stars changing trains in Chicago (the Super Chief / Twentieth Century Limited layover) [UNVERIFIED — V2_R16] came to "lunch with Ernie."
  • The odd detail: Booth One had a private telephone line with a number that was rarely given out — a table you could call.
  • People: Ernie Byfield — hotelier, son of a Hungarian immigrant (Beifeld → Byfield); Samuel A. Marx — designer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pump_Room ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernie_Byfield ; https://drloihjournal.blogspot.com/2019/07/pump-room-restaurant-famous-for-catering-to-mid-20th-centurys-biggest-celebrities.html
  • Confidence: High on date/founder/location; the costumed servers ("in the style of 18th-century footmen") are widely described but not confirmed from a page read this session — UNVERIFIED detail.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (opens late in the window: Roosevelt years).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A booth that stays empty until "someone worthy" arrives, with its own phone — an old-money vampire's standing table; the maître d' knows who's expected before they arrive.

The Aragon Ballroom, Lawrence and Winthrop (Uptown) — opened July 15, 1926

  • Where: NW corner of Lawrence and Winthrop Avenues, Uptown (a block from the Green Mill, about a block east of the Uptown Theatre ⟨fixed: V2_R16⟩).
  • What happened: Andrew and William Karzas, who made their first money in nickelodeons, opened the Aragon on July 15, 1926 (the first event the night before was an Elks beauty contest); Huszagh & Hill built the Spanish-Baroque shell and movie-palace designer John Eberson dressed the inside as a Moorish courtyard under a night sky. The 21,036-square-foot spring-loaded floor (cork and felt padding) held about 5,000 dancers. The house rules were strict: dress code, no alcohol, "dance watchdogs" (hostesses from the Juvenile Protective Association), the Charleston and space-hogging novelty dances banned, a waltz every third number, and tuxedoed floor men who broke up "wild dancing and/or displays of affection." Wayne King, "the Waltz King," took over the bandstand in spring 1927 and WGN began live broadcasts from the Aragon that year, six nights a week for decades. The acoustics were so bad at first ("when we'd hit a chord, you'd hear it three times coming back at you" — Ted Fio Rito) that management reportedly spent $70,000 on the ceiling before soprano Claudia Muzio sang there in January 1928.
  • The odd detail: Trumpeter Wingy Manone's band was made to play the compulsory waltzes in paper hats; when a visiting jazzman heckled ("take off the false whiskers, Wingy, I know you!"), the band broke off a waltz for "Jazzin' Babies Blues" — and the next night Wayne King was back on the stand ⟨fixed: V_R16⟩.
  • People: Andrew and William Karzas — Greek-American ballroom kings; Wayne King — house bandleader; the JPA "dance watchdogs."
  • Sources: https://www.robertloerzel.com/2024/07/02/the-aragon-ballroom-dance-watchdogs-and-weird-echoes/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aragon_Ballroom_(Chicago)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 timeline unaffected; holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): 5,000 people, no drink, chaperones on the floor and a radio wire to the whole Midwest — a place to pass a message in a waltz, and a "third echo" that the sound men never quite fixed.

The Trianon Ballroom, 6201 Cottage Grove Avenue (Woodlawn) — opened December 6, 1922

  • Where: 6201 Cottage Grove Avenue at 62nd Street, Woodlawn, South Side.
  • What happened: The Karzas brothers' first palace, by theatre architects Rapp & Rapp, opened December 6, 1922 and was sold as "The World's Most Beautiful Ballroom" (its own 1925–28 radio station was WMBB — the initials). Main floor 100 × 140 feet, reportedly 3,000 dancers, air-conditioned, coats and ties for men and gowns for women, floor men to enforce it, many WGN live broadcasts. Through the 1930s it stood in a neighbourhood whose Black population was growing block by block, and the ballroom was — by long-standing accounts — a whites-only house until the 1950s (that policy is widely stated; not confirmed from a page read this session, so UNVERIFIED here). Demolished 1967.
  • The odd detail: A palace named for Versailles' Trianon, with its own radio call letters bragging about itself, at the edge of the Black Belt.
  • People: William and Andrew Karzas — owners; Rapp & Rapp — architects.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trianon_Ballrooms
  • Confidence: High on opening/size; Medium on the 1930s racial policy (UNVERIFIED this session).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 timeline unaffected; holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The colour line at the door is the period's own social sorting (the owner's rule: the decade's categories, not ours); a Bronzeville contact can't walk in the front — but the band can.

The Edgewater Beach Hotel and the Beach Walk, 5300 block of N. Sheridan Road — 1916/1924, the 1930s radio years

  • Where: 5301–5355 N. Sheridan Road, Edgewater, on the lakefront (main building opened June 3, 1916; tower building February 9, 1924; the pink apartments 1928).
  • What happened: The "sunrise yellow" hotel with a 1,200-foot private beach ran the Beach Walk — an open-air marble-tiled dance floor and bandshell on the lake side, "the only one of its kind in the United States" [UNVERIFIED — V2_R16] — in summer, and the Marine Dining Room in winter. The hotel's own station WEBH (studio: the glass-walled "Crystal Studio" on the lobby level, broadcasting Tue–Sat 10 p.m.–2 a.m. and Sundays 6–10 p.m. in 1923–24, silent Mondays) closed in November 1928; after 1929 the bands were carried live on network radio ⟨fixed: V2_R16⟩. Over the years the bandstand held Wayne King, Ted Fio Rito and Dan Russo (their Oriole Orchestra was there in 1924), Benny Goodman, Tommy Dorsey, Glenn Miller, Artie Shaw and Xavier Cugat ⟨fixed: V2_R16⟩. Guests over the years included FDR, Chaplin, Bette Davis. (In 1949 Ruth Steinhagen shot ballplayer Eddie Waitkus here — the seed of The Natural — outside the window but worth knowing.)
  • The odd detail: The radio studio had soundproof triple-paned windows so hotel guests could stand and watch the broadcast — a fishbowl of musicians at midnight.
  • People: Ted Fio Rito and Dan Russo — the Oriole Orchestra (first remote broadcast from the hotel March 29, 1924); Wayne King — bandleader.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgewater_Beach_Hotel ; http://www.edgewaterhistory.org/ehs/content/v27-4-radio-broadcasting-edgewater-beach-hotel-part-1-webh-am
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A lakefront ballroom whose music goes out live to every farmhouse radio in five states — a coded set list is a broadcast to a whole cult; and the beach at 2 a.m. is where the lake gives things back.

2. Prohibition's end, speakeasies and vice

Taxi-dance halls and Cressey's "The Taxi-Dance Hall" — 1920s–1930s; book 1932

  • Where: Halls clustered on the Near North and West Sides (Clark Street, Madison Street, the Loop fringe) — Cressey's book maps the "taxi-dance districts."
  • What happened: The ticket-a-dance system reached Chicago about 1920: Godfrey Johnson of the Mader-Johnson Dance Studios copied a New York "ten-cent-ticket-a-dance" scheme for his third-floor hall and found "a rough hoodlum element from Clark Street" replacing his pupils; Greek immigrant Nicholas Philocrates and others then opened halls that dropped the pretence of instruction. A dance cost a dime; the girl kept roughly half. Paul Goalby Cressey (1900–1955) [UNVERIFIED — V2_R16], of the University of Chicago sociology department, published The Taxi-Dance Hall: A Sociological Study in Commercialized Recreation and City Life in 1932 from field notes and interviews with dancers, patrons and owners; his dancers were 15 to 28 years old, largely daughters of Polish, Swedish, German, French and Dutch immigrant homes, two-thirds without a father's support, many using aliases; his patrons were Italians, Poles, Greeks, Jews, lonely newcomers, older men and — his most-discussed group — Filipino men, who were welcome nowhere else. Reformers and the Juvenile Protective Association fought the halls for licensing and police supervision through the decade [UNVERIFIED — V2_R16].
  • The odd detail: The girls' aliases: a taxi-dancer could be three different names in three halls, and a lot of them were.
  • People: Paul G. Cressey — sociologist; Godfrey Johnson — dance-studio owner who accidentally started it; Nicholas Philocrates — Greek hall operator.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxi_dance_hall ; https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/001063360
  • Confidence: High on the book and the system; Medium on the specific Chicago hall count (not given).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A taxi-dance girl with three names in three halls is a ready-made informant (or a fae changeling hiding in plain sight); the dime tickets are an untraceable currency for passing messages.

4. Baseball

Babe Ruth's "called shot," Wrigley Field — October 1, 1932

  • Where: Wrigley Field, 1060 W. Addison Street, Lake View (Game 3 of the World Series, Yankees at Cubs).
  • What happened: The Series was poisoned before it started: the Cubs had voted mid-season pickup Mark Koenig (an ex-Yankee) only a half share of Series money, and the Yankees rode them for it; Chicago fans reportedly cursed and spat at Ruth and his wife Claire arriving at the park, and pelted him with lemons in left field and at the plate. In the fifth inning, score 4–4, with two strikes on him from Charlie Root, Ruth made some gesture — toward the centre-field flagpole, the scoreboard, or the Cubs' dugout — and hit the next curveball an estimated 440–490 feet to deep centre. Root swore to his death that Ruth never pointed ("he'd have ended up on his ass"); Lou Gehrig (on a 2020-surfaced audio clip) said he pointed "toward the flagpole in center field." In the stands: Governor Franklin D. Roosevelt, the Democratic candidate, five weeks from the election and five months from the presidency ⟨fixed: V_R16⟩, and twelve-year-old John Paul Stevens, who as a Supreme Court justice still insisted Ruth pointed at the scoreboard. Two amateur 16-mm films (Kandle's, surfaced 1970s; Warp's, 1999) show a gesture and settle nothing.
  • The odd detail: The future President and a future Justice were both in the seats: Roosevelt reportedly laughed as Ruth ran the bases; Stevens insisted for the rest of his life that Ruth pointed ⟨fixed: V2_R16⟩.
  • People: Babe Ruth; Charlie Root — Cubs pitcher; Mark Koenig — the half-share grievance; Gov. Franklin D. Roosevelt — spectator; John Paul Stevens — 12-year-old witness.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babe_Ruth%27s_called_shot
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Roosevelt is still the 1932 candidate in the setting).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Section 13 has a man in the box behind Roosevelt; what the film "doesn't show" is a third layer of truth — 40,000 witnesses and nobody can agree, which is what a glamour looks like from the outside.

The first All-Star Game, Comiskey Park — July 6, 1933

  • Where: Comiskey Park, 35th and Shields, Armour Square (South Side).
  • What happened: Mayor Edward J. Kelly asked for a big sporting event to go with the Century of Progress; Tribune sports editor Arch Ward answered with a one-off "Game of the Century" — fans picked the starters by ballots printed in 55 newspapers, the managers (Connie Mack, John McGraw) picked the rest. 47,595 paid (the Tribune said 49,000); net gate of $45,000 went to a fund for disabled and needy ex-players; the American League won 4–2 in 2 hours 5 minutes, on Babe Ruth's two-run homer in the third. It was meant to happen once "to boost morale during the Depression"; it never stopped.
  • The odd detail: The same park hosted, from that same summer, the Negro leagues' East-West All-Star Game (1933–1960), which some years outdrew the white one (cross-ref R14).
  • People: Arch Ward — Tribune sports editor and the decade's great promoter (Golden Gloves, All-Star Game, College All-Star Game); Mayor Edward J. Kelly; Connie Mack; John McGraw; Babe Ruth.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1933_Major_League_Baseball_All-Star_Game ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comiskey_Park
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Arch Ward is the man who can put 50,000 people in one place on a Thursday afternoon; whoever owns Ward owns the crowd — a Golden Circle asset, or a Section 13 one.

The 1935 Cubs' 21-game streak and the Series they lost — September 4–28, October 2–7, 1935

  • Where: Wrigley Field; Navin Field, Detroit.
  • What happened: Trailing by 2½ games on September 4, Charlie Grimm's Cubs won 21 straight, not losing again until September 28 ⟨fixed: V_R16⟩ (tying the 1880 White Stockings' franchise mark), finished 100–54 and took the pennant; Gabby Hartnett became the first NL catcher voted MVP, Billy Herman hit 57 doubles. They then lost the World Series to Detroit in six games (Oct 2–7). Owner P. K. Wrigley, GM Charles Weber.
  • The odd detail: Three weeks of a city convinced its team could not lose, ending in Detroit.
  • People: Charlie Grimm — manager; Gabby Hartnett — catcher/MVP; P. K. Wrigley — owner.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1935_Chicago_Cubs_season
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A streak like that has a price; a numbers-runner who "knew" every game in advance is the thread to pull (cross-ref the policy racket, R14).

The "Homer in the Gloamin'," Wrigley Field — September 28, 1938

  • Where: Wrigley Field (no lights until 1988).
  • What happened: Pittsburgh led the Cubs by 1½ games coming into the series. The game began at 3:00 p.m.; sunset was 5:37 (Tribune). Bottom of the ninth, 5–5, two out, 0–2 count, near-dark — the umpires had ruled this was the last inning, and the rules of the day did not allow a suspended game: it would have been replayed entirely next day. Player-manager Gabby Hartnett hit Mace Brown's pitch "into the darkness" for the winning homer; players and fans stormed the field and escorted him round the bases. The Cubs took first place, won 10–1 next day and clinched three days later — then were swept by the Yankees.
  • The odd detail: Nobody in the park saw the ball land; they saw Hartnett running and believed him [UNVERIFIED — V2_R16].
  • People: Gabby Hartnett — catcher-manager; Mace Brown — Pirates pitcher.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer_in_the_Gloamin%27
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The one game in the decade won by something no witness saw — perfect for a scene where an investigator is in the bleachers on other business.

Wrigley's 1937 face (bleachers, ivy, the scoreboard) and Comiskey's first night game — 1937 / August 14, 1939

  • Where: Wrigley Field (Lake View); Comiskey Park (Armour Square).
  • What happened: In 1937 the young Bill Veeck Jr., son of the Cubs' president, oversaw the bleacher rebuild and planted the ivy on the outfield wall (inspired by Perry Stadium, Indianapolis) and the hand-operated centre-field scoreboard went up — no batted ball has ever hit it. The Bears had played at Wrigley since 1921 (50 straight seasons, an NFL record). Across town, Comiskey (52,000 seats 1927–37) turned on its lights for the first night game on August 14, 1939, beating St. Louis 5–2 before about 35,000. The Chicago Cardinals played NFL football at Comiskey in parts of 1929–30.
  • The odd detail: In the whole decade the North Side ballpark got ivy and the South Side one got electric light — two theologies of the game.
  • People: Bill Veeck Jr. — the ivy; P. K. Wrigley — owner who would not buy lights.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrigley_Field ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comiskey_Park
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Something that never gets hit, for eighty years, is a ward; who set it and what is it keeping in the scoreboard?

5. Football, boxing, wrestling, hockey

The indoor playoff at Chicago Stadium — December 18, 1932

  • Where: Chicago Stadium, 1800 W. Madison Street (Near West Side).
  • What happened: Blizzards followed by severe cold and wind chill drove the Bears–Portsmouth Spartans playoff for the NFL title indoors (game day itself reached a high of 20°F, warmer than anticipated earlier in the week) ⟨fixed: V_R16⟩ to the world's largest arena (26,000 with floor seats). The Great Lakes Exposition, Cleveland — June 27, 1936 to September 1937

Cleveland Stadium's first event: Schmeling–Stribling — July 3, 1931

Bridgeport and Old Town: Schaller's Pump and Twin Anchors — the speaks the machine drank in

Twin Anchors' 1881 building had a saloon from 1890; during Prohibition Don and Ethel Humphreys ran it as "Tante Lee Soft Drinks" with the front windows boarded to look empty; Bob Walters and Herb Eldean, Chicago Yacht Club men, reopened it as Twin Anchors (1932 is the usual date — UNVERIFIED on the page read) with no kitchen — Walters' wife cooked Sunday dinners until the ribs forced them to build one.

9. Living memory (pre-1930) — what a 1930s Chicagoan still talked about

The Everleigh Club and the Levee — 1900–1911 (pre-1916, happened)

  • Where: 2131–2133 S. Dearborn Street, in the Levee (roughly 18th to 22nd, State to Clark [UNVERIFIED — V2_R16]), South Loop.
  • What happened: Ada and Minna Everleigh put a $35,000 inheritance into the most expensive brothel in America (February 1900): a $15,000 gold-leafed piano in the Music Room, silk curtains, mirrored ceilings, 15 to 25 servants. On March 3, 1902 Prince Henry of Prussia was entertained there — legend says the champagne-from-a-slipper custom began when a girl's shoe came off. Mayor Carter Harrison Jr. closed the Club on October 24, 1911 after the Vice Commission counted nearly 600 brothels in the city; the sisters left with about a million dollars and lived out their lives in New York under another name. (The papers called Marshall Field Jr.'s fatal gunshot of November 22, 1905 an accident at home; Wikipedia says there is "some evidence" he was shot by a prostitute at the Club, and a rival madam accused the sisters ⟨fixed: V2_R16⟩.) The Levee's aldermen, "Bathhouse" John Coughlin and "Hinky Dink" Kenna, outlived the district; their successors Colosimo, Torrio and Capone inherited its cafés (the Four Deuces at 2222 S. Wabash [UNVERIFIED — V2_R16] is the next generation's address — R01's lane).
  • The odd detail: A Prussian prince in a Chicago brothel in 1902; in the setting, the same prince's country won the war.
  • People: Ada and Minna Everleigh; Prince Henry of Prussia; Carter Harrison Jr. — mayor; Bathhouse John and Hinky Dink — the Lords of the Levee.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everleigh_Club
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Everleigh guest book is the best list of the city's ruling men ever compiled — where is it in 1936, and who wants it?

The First Ward Ball at the Coliseum — 1896–1908 (pre-1916, happened)

  • Where: The 7th Regiment Armory, then the Chicago Coliseum, 1513 S. Wabash.
  • What happened: Coughlin and Kenna's annual "fundraiser" for the First Ward — 10,000 to 15,000 a night, "a wild mix of society thrill seekers, police captains, politicians," madams, pimps and the girls of the Levee; over $50,000 a year for the two aldermen; most years ending in near-riot. The 1908 ball "made that affair look tame" ⟨fixed: V2_R16⟩; Mayor Fred Busse ended it in 1909 by pulling the liquor licence.
  • The odd detail: The same hall that held the Republican conventions and the Black Hawks held the vice trade's annual masquerade — and in 1935 the first roller derby.
  • People: Bathhouse John Coughlin; Hinky Dink Kenna; Mayor Fred Busse.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Ward_Ball ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Coliseum
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A masquerade in a hall built from a Confederate prison, every year for twelve years — if the Golden Circle wanted a Chicago sabbath, it had one, and the 1930s old-timers remember who wore what.
