R37 — Gary and the Calumet Region, 1906–1940: the steel towns and their communities

Lane: Gary, East Chicago / Indiana Harbor, Hammond, Whiting, and South Chicago / the Illinois Calumet (Calumet City, Hegewisch). Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Sonnet 5.5 · Status: complete — 37 entries. Facts marked "read" were on pages fetched this session; "search-result summary only" means the page could not be fetched (paywall, redirect, image-only PDF) and the detail is Low–Medium confidence.

Overview. The Calumet region is where the Chicago story meets steel and oil: five cities built or swollen by three companies (U.S. Steel at Gary, Inland Steel and Youngstown at Indiana Harbor/East Chicago, Standard Oil at Whiting) in the 25 years before the Depression. The strongest material is not the famous gangsters (Scalise/Anselmi, Dillinger and Anna Sage are in R01/R02) but the institutions: Gary's founding by moving a river and 12 million cubic yards of sand; the company town's model First Subdivision against the 200-saloon "Patch"; the 1919 strike and the Black and Mexican workforces it created; a Prohibition prosecution of the mayor and about 55 Lake County officials, with a witness shot on the eve of testimony; the Klan-backed mayors that followed; the 1927 Emerson walkout ("We won't go back until Emerson is white") and the Black high school built as its settlement; the 1932 "relief" that put 3,330 Lake County Mexicans on trains at $15 a head, paid by Inland Steel and organised by a Legion post commander who was also the steel company's transportation agent; Whiting's 1921 still explosion ("an act of God") and the 1929 shareholder coup that Rockefeller won from Egypt; and a run of oddities — a Hammond race track built on an island in Wolf Lake, a trapper who held off 26 deputies from a World's Fair building, a Hegewisch priest shot for his sacramental wine. Every place is real and most of the numbers come from county reports, Indiana state-history essays and local historical societies. The setting's main pressure points: Leonard Wood (Gary 1919), Coolidge's pardon of Mayor Johnson (March 1929 — BENDS to Dawes/Hoover), Teapot Dome in the Whiting proxy fight (BENDS), the Mexican colonia (BENDS — annexed north), Serbs, Croats and Hungarians (BENDS — Austria-Hungary survives), and the American Legion (BENDS — no Great War). Gaps: no named 1930s Calumet City nightclubs were found; Whiting's other fires, Gary Works fatalities and the Depression-era numbers for Gary are thin; Chronicling America returned nothing usable on the strip.

PART A — GARY: THE MILL AND THE COMPANY TOWN

A town laid out in a snowstorm — Gary, 12 March 1906 to first steel, February 1909

  • Where: Gary, Lake County, Indiana; the south shore of Lake Michigan at the mouth of the Grand Calumet (Gary Works, the "Patch" and the First Subdivision to the south).
  • What happened: U.S. Steel's chairman Elbert H. Gary announced the project in 1905; on 12 March 1906 engineer Ralph E. Rowley's crew began laying out the mill. Corporation attorney Armanis F. Knotts (a former mayor of Hammond) had quietly bought up 9,000 acres along seven miles of lakeshore for about $7.2 million. Builders removed more than 12 million cubic yards of sand, poured 2 million cubic yards of concrete, dug a harbor a mile long and 25 feet deep, and moved the Grand Calumet River. The first ore boat entered the harbor on 23 July 1908, the first blast furnace ran in December 1908, and open-hearth steel came in February 1909; by the end of 1909 the mill had 6,800 employees and had made 570,000 tons. By 1920 there were 12 blast furnaces, 838 coke ovens and 45 open-hearth furnaces, and 52 nationalities in a city that had not existed 14 years earlier. The corporation spent about $42 million on mill and town together by 1908.
  • The odd detail: The city was founded by rerouting a river and stripping a dune range; the sand had to go somewhere, and the founding story never says where. (Wikipedia's Gary Works page — the source R07 used — gives "about 11 million cubic feet" of sand and the first ore shipment as 23 June 1908; the IU essay gives 12 million cubic yards and the first ore boat on 23 July 1908. The two sources differ by a factor of about 30 on the sand and by a month on the ore; the IU figures are quoted here and the difference is flagged.)
  • People: Elbert H. Gary — U.S. Steel chairman, the town's namesake · Ralph E. Rowley — construction engineer who broke ground · Armanis F. Knotts — attorney and secret land-buyer, ex-mayor of Hammond (1902–04), then manager of the Gary Land Company (d. 1937, Yankeetown, Florida — he founded that town in 1923) · Thomas Knotts — his brother, "heavily involved in the development of" Gary and named as its founding mayor by a local-history page.
  • Sources: https://webapp1.dlib.indiana.edu/ussteel/context/essay.jsp (read) · https://discoverindianahistory.org/tours/show/60 (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armanis_F._Knotts (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Works (read) · https://www.ourgarystories.com/post/the-dark-history-of-gary-mayoral-leadership-in-the-jazz-age (read)
  • Image: Indiana University's US Steel Gary Works Photograph Collection, 1906–1971 — https://webapp1.dlib.indiana.edu/ussteel/ (photographs of the construction; rights not checked this session).
  • Confidence: High on dates and quantities as given by the IU essay; the sand-removal unit and the "first operation" date differ between sources.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A river moved and 12 million cubic yards of dune shifted in three years — the kind of earth-moving that turns up something. An IHS priest or Secret Service agent reading the 1906 survey notes finds why the Grand Calumet was rerouted exactly there.

Two Garys: the First Subdivision and "the Patch" — 1906–1920s

  • Where: Gary, Indiana. The First Subdivision runs from the mill entrance south to the Wabash tracks (9th Avenue) and from Tennessee Street west to Fillmore Street; the Patch lies south of it; the 5th Avenue Apartments (brick, mostly four-storey, west of Broadway) sit inside the model district.
  • What happened: The Gary Land Company, a U.S. Steel subsidiary, built a model town for skilled workers, foremen and supervisors: 800 acres platted into 4,000 lots, 506 houses for supervisors and skilled hands, paved and landscaped streets, deed restrictions to keep the look orderly (engineer A. P. Melton did the planning). South of it, off the corporation's land, an unplanned district — "the Patch" — housed the unskilled: over 200 saloons, unpaved streets, no sewers and no running water in the early years. East and west of Broadway, southern and eastern European immigrants clustered by nationality; Black families settled in the Central District that became Midtown. Company-owned boarding houses initially took Mexican workers; by 1928 none remained in such housing.
  • The odd detail: One city, two legal regimes — the corporation's tidy subdivision and, a few blocks south, over 200 saloons on mud streets that the corporation did not own and did not have to police.
  • People: A. P. Melton — Gary Land Company planning engineer.
  • Sources: https://discoverindianahistory.org/tours/show/60 (read) · https://webapp1.dlib.indiana.edu/ussteel/context/essay.jsp (read) · https://discoverindianahistory.org/items/show/604 (read)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "The Patch" is where the Prohibition-era bootleggers, the Klan and the fortune-tellers would set up: no water, no sewers, no company inspector. Anything that wants to feed on immigrant steelworkers starts south of 9th Avenue, where the mill's model town ends.

The town's settlement houses, the International Institute and Froebel School — 1912–1921

  • Where: Gary, Indiana (Froebel School; the International Institute; Stewart House).
  • What happened: In 1910 49.1 percent of Gary's population was foreign-born and about 71 percent were either foreign-born or had a foreign-born parent; the manual workers were mostly from eastern and southeastern Europe. The city set up four settlement houses, but only Stewart House (opened 1921) served African Americans. The International Institute (1919) helped the foreign-born with naturalization and English. Froebel School (opened 1912) was an "experiment in progressive education" and the city's first integrated school, though Black pupils there faced "virtual segregation." Indiana's 1922 state law officially supported segregated schooling; Gary's schools ran on "partial segregation" between 1908 and 1947.
  • The odd detail: A city that built a school in 1912 on the theory that children of many origins could learn together, and then, by 1927, ended up building a separate Black high school after a walkout (see the Emerson entry).
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://discoverindianahistory.org/items/show/604 (read)
  • Confidence: High (single, well-sourced essay).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 / holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The International Institute is exactly where a naturalization file, an immigrant's real name and a hidden past pass through a clerk's hands; Section 13 or the Secret Service would have a friend there.

PART B — 1919: THE STRIKE AND THE MEN WHO LEFT THE MILL

Louis Caldwell, C. D. Elston and the Black steelworkers of 1919 — February–October 1919

  • Where: Gary, Indiana; Gary Works and American Sheet and Tin Plate; the Black neighbourhood south of the mills ("the Patch", between 15th and 25th Avenues).
  • What happened: By 1919 about 2,699 Black workers were on the payroll of the U.S. Steel mills in Gary, in a Black community that had grown from roughly 400 people in 1906 to more than 5,000 in 1920. Louis H. Caldwell — born in Copiah County, Mississippi, a Pullman porter who worked his way through law school, in Gary from 1915 and one of only three Black attorneys there, a founding officer of the Gary NAACP — and steelworker C. D. Elston, who quit his job to work for the Allied Iron and Steel Council, began holding separate meetings for Black workers in February 1919 and spoke "at almost every" ethnic workers' gathering across the city through September. When the strike began, roughly 85 percent of the regional workforce joined it; one account says the company offered $5 to Black workers who would scab and that most took the money and left. Later Black steelworker organizers named in the same account include George Kimbley (first Black union staff representative) and William Young (first Black grievance man, hired 1922).
  • The odd detail: The famous story is that Black workers were brought in as strikebreakers — the local record shows a Black lawyer and a Black steelworker addressing Slovak, Serb and Polish meetings for the union that spring.
  • People: Louis H. Caldwell — lawyer, NAACP founder in Gary · C. D. Elston — steelworker turned organizer · George Kimbley; William Young — later Black union officials.
  • Sources: https://portside.org/2019-09-30/gary-1919-untold-story-racial-solidarity-garys-history (read; an advocacy publication — the $5 claim is unconfirmed) · https://discoverindianahistory.org/items/show/604 (read)
  • Confidence: Medium (numbers from a single advocacy summary; the outline is consistent with the Discover Indiana material).
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — the 1919 steel strike, the NAACP and the Great Migration are domestic and can hold, but the setting has no Red Scare and a different national backdrop (Marshall as president in 1919, Wood the general).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Caldwell is the sort of man a Kabbalah or IHS contact would need in Gary — a lawyer everyone trusts and no one watches. The Steel Trust's spy files on him would list every meeting he addressed; a GM can read them as a map of the town's ethnic fault lines.

The brick on Broadway, the trolley, and the workforce the strike changed — 22 September–6 October 1919 and after

  • Where: Gary, Indiana; Broadway (main north–south street), the mill gates; martial-law zone covering Gary and (by some accounts) East Chicago and Indiana Harbor.
  • What happened: The national strike began 22 September 1919 (350,000 workers across the country); in Gary about 85 percent of steelworkers refused to work. They worked twelve-hour shifts, 66-hour weeks. Discover Indiana dates the trigger to 5 October (Wikipedia's Gary page says 4 October): strikers wrecked a trolley carrying replacement workers on Broadway and a brick hit a strike supporter, and police responded. On 6 October the mayor called out seven Indiana militia units and federal troops under General Leonard Wood arrived; by evening the whole city was under Army control (R36 has the full Wood story). The company ordered 30,000+ Black workers from the South as replacements, and Mexican workers were hired as non-union replacements — the first Mexicans in the mills in numbers; some were housed on mill grounds. The strike ended in Gary within a month of the Army's arrival and nationally in January 1920. The eight-hour day arrived in 1923. The Black share of the mill workforce went from 8.8% (1919) to 20.5% (1923); the Mexican share grew to 8% by 1925.
  • The odd detail: The strike that was crushed in October 1919 built the two communities — Black and Mexican — that the Depression then turned on.
  • People: Maj. Gen. Leonard Wood — commander of the federal troops · Elbert H. Gary — U.S. Steel chairman · William Hodges — mayor of Gary in 1919 (per ourgarystories; UNVERIFIED as the mayor who called the militia).
  • Sources: https://discoverindianahistory.org/items/show/603 (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary,_Indiana (read, History section) · https://www.ourgarystories.com/post/the-dark-history-of-gary-mayoral-leadership-in-the-jazz-age (read)
  • Confidence: High on the outline; the 4-vs-5 October date differs by source.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — same as R07/R36: no Red Scare, Wood becomes president in 1921, Marshall is president in 1919; the strike itself holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The workforce shift 1919–25 is the ground for every 1930s Gary story: the people who arrived under the guns of 1919 are the ones a Depression-era relief board sends home in 1932.

PART C — GARY'S SCHOOLS, SEGREGATION AND THE BLACK COMMUNITY

The Emerson School strike — 26–30 September 1927

  • Where: Emerson School, Gary, Indiana (kindergarten–12 "unit school"; the first Gary school run on Wirt's work-study-play system); East Side Park (mass meeting); the city council chamber.
  • What happened: In 1926 six Black students were transferred into Emerson to relieve overcrowding; in September 1927 eighteen more came from the Virginia Street School, bringing Black enrolment to 24 out of about 2,800. Over 600 white students walked out on Monday 26 September 1927, carrying a banner reading "We won't go back until Emerson is white," and by the third day 1,357 — about three-quarters of the high-school students — were out. On 30 September the city council settled it: the eighteen were sent back to Virginia Street and money was voted for a separate all-Black high school (Roosevelt) and a temporary school. By November 1927 no Black students remained at Emerson. (A 1928 Emerson yearbook account dates the walkout to 22 September and gives about 900 strikers out of roughly 3,000; single-source and inconsistent with Discover Indiana.)
  • The odd detail: A student-led strike, and its demand was the pupils' own — the adults' compromise was the order to build a school for the excluded. A locked classroom door (and one remark) are the reported trigger.
  • People: William Wirt — school superintendent who approved the transfers · Drucilla Miller; Lucia Beddow — student authors of the strike account in the 1928 yearbook · "Esh the orator" — a strike leader named in that yearbook.
  • Sources: https://discoverindianahistory.org/items/show/605 (read) · https://michellemcgillvargas.com/2015/09/21/the-integration-situation-the-1927-emerson-school-boycott/ (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerson_High_School_(Indiana) (search result only)
  • Confidence: High on the outcome; day counts and numbers vary by source (26 Sept per Discover Indiana and Wikipedia; 22 Sept per the yearbook).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A schoolyard walkout of a thousand white teenagers in a single morning with a banner already lettered: someone organised it. The Golden Circle's second-coming Klan (Mayor Floyd Williams was Klan-backed) makes a good candidate.

Roosevelt High School and the Gary Colored American — 1927–1931

  • Where: Roosevelt High School, Gary (Midtown); the offices of the Gary Colored American / Gary American newspaper.
  • What happened: Roosevelt was the Black high school the Emerson settlement promised. Architect William B. Ittner of St. Louis designed a Colonial Revival main building (construction 1929–30; brick pavilion, limestone Doric columns, a cupola modelled on Independence Hall's); it was dedicated in April 1931, and the first graduation in the new building was 1933, thirty seniors. Frederick C. McFarlane was principal from 1929 to June 1933. The city's Black newspaper started six weeks after the walkout — the Gary Colored American, first issue 10 November 1927, founded by Arthur B. Whitlock (Gary's first Black city councillor, elected 1921), David E. Taylor and Chauncey Townsend, with about 2,000 readers; it became the Gary American in 1928, campaigned for Roosevelt, against lynching and for integrating Marquette Beach. Roosevelt's alumni include the actor William Marshall and the athletes Lee Calhoun and Glenn Robinson.
  • The odd detail: The school was built by a settlement whose whole purpose was segregation, and it was so good that it ended up on the National Register of Historic Places.
  • People: William B. Ittner — architect · Frederick C. McFarlane — principal · Arthur B. Whitlock — Gary's first Black councilman, newspaper co-founder · Chauncey Townsend — editor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roosevelt_College_and_Career_Academy (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_American (read) · https://discoverindianahistory.org/items/show/605 (read)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Gary American (and the Chicago Defender) are the Black community's early-warning system; a paper that fought the Marquette Beach ban would print the sighting the white dailies won't.

PART D — POLITICS, VICE AND THE COURTHOUSE

The Gary rum case — March 1923 and Mayor Johnson's prison term

  • Where: Gary (the Black & Tan Saloon, 1720 Adams Street; the Monti house at 1732 Adams); the federal courthouse in Indianapolis; the Atlanta federal penitentiary.
  • What happened: Gary's mayor Roswell O. Johnson (first elected 1913, defeated 1917 by William Hodges, back in 1921) ran a system in which illegal saloon and gambling-hall keepers paid "bonds" to the city and the money went to political campaign accounts. A federal Prohibition prosecution (US Attorney Homer Elliott) reached the courtroom in March 1923, with armed guards in the courthouse and federal marshals bringing Gary witnesses to Indianapolis by train from Union Station. The star witness's story: Gaspare (Gaspari) Monti and his wife Mary ran the Black & Tan Saloon; a raid found dynamite and liquor in a tunnel between their house and the saloon; on 12 March 1923 Monti was shot dead by a sawed-off shotgun outside his home, days before he was due to testify against Mayor Johnson and City Judge William Dunn; his widow testified in his place. Sentences: Johnson, Judge Dunn and fifty-three other Lake County officials went to federal prison (a separate line of the same source says "over six dozen") [UNVERIFIED — V_R37: the count is single-blog and self-contradicting; the Indiana Historical Bureau (https://blog.history.in.gov/the-case-of-agnes-szabo-bootlegger-entrepreneur-whistleblower/) confirms only "over sixty officials were indicted," including Johnson and Judge Dunn]; Johnson left Atlanta in November 1925.
  • The odd detail: A tunnel between a house and a saloon holding dynamite, and a shotgun killing on the eve of testimony — in a city whose mayor was, on paper, the prosecution's defendant.
  • People: Roswell O. Johnson — mayor · William Dunn — city judge · Gaspare Monti — saloon keeper, murdered witness · Mary Monti — widow, testified · Homer Elliott — US Attorney.
  • Sources: https://www.ourgarystories.com/post/the-dark-history-of-gary-mayoral-leadership-in-the-jazz-age (read; a local blog — the Monti details and the conviction counts are single-source)
  • Confidence: Medium (single blog; conviction counts vary within it).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Prohibition prosecutions are domestic). One BENDS: the trial year 1923 falls under President Leonard Wood in the setting, not Harding, so the "U.S. Attorney General" and the pardon are different men.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A tunnel between a house and a saloon on Adams Street — the setting's occult cell could run a whole cellar network under the "Black & Tan" rows south of Broadway, and the Gary rum case is the reason nobody looked.

Mayors Johnson, Fulton and Williams — 1925–1933 (Klan mayor, a suicide, a pardon)

  • Where: Gary City Hall; the Miller summer home at 739 Lincoln Street; 7500 Oak Avenue, Miller.
  • What happened: On 29 March 1925 the outgoing Johnson appointed William J. Fulton to finish out his term; Fulton served eight months, made Alice Johnson (the mayor's wife) the second woman in the U.S. to hold a public finance office, and lost the November election to Floyd E. Williams, who had won with "open and proud" Klan support (Indiana's Klan peaked in 1923–25 under D. C. Stephenson — see R07). Williams served until 1930 and was declared bankrupt in 1933. Johnson, released in Nov 1925 and pardoned in March 1929 by President Calvin Coolidge, won a third term; the Indiana Attorney General and the city council fought it, the Indiana Supreme Court threw out their injunctions, and he was inaugurated 3 January 1930. In 1932 he was charged with using city materials and labour on his Miller summer home and a rock garden (not convicted); he died 15 June 1938. William Fulton shot himself on 8 May 1930 at his home at 7500 Oak Avenue; his wife Mary found the body.
  • The odd detail: A convicted mayor pardoned in March 1929 and running the city again the following January — and the man he had appointed to keep the seat warm shot himself four months later.
  • People: Roswell O. Johnson — mayor, convict, pardoned · William J. Fulton — appointed mayor, suicide 8 May 1930 · Floyd E. Williams — Klan-backed mayor, bankrupt 1933 · Alice Johnson — city finance officer.
  • Sources: https://www.ourgarystories.com/post/the-dark-history-of-gary-mayoral-leadership-in-the-jazz-age (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Gary,_Indiana (search result only)
  • Confidence: Medium (local-history blog; dates match the search summaries).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Coolidge was never president in the setting. Johnson's pardon (March 1929) would come from Charles Dawes (if before 4 March) or Hoover (after). The Klan side holds (the setting keeps the KKK's "second coming").
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Dawes is a Chicago banker who leaves office on 4 March 1929. A pardon signed in his last days — or by Hoover just after — for a Klan-adjacent Indiana mayor is exactly the paperwork an FBI Section 13 file would flag; and the man he had appointed to keep the seat warm shoots himself within fourteen months.

PART E — INDIANA HARBOR AND THE MEXICAN COLONIA; THE EASTERN EUROPEAN PARISHES

La Colonia del Harbor — Indiana Harbor, East Chicago, 1919–1930

  • Where: Indiana Harbor (East Chicago), Lake County, Indiana; the "Block and Pennsy" streets around Pennsylvania Avenue; Inland Steel, Youngstown Sheet & Tube and the ship canal.
  • What happened: Mexican workers first came to the mills in numbers as strikebreakers and labour-shortage hires in 1919; by the 1930 census Indiana Harbor held 5,343 Mexicans (about 20 percent of its foreign-born white population), and Gary another 3,486 — in all 9,007 Mexicans in Lake County, out of 9,642 in the whole state. Some historians call it the most densely packed Mexican community in the country: about 5,000 people in under one square mile, known as La Colonia del Harbor. The colonia founded mutual-aid societies, a community newspaper, and a Mexican Independence Day tradition, and between 1922 and 1928 self-funded a church (the first of three buildings of Our Lady of Guadalupe), a community library, and a music and theatre programme. Labour recruitment from Texas to the Midwest was restricted by "Emigrant Agent" acts in the 1920s.
  • The odd detail: A colonia of 5,000 in a square mile that had its own library and classical theatre productions, self-funded, in a mill district with no city services worth naming.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://indianahistory.org/blog/by-the-train-loads-mexican-repatriation-movement-in-the-midwest-part-i/ (read) · https://indianahistory.org/blog/latino-arts-movement-of-the-early-twentieth-century/ (search-result summary only) · https://discoverindianahistory.org/items/show/604 (read)
  • Confidence: High on the census numbers; "densest Mexican community" is "some historians believe."
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in the setting the U.S. won the Southern War and annexed Mexico's mineral north, so many of the "Mexicans" in the mills are residents of ceded provinces (or arrive from an occupied Mexico), not foreign labour; the 1918 binational labor agreement the IHS page cites is a wartime one and the setting's version would be different.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A community that built its own library and theatre by 1928 is a community with its own priests, healers and curanderas — the IHS's Indiana Harbor desk would treat the colonia as an ally, and the Kabbalahs would treat it as a rival source of unofficial knowledge.

$15 a head: Lake County's relief board pays the fare home — 1931–1932

  • Where: Lake County, Indiana (Gary, Indiana Harbor); the Lake Superior Court (Crown Point).
  • What happened: By the county's own 1932 report, The Story of Unemployment Relief Work in Lake County, Indiana, repatriation removed 1,800 people from Indiana Harbor, 1,500 from Gary — 3,330 in all, including 250 children. The one-way rail fare to Mexico was $49; Judge Maurice E. Crites of the Lake Superior Court, a former East Chicago city attorney and Community Chest leader, negotiated a "charity rate" of $15 for adults and $7.50 for children, and the report claimed $21,000 a year in savings from repatriating the children. Walter J. Riley chaired the county relief committee. Newspapers presented it as a "relief program"; historians describe it as "subtly coercive," since being ineligible for relief rolls and withholding help from the colony pushed families to go. Over 34 percent of the Mexican population of northwest Indiana had left by 1932.
  • The odd detail: The county's accounting shows a price list: $49, $15, $7.50 — and the annual saving from sending children away.
  • People: Maurice E. Crites — judge, negotiator · Walter J. Riley — chair, Lake County relief committee.
  • Sources: https://indianahistory.org/blog/by-the-train-loads-mexican-repatriation-movement-in-the-midwest-part-i/ (read) · https://indianahistory.org/blog/by-the-train-loads-mexican-repatriation-movement-in-the-midwest-part-ii/ (read)

PART F — WHITING: STANDARD OIL'S TOWN

"An act of God": the Whiting still explosion — 4 July 1921

  • Where: Standard Oil (Indiana) refinery, Whiting, Indiana — Still No. 95 in the 8th battery of pressure stills; Passavant Hospital, Chicago.
  • What happened: At 8:15–8:30 a.m. on 4 July 1921, about 45 minutes before the town's Independence Day parade, a pressure still holding a couple of hundred barrels of oil at about 700 degrees Fahrenheit failed; a steel cylinder from the top flew 100 feet into another still, and oil and gas sprayed 200 yards, igniting on contact and catching four stills in two batteries. Eight men died and 44 were injured (24 seriously). Names of the dead include John Dzurovcak (32, a Slovak fireman with five children), Thomas Reed, Fred Harbrecht, Gustav Hokasson ("Gus Johnson," Swede), George Wildrick, Fred Engle, Frank Stout, and Joseph Paylo, assistant chief of the refinery fire department and a First World War veteran (his funeral on 7 July had the Standard Oil Band and 40–50 vehicles). Bodies were identified by clothing, watches and jewellery; wives crowded the refinery fence. The inquest (8 July, Coroner E. E. Evans) heard that the still was six years old and had been inspected 10–12 hours before, with pressure at 93–95 pounds; the coroner found it "one of those things that happen now and then due to no one's fault." Standard Oil called it "an act of God." The parade went ahead on schedule and the fireworks were fired that evening. The cost was about $2 million; the refinery had had no pressure-still deaths in eight years; 120 of the country's 700 pressure stills were in Whiting.
  • The odd detail: The company statement invoked God and his "mysterious way" for a six-year-old steel vessel operated by human beings — and the town held its Fourth of July parade forty-five minutes later.
  • People: John Dzurovcak; Thomas Reed; Fred Harbrecht; Gustav Hokasson; George Wildrick; Fred Engle; Frank Stout; Joseph Paylo — the dead · Walter Bertow — safety director · E. E. Evans — Lake County coroner · William M. Burton and Robert E. Humphreys — developers of the 1913 pressure-still cracking process.
  • Sources: https://www.wrhistoricalsociety.com/1921-explosion (read)
  • Confidence: High (a detailed local-society account with named victims; the "no pressure-still deaths" claim is the page's).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the 1921 date falls under President Wood in the setting, which changes nothing here).   • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The company blamed God and the coroner agreed. Father Chodniewicz shot for sacramental wine — Hegewisch, 22 January 1922 (living memory)

  • Where: St. Florian Rectory, 13145 S. Houston Avenue, Hegewisch, Chicago; St. Margaret's Hospital, Hammond, Indiana.
  • What happened: Rev. Hegewisch: Pullman's poor cousin becomes a Polish mill town — 1883–1930

Chanute's gliders on the dunes of Miller — summer 1896–1897 (living memory)

  • Where: The dunes of Miller Beach (now Marquette Park), Gary, Indiana — the 200-acre Carr family homestead.
  • What happened: In the summer of 1896, Octave Chanute (age 64), with the younger Augustus M. Herring and William Avery, tested a series of gliders in the Indiana dunes on the south shore of Lake Michigan: three new designs built in Chicago that summer, including a triplane whose bottom wing dug into the sand and was removed — leaving a biplane that made "hundreds" of controlled flights, the longest about 359 feet and 14 seconds. Chanute's chief innovation was a rigid truss structure, based on railroad bridge engineering, that let an engineer calculate the glider's strength; his tests continued in 1897. The design was the model for the Wright brothers' glider. (Wikipedia's Marquette Park page places the tests on the Carr family's land, 1896–97.)
  • The odd detail: The father of American aviation flew from the same dunes that U.S. Steel later shovelled into a mill's foundation — a few miles west of where the mill's concrete now stands.
  • People: Octave Chanute — engineer, organiser · Augustus M. Herring — pilot/co-builder · William Avery — builder and pilot · Robert and Druscilla Carr — landowners.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octave_Chanute (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marquette_Park_(Gary) (read) · https://www.britannica.com/topic/Chanute-glider-of-1896 (search-result summary only)
  • Confidence: High on the year and people; distances/times are from search summaries.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Chanute wrote up every flight; a log page from the 1896 dune camp records something else seen in the air over Miller — the sort of clipping a Section 13 archivist keeps, and a Golden Circle spy in the aero clubs would want.

Marquette Park, the Aquatorium and the steel corporation's gift of a beach — 1919–1924

  • Where: Marquette Park (Miller Beach), Gary, Indiana; the Gary Bathing Beach Aquatorium.
  • What happened: After Gary annexed Miller in 1919, U.S. Steel — which Wikipedia says "illegally claimed ownership" of the shore — "bought" 120 acres and gave it to the city as a park. George W. The bathing beach itself was contested: the Gary American campaigned to integrate Marquette Beach. The Aquatorium closed in 1971, was rescued in 1991 (the Chanute Aquatorium Society coined "Aquatorium," "place to view the water"), and restored in 1998–2000 as a museum of Chanute and the Tuskegee Airmen; NRHP listing 25 November 1994.
  • The odd detail: The land the steel company "bought" from itself to give to the city is the very ground Chanute flew on, now a museum to flight, in a building that closed for twenty years.

Wirt's Gary Plan and the New York school riots — 1907–1917 (living memory)

  • Where: Gary's Emerson School (the first Gary school on the "work-study-play" system) and New York City public schools.
  • What happened: William Wirt, Gary's first school superintendent, put the Gary Plan — the "platoon" or work-study-play system, in which pupils rotated among classrooms, shops and playgrounds — into practice from 1907. Mayor John Purroy Mitchel hired Wirt as a consultant in 1914 to introduce it to New York, and a Rockefeller-funded General Education Board study (Flexner and Bachman, 1917) examined it. A January 1916 Board of Education report found lower achievement; labour unions, parents and the Jewish community — who saw it as training children for factories — opposed it, and riots in 1917 killed it in New York and helped defeat Mitchel that November. By 1929, 1,068 schools in 202 cities were using versions of it, for 730,000 pupils.
  • The odd detail: A Gary school experiment that swept the country's largest city and ended in street riots by Jewish parents and pupils — and cost a mayor his job.
  • People: William Wirt — superintendent, designer · John Purroy Mitchel — New York mayor, defeated 1917 · John Dewey — philosophical inspiration.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Plan (read)
  • Confidence: High (Wikipedia; riot details from a search summary).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (New York 1917 is a domestic event; America is not at war, but that changes little here).

City Methodist and its pastor "Sunny Jim" Seaman — Gary, 1916–1929 (deepens R29)
