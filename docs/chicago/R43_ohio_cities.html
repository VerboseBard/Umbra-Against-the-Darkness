R43 — Ohio beyond Cleveland and Toledo, 1920–1940: the cities and their communities

Lane: Youngstown and the Mahoning Valley, Canton, Columbus, Dayton, Cincinnati, Lima, Sandusky and the Lake Erie islands, Marietta, Athens, Marion, the Ohio mound country, Ohio's Black communities and the Klan. Cleveland (R25), Toledo (R40), Akron (R07) and Anna Marie Hahn (R06) are elsewhere — cross-referenced, not repeated. Date of sweep: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 · Tools: WebFetch (Wikipedia, Ohio History Connection / Ohio History Central, Encyclopedia of Cleveland History, Chronicling America, university and state sites); no GPU, no git.

Overview

Thirty-seven entries on Ohio's other cities and communities, 1920–1940, plus living-memory anchors. The best single find for the setting is that the Knights of the Golden Circle's first "castle" and national headquarters was in Cincinnati (1854) — every player agency in the setting has an Ohio address (IHS at Cleveland, the Golden Circle at Cincinnati, and the 1864 Sandusky plot on the lakes — a Confederate espionage operation that federal agents tied to the Golden Circle's successor order, not a Golden Circle raid as such) ⟨fixed: V_R43⟩. The best disaster is the Ohio Penitentiary fire of 21 April 1930 — the cell keys were kept 900 feet from where the fire started (and were missing when guards went for them) ⟨fixed: V_R43⟩, the dead were buried by race in two cemeteries a mile apart, and the ground is now an arena. The best crimes are the Don Mellett murder (Canton, 1926) and George Remus's murder of his wife in Eden Park (Cincinnati, 1927; a jury out 19 minutes, then freed 4–3 by the Ohio Supreme Court from Lima State Hospital). The Klan material is new to the library: Niles 1924 (an anti-Klan order called the Knights of the Flaming Circle), the Buckeye Lake Konclaves of 1923 and 1925 (75,000 and nearly 100,000 people) and the Black Legion's Ohio home in Lima. Three entries are hard divergences (BENDS) that show the 1916 break at work on Ohio lives: Orville Wright's Flyer in a London museum, Harding's Marion, and Colonel Charles Young's 1918 ride. Lima's Dillinger jailbreak is R02's; Anna Marie Hahn is R06's; Cleveland, Toledo and Akron are R25/R40/R07's — cross-referenced, not repeated. Several sources refused the fetch (Ohio Memory, 10tv, asylumprojects, mariettaoh.net, gendisasters).

PART ONE — THE MAHONING VALLEY AND CANTON

East Youngstown burns — the steel-strike riot of 7 January 1916 (living memory)

  • Where: East Youngstown (today the city of Campbell), Mahoning County, Ohio — the north gate of the Youngstown Sheet & Tube plant on Wilson Avenue, then the village's business blocks.
  • What happened: Workers at Republic Iron and Steel struck first, demanding a raise from 19.5 to 25 cents an hour; Sheet & Tube's men followed days later, and around 16,000 people were out of work between the two plants. On Friday 7 January pickets gathered at the north gate; someone in the crowd reportedly fired a shot or threw a stone, and company-hired guards (described in one account as off-duty police) fired into the crowd — three dead and at least 27 hurt [UNVERIFIED — V_R43: the Vindicator page read says only "several fatalities and dozens of injuries"]. The village then burned. Accounts of the damage differ: one local account says about 100 business blocks and residences and $1 million; a centennial account says 34 buildings and $1.5 million [UNVERIFIED — V_R43: search summary; the Vindicator page read says "nearly 100 business blocks and residences" and $1,000,000]. Rioters cut fire hoses, burned company records at the headquarters, and burned all but two of the village's 20 saloons. The sheriff wired the governor: "Please send National Guard to village of East Youngstown at once. Lives are being taken and many portions of East Youngstown are being burned." Three regiments of the Ohio National Guard arrived about 3 a.m.; a summary of the centennial account says Gov. Frank Willis sent 24 companies. Workers went back within days at 22 cents an hour, and Sheet & Tube built four housing sites at about $20 a month.
  • The odd detail: The rioters burned the company's own records at headquarters — and eighteen of the village's twenty saloons.
  • People: Frank Willis — Ohio governor who called out the Guard · the anonymous shooter or stone-thrower at the gate — never identified in the accounts read · James Anson Campbell — Sheet & Tube's president, for whom the village was later renamed [renaming not confirmed on a page read this session].
  • Sources: https://vindyarchives.com/news/2016/jan/24/youngstown/ (read) ; search summaries of https://businessjournaldaily.com/symposium-commemorates-1916-east-youngstown-riots/ and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Anson_Campbell (not fetched).
  • Confidence: High on the event and the 7 January date; Medium on the damage figures (the accounts disagree) and on the count of dead.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (January 1916, before the break was final), happened; the war-orders boom that was starving the mills of labour holds — the war still happens and Britain and France still buy, whoever wins it.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Valley's Slovak, Hungarian and Italian parishes were the people in the gate. A ledger burned "by the mob" in the first hour is exactly what a conspiracy would want burned in a riot it also shot into — and the Guard, arriving at 3 a.m., is the only party that found the ashes.

The Klan comes to Niles — the siege of 1–2 November 1924 (also 1923 Youngstown Klan headquarters break-in)

  • Where: Niles (Trumbull County), Youngstown and Warren — the Mahoning Valley; the Niles rally ground at North Main and Federal streets.
  • What happened: By 1923 the Klan is said to have held the balance in mayoral elections in Warren, Niles and Youngstown, with roughly 10,000 Klan members in the Valley by the mid-1920s (search summary of a Tribune Chronicle piece — one source) [UNVERIFIED — V_R43: Belt Magazine, read, says "as many as 7,000" by early 1924]. In 1923 a break-in at the Youngstown Klan headquarters stole the membership record book [UNVERIFIED — V_R43] (search summary; the vtuhr.org article on the Valley's anti-Klan movement appears to be the source). The Klan marched downtown in Niles in May 1924 and tried again in June; Niles was targeted for its large Italian- and Irish-American Catholic population. A march was permitted for 2 p.m. on Saturday 1 November 1924. The Knights of the Flaming Circle — an anti-Klan order formed in 1923 in Kane, Pennsylvania, that admitted Catholics, Blacks and Jews — planned a counter-parade; a large armed force gathered in a park at North Main and Federal and set up blockades (Belt Magazine gives no head-count; Wikipedia says the order had pledged a 10,000-strong parade; the "1,700" in this chapter's first draft was the crowd at a 6:30 a.m. Mass at Our Lady of Mount Carmel, not the Flaming Circle) ⟨fixed: V_R43⟩. On the early morning of 29 October, Mayor Harvey Kistler's house was bombed after he refused to revoke the permit on First Amendment grounds. On the day, eight shots were fired from a passing car on Main Street and Frank McDermott was wounded; Gov. Vic Donahey called out the Guard at 1:16 p.m. (over 1,000 troops) and martial law followed an hour later; a train carrying 1,000 Klansmen arrived minutes afterward and left at once. Wikipedia's Niles article says "18 hours of full-blown rioting", ten days of martial law and 104 people indicted; the Belt Magazine account says no major charges came out of the grand jury. The Klan never paraded in the Valley again.
  • The odd detail: The Klan's newspaper in the fight, The Citizen, is given a 25,000 circulation — and the order that beat it in the street had a name, the Flaming Circle, that a Golden Circle reader will hear twice.
  • People: Harvey Kistler — Niles mayor, bombed for keeping the permit · Vic Donahey — Ohio governor who declared martial law · Frank McDermott — the wounded man on Main Street.
  • Sources: https://beltmag.com/fighting-the-klan-in-ohio-1924/ (read) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niles,_Ohio (read) ; https://www.tribtoday.com/news/local-news/2017/08/ku-klux-klan-came-to-valley-in-early-1920s/ (search summary only).
  • Confidence: High on the 1 November event, Kistler bombing and martial law; Medium-Low on the indictment figures (sources conflict) and on the 1923 record-book theft (one search summary).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (In the setting the Klan's second coming is canon-adjacent and tied to the Golden Circle, R10.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A counter-Klan order with a "Circle" in its name, formed in a Pennsylvania town in 1923, is a gift: a GM can make the Knights of the Flaming Circle the other Golden Circle front, arming both sides of the same street — and Niles's priests (the IHS's most natural neighbours, an hour from Cleveland) can be the ones who spot it.

The Klan's Ohio "Konclaves" at Buckeye Lake — 1923 and 1925 (and Dayton's one-in-five)

  • Where: Buckeye Lake, Licking–Fairfield–Perry counties, about 30 miles east of Columbus; Dayton's Montgomery County Fairgrounds.
  • What happened: The Klan held two enormous gatherings at the Buckeye Lake amusement park: over 75,000 people in 1923 and nearly 100,000 in 1925. The 1923 event had Imperial Wizard Hiram Wesley Evans and Indiana Grand Dragon D. C. Stephenson (the organiser), guides in cloaks and hoods, three bands, three crosses (the largest set ablaze) and the induction of about 1,700 new members, ending with fireworks that spelled an American flag overlaid with "KKK"; one Klansman died of a heart attack from the heat. The four-day 1925 event added weddings and a skit on moral decline, and denounced evolution after the Scopes trial. Stephenson was convicted within months — life sentence, 16 November 1925 — of the abduction and murder of Madge Oberholtzer (already in the setting, R06). The Klan's Ohio total is put at about 450,000 (search summary), with Akron said to hold the largest membership of any city by 1925 and Dayton put in the top six cities by share — 15 to 20 per cent of its population, with rallies of 10,000-plus at the old Montgomery County Fairgrounds (search summary; secondary) [UNVERIFIED — V_R43: none of these figures is on the Wikipedia page read]. Klan-endorsed politicians won offices but the order's influence had largely gone by 1928.
  • The odd detail: The 1923 induction of some 1,700 — three crosses, three bands, the largest cross burned — was staged at an amusement-park lake near the state capital, and its organiser was in a cell for murder two years later (R06, R07).
  • People: Hiram Wesley Evans — Imperial Wizard · D. C. Stephenson — Indiana Grand Dragon, organiser · Madge Oberholtzer — his victim.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ku_Klux_Klan_Konclaves_at_Buckeye_Lake (read) ; search summary: Ohio Klan pages on ohiohistory.org and spectrumnews1.com (not fetched).
  • Confidence: High on the two gatherings and headline numbers; Medium-Low on the Dayton 15–20% and Akron claims (search summary, secondary).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A hundred thousand people in one field, an hour from Columbus, in 1925 — the order's own membership book would be the most dangerous document in the Midwest. In the setting it is a Golden Circle recruiting list; the players find a page of it in a Youngstown fire.

The murder of Don Mellett, Canton's crusading editor — 16 July 1926 (living memory)

  • Where: Canton, Stark County, Ohio — Mellett's own garage; the Canton Daily News.
  • What happened: Donald Ring Mellett (born Elwood, Indiana, 26 September 1891) became editor of the Canton Daily News in January 1925 and began an anti-vice, anti-corruption campaign aimed at Canton's police chief, S. A. Lengel; he pressured the mayor to suspend the chief. On 16 July 1926, aged 34, he was shot dead in his garage. A bootleg-and-police alliance had hired an ex-convict from Pennsylvania, Patrick Eugene McDermott, who with Steve Kascholk was introduced through the bootlegger Ben Rudner to another bootlegger, Louis Mazer; McDermott was the triggerman. An outside detective, Ora Slater, is credited with solving it. A former Canton police detective was convicted as the plot's originator and implicated Chief Lengel, who — nearly a year and a half later — was acquitted; Wikipedia says four people, including Rudner, got life terms. The Daily News and Mellett, posthumously, took the 1927 Pulitzer Prize for public service.
  • The odd detail: The gunman was imported, an ex-con with no local ties — and the case was broken not by Canton's police but by a detective from out of town. Wikipedia's page (as summarised) says McDermott later escaped and appeared on an FBI Ten Most Wanted list — UNVERIFIED and hard to square with the list's 1950 start.
  • People: Don Mellett — editor, murdered · S. A. Lengel — police chief, acquitted · Patrick McDermott — triggerman, life sentence · Ben Rudner and Louis Mazer — bootleggers in the plot · Ora Slater — outside detective who broke it.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Mellett (read) ; https://www.kentstateuniversitypress.com/2010/murder-of-a-journalist/ (search summary of Thomas Crowl's book) ; https://ohiomysteries.com/ohio%20mysteries/1926-the-assassination-of-donald-mellett (page did not yield content).
  • Confidence: High on the murder, motive and the Pulitzer; Medium on the sentences and roles (sources word them inconsistently).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (a pre-1929 case, in the 1930s' living memory as the Prohibition-era newspaper martyrdom).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Bootleggers, a police chief and a Klan-adjacent town — Canton is where the Golden Circle's local face and the Black Hand's bootleg arm shook hands, and a dead editor's files (he was about to print names) are still in the Daily News morgue. Cleveland's IHS seat is an hour up the road; a young priest at John Carroll has a cousin on the paper.

PART TWO — COLUMBUS AND THE STATE'S BIG HOUSES

The Ohio Penitentiary fire, the deeper cut — Easter Monday, 21 April 1930 (extends R06 and R13)

  • Where: The West Block of the Ohio Penitentiary, downtown Columbus, east of Neil Avenue — the ground is now Nationwide Arena in the Arena District.
  • What happened: A candle set against oily rags on the West Block roof — the official story — started the fire that killed 322 prisoners (early reports said 317) and hospitalised about 230 (other accounts say about 130 injured). The prison held roughly 4,000–4,300 men in space for 1,500; the cell block beside the fire held about 800, most already locked in for the night [UNVERIFIED — V_R43]. Guards refused to open cells. The cell keys were kept about 900 feet from where the fire started, and were missing when guards went to get them (Marietta Times, quoting the 1930 press). ⟨fixed: V_R43⟩ Warden Preston E. Thomas told investigators he was more worried about escapes than the fire and relied on guard "common sense" rather than an evacuation plan. Some inmates overpowered a guard for his keys and freed others; the convict-writer Chester Himes — an East High School graduate and former Ohio State student doing time for armed robbery — watched men working "through the smoke with reckless haste" to save each other and wrote it up as "To What Red Hell" for Esquire. Five hundred soldiers from Fort Hayes with machine guns and orders to shoot to kill, and about 1,000 National Guard, ringed the walls; inmates stoned the arriving firemen. State Welfare Director Hal Griswold demanded, "Where were the keys to those cells?" and Gov. Myers Cooper faced calls to suspend Thomas; the prisoners refused to testify about how it started. The prisoners, segregated by race, refused hard labour for two weeks. Five days after the fire the unclaimed dead were buried — whites at Eastlawn Cemetery, Black inmates at Evergreen Cemetery a mile away.
  • The odd detail: The keys were 900 feet from where the fire started. ⟨fixed: V_R43⟩ And the deaths were sorted by race even in the burying — two cemeteries, one mile apart, one fire.
  • People: Preston E. Thomas — warden · Myers Cooper — governor · Hal Griswold — state welfare director · Chester Himes — inmate witness, later novelist · James Raymond, Hugh Gibbons, Clinton Grate — the three the prison blamed (R06) · William R. Noel — inmate who testified about the screaming.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_Penitentiary_fire (read) ; https://www.columbusnavigator.com/ohio-penitentiary-fire-1930/ (read) ; https://www.zinnedproject.org/news/tdih/ohio-state-penitentiary-fire/ (read) ; https://www.mariettatimes.com/opinion/local-columns/2026/04/the-way-i-see-it-fire-devastated-ohio-pen-in-1930/ (read) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_Penitentiary (read).
  • Confidence: High on the event, the 322 dead, the keys, the burial split and the troops; Medium on the injured count and the exact population.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The same prison put 315 men in its electric chair between 1897 and 1963, and Pierpont and Makley (R02) and Anna Marie Hahn (R06) ended there. Put the Death House and the West Block under one ground: something that feeds on the moment before a door is opened. A GM gets a prison-shaped haunting, a race-segregated burial trail for the Kabbalah/IHS to walk, and Company A of the 166th Infantry from Marietta (45 men, called out by the city hall fire bell on 29 April) as the ordinary boys who guarded the wreck.

Warren Harding's Marion — the tomb, the mistress who wanted no war with Germany, and a president who never was (BENDS)

  • Where: Marion, Marion County, Ohio — the Harding Tomb in Marion Cemetery; the Phillips house across the street from the Hardings' on Mount Vernon Avenue [address not confirmed this session].
  • What happened: Warren G. Harding died on 2 August 1923 at the Palace Hotel, San Francisco; his widow died 21 November 1924. A national competition in 1925 chose the design of Henry Hornbostel, Eric Fisher Wood and Edward Mellon — a circular Greek temple with 24 Doric columns and 22 inner Ionic columns, 103 feet across, open to the sky at the wish of the couple — built 1926–27 with about $978,000 from over a million donors, including some 200,000 schoolchildren's pennies. The bodies were moved from the receiving vault to the tomb on 21 December 1927, but the memorial was not formally dedicated until 16 June 1931, when President Herbert Hoover delivered the eulogy. The cemetery's other secret: Carrie Fulton Phillips (1873–1960), a Marion woman and Harding's mistress from 1905 to about 1920, who is said to have threatened in 1920 to expose the affair if Harding voted for war with Germany. The Republican Party paid for an extended trip through Asia and the Pacific and an annual stipend for life — "the first woman known to have successfully blackmailed a president." About 1,000 pages of their letters were sealed by the Library of Congress for 50 years and released on 29 July 2014.
  • The odd detail: In the real record the mistress is said to have threatened to expose the affair if Harding voted for war with Germany — a sentence that needs no help to become a plot in a world where Germany won and America never went to war at all.
  • People: Warren G. Harding — senator, later 29th president (real history) · Carrie Fulton Phillips — the mistress · Herbert Hoover — dedicated the tomb in 1931.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harding_Tomb (read) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrie_Fulton_Phillips (read) ; https://hardingpresidentialsites.org/harding-memorial/ (search summary).
  • Confidence: High on the tomb, dates and dedication; Medium on the Phillips blackmail detail (one summary of a Wikipedia article).
  • Divergence check: BENDS hard — in the setting Harding never became president (Marshall then Wood held the office 1916–27), so there is no Harding Tomb as such, no Teapot Dome, no "Ohio Gang" at the Justice Department, and no war vote to blackmail him over. What holds: he was an Ohio senator, from Marion, and Marion still has a very famous cemetery plot to fill. Flag for the owner: Harding as senator and then as something else (who ran the "Ohio Gang" in the setting?). Also: the memorial's 1931 dedicator, Hoover, is the setting's president too.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Carrie Phillips is the small-town Ohio woman said to have leaned on a senator about a war with Germany. In the setting the war came and America stayed out; the letters are a blackmail file about a man who did lobby — and the Golden Circle, which loves a Senate hearing (Col. Wood, 1865), needs them.

The Ohio mound country — Serpent Mound and the solstice lines

  • Where: Serpent Mound, near Peebles, Adams County, southern Ohio; the wider Adena, Hopewell and Fort Ancient earthworks of the state.
  • What happened: The Serpent Mound is 1,348 feet of coiled earthwork averaging about 4 feet high and 22–24 feet wide. Frederic Ward Putnam of Harvard's Peabody Museum raised the money in 1886 to buy 60 acres around it; in 1900 the land was granted to the Ohio State Archaeological and Historical Society (today's Ohio History Connection), which kept it through the 1930s. Builders and date are still argued: a 2014 radiocarbon study said Adena around 300 BCE with Fort Ancient repairs about 1100 CE; the archaeologist Brad Lepper disputes that and gives the Fort Ancient culture the leading role. In 1987 Clark and Marjorie Hardman proposed that the oval at the head aligns to the summer-solstice sunset and the coiled tail to the winter-solstice sunrise. The mound sits on the Serpent Mound crater, an impact structure 8.7 miles across formed 252–330 million years ago, and has drawn Garden-of-Eden and New Age readings.
  • The odd detail: The tail of the snake points at the winter solstice sunrise, and it sits on a crater of unknown origin — a natural hole in the ground under a man-made snake.
  • People: Frederic Ward Putnam — Harvard, saved it in 1886 · Clark and Marjorie Hardman — solstice-alignment proposers, 1987 · Brad Lepper — archaeologist, disputes the Adena date.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serpent_Mound (read).
  • Confidence: High on the site facts as listed; the 1930s Ohio Historical Society stewardship is inferred from the 1900 grant (not stated for the decade).
  • Divergence check: holds — pre-1916 landmark; nothing in it touches the break.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The owner picked the alpha's year so the December sky matched the plot — the winter-solstice sunrise (22 December in the alpha's year) lines up with the snake's tail. A cult (the Golden Circle's "map" people) or a Church man treating Ohio's mounds as the pre-Flood evidence for something buried is a natural scenario, and Ohio is IHS's own state.

PART THREE — DAYTON

"I'll kill you if you don't put them up" — Dillinger taken in bed at a Dayton boarding house, 22 September 1933 (also in R02; this is the Dayton-side cut)

  • Where: A boarding house on West First Street, Dayton, Montgomery County, Ohio.
  • What happened: Mary Longnecker, 23, the sister of one of Dillinger's Indiana prison friends, lived in Dayton, and Dillinger visited her at the boarding house through the summer. Pinkerton agents learned of the romance; with the landlady, Lucille Stricker, and Dayton police a trap was set. Stricker saw his car arrive and alerted police; at about 1 a.m. she knocked on Mary's door, Mary opened it, and Detective S. E. Yendes's squad came in. Officer Russell Pfauhl told the man in bed to raise his hands — "I'll kill you if you don't put them up" — and Dillinger, reportedly thinking they were rival gangsters rather than police, complied without a fight. The room held four pistols (one a Colt .38 Super semiautomatic), $2,600 in cash, rifle and shotgun shells, written notes on the fastest ways out of various cities, and sacks of carpet tacks meant to puncture pursuers' tyres. Judge Robert C. Patterson transferred him to armed guards for the trip to the Lima jail on the Bluffton bank charge — and the Lima jailbreak of 12 October (R02) followed.
  • The odd detail: The carpet tacks. The most famous fugitive in the country was arrested in a boarding-house bed with sacks of tacks and a notebook of getaway routes — and an escape plan already in his head for the Lima cell.
  • People: John Dillinger — the arrested man · Mary Longnecker — the girlfriend, 23 · Lucille Stricker — landlady who tipped police · S. E. Yendes — Dayton detective, leader of the arrest · Russell Pfauhl — officer who made the arrest · Judge Robert C. Patterson — ordered him held for Lima.
  • Sources: https://www.daytondailynews.com/local/ill-kill-you-if-you-dont-put-them-up-the-history-of-john-dillingers-1933-arrest-in-dayton/2FJEXEDOSBCVPA6YTS3AY5PHNQ/ (read) ; https://www.thedaytonmagazine.com/dillinger-dayton/ (search summary).
  • Confidence: High on date, place, seizure and the quoted line; Medium on Mary Longnecker's exact relationship to Dillinger's cellmate (sources word it two ways).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A Pinkerton-arranged trap in a boarding house — private detectives, a landlady informant, and a fugitive who thought the men at the door were rivals. The Secret Service (which took Capone) and Section 13 both want the "mob" cases to look like nothing but mob cases; a Dayton landlady is the perfect quiet witness.
  • Image: Wright State University's Dayton Police arrest photograph — https://corescholar.libraries.wright.edu/special_ms181_photographs/297/ (linked, not downloaded; rights per Wright State Special Collections, not verified).

The Wright Flyer in exile — why Dayton's first aeroplane spent 1928–48 in London (BENDS)

  • Where: Dayton (the Wright brothers' workshop; Orville still lived there); the Science Museum, South Kensington, London.
  • What happened: In 1928 the Smithsonian's new secretary, Charles Abbot, offered to revise a museum label crediting Samuel Langley's Aerodrome as "the first man-carrying aeroplane capable of flight" — but only if Orville Wright would publicly say he believed the Langley machine capable of flight [UNVERIFIED — V_R43: the Smithsonian's own page says only that Abbot offered to modify the label "not in confession of error, but in a gesture of good will"]. Orville took it as an insult and lent the original 1903 Flyer to the Science Museum of London that year. The Smithsonian did not apologise until 1942, when it reported that Abbot had tendered apologies to Orville for misleading statements by former officials. Orville died on 30 January 1948, and the Flyer came home and was unveiled at the Smithsonian's Arts and Industries Building on 17 December 1948.
  • The odd detail: In every 1930s year the machine that ended Dayton's claim to fame sat in a London museum, 3,000 miles from its makers, because of an argument about a label.
  • People: Orville Wright — surviving brother, Dayton · Charles Abbot — Smithsonian secretary, 1928 · Samuel Langley — Smithsonian's late secretary and rival aircraft builder.
