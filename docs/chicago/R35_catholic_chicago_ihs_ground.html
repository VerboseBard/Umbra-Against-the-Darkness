R35 — Catholic Chicago in the 1930s: the ground a secret Church order (IHS) would stand on

Completed 2026-09-27 by a follow-up pass; 26 entries; fact-checked 2026-09-27 (V_R35).

  • Lane: R35 — the Archdiocese of Chicago's buildings, clergy, relics, rites, institutions and secrets, 1929–1939 (with living-memory landmarks): the structure and size of the archdiocese, the cathedral and chancery, the cardinal's house, the old churches, the seminaries, the Jesuit college, the religious orders and their houses, hospitals, cemeteries, the Catholic press and charities, fraternal orders, the rite of exorcism as a 1930s priest would have had it, anti-Catholic flashpoints, and the Church's dealings with City Hall and the Outfit.

Overview

This chapter maps the ground an IHS agent would stand on in 1930s Chicago. It is built mostly on the Church's own annual register, the Official Catholic Directory for 1931 and 1938 (read in full-text OCR), and on the parishes' own histories. The machine: 1.25 million Catholics, 1,267 priests and 387 churches. Every official request went through the chancery at 719 N. Wabash, open nine to noon. It housed a canon-law court, a one-man anti-Modernist "Council of Vigilance" and nine book censors. The cathedral rectory billeted a five-priest Mission Band, and a named priest held the key to every jail, asylum and county ward. Exorcism: every priest was ordained an exorcist, but none could use the rite without the bishop's written leave. The dead are the richest vein. Holy Family has its seven lights, Damen's novena against "disturbing echoes" and its altar-boy ghost story, attested by 1923 and claimed for the 1870s. Mundelein founded St. Odilo in Berwyn in 1928 as "the nation's only shrine dedicated to the holy souls in purgatory." The Helpers of the Holy Souls nursed the poor for the dead's sake, and their chaplain was the priest who brought St. Anne's relic to Chicago. Bishop Duggan, declared mad by Pius IX, lay apart from his brother bishops. The orders' houses: enclosed Poor Clares came from Cleveland, the IHS city, after fleeing Bismarck. Sisters kept perpetual adoration at Mundelein. The Alexian Brothers ran a men-only hospital, and the register quietly listed "absent" priests there, plus one care of the French Legation in Kabul. Divergence flags: the Kulturkampf nuns, whose persecutor wins in the setting; the soldier-chaplains, whose war was Mexico, not France; and Old St. Patrick's 1922 MacSwiney window, a memorial to a death that Berlin's peace for Ireland probably prevented.

Part 1 — The machine: the archdiocese's size, offices and addresses (from the Official Catholic Directory)

Method note: the Boston Public Library's scans of the Official Catholic Directory for 1931 and 1938 are on the Internet Archive with full OCR text. I downloaded both texts this session and read the Archdiocese of Chicago section line by line. The OCR is noisy (names are sometimes mangled — e.g. "Shhil" for Sheil) and I give only what reads cleanly. This is the Church's own annual register, so it is the best available source for who sat in which office and at what address.

The archdiocese by the numbers — the 1931 register (the start of the decade)

  • Where: Archdiocese of Chicago — Cook, Lake, DuPage, Kankakee, Will and Grundy counties; 3,620 square miles (1938 heading)
  • What happened: The 1931 Directory's "Recapitulation" for Chicago lists: 1 archbishop (Mundelein), 1 auxiliary bishop (Sheil), 1 mitred abbot (the Benedictine abbot at Lisle); 767 secular (diocesan) priests and 500 priests of religious orders — 1,267 priests; 253 city churches and 134 country churches with resident priests — 387 churches; 395 diocesan seminarians plus 8 seminaries for religious orders with 602 students; 1 preparatory seminary (Quigley) with 920 students; 39 girls' academies and colleges; 19 boys' high schools and colleges with 6,940 students; 231 parochial grammar schools in the city and 106 in the country, 188,910 pupils in all; a school for the deaf ("School for Mutes", 122 pupils); 6 orphanages and industrial schools with 3,429 children, plus 1 orphanage for "Colored Girls" (91); 20 day nurseries and kindergartens; 20 hospitals; 3 communities nursing the sick in their homes. Catholic population: 1,250,000.
  • The odd detail: The Directory still printed a separate line for an orphanage for "Colored Girls" — segregation written into the Church's own ledger, in the same table as the mitred abbot.
  • People: George Mundelein — the archbishop; Bernard J. Sheil — the one auxiliary bishop in 1931.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/officialcatholic1931unse (full text: https://archive.org/download/officialcatholic1931unse/officialcatholic1931unse_djvu.txt , Chicago recapitulation just before "ARCHDIOCESE OF CINCINNATI")
  • Confidence: High (the figures read cleanly; the 160,580 city-pupil figure is on the line but its label is garbled, so I give only the total).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Scale for the GM: an IHS agent in Chicago stands inside a city of 1.25 million Catholics and 1,267 priests. The order does not need its own network; it needs to know which of 387 rectories to knock at.

The schools shrink — the 1938 register

  • Where: as above
  • What happened: By the 1938 Directory the city had 245 parochial grammar schools with 127,085 pupils and the country 119 schools with 27,328 — 154,413 in all, about 34,500 fewer than in 1931, even though the number of schools rose (from 337 to 364). Other 1938 lines: 297 diocesan seminarians; 12 seminaries for religious orders (491 students); Quigley's 650 students; 39 girls' academies (11,480 students); 19 boys' high schools and colleges (9,000 students); 151 country churches with resident priests; orphanages with 2,401 children; 4,500 adult converts in the year. (The priest and population lines of the 1938 recapitulation are illegible in the OCR.)
  • The odd detail: The Depression shows up as empty desks — more schools, fewer children — the birth rate falling faster than Mundelein could build.
  • People: Daniel F. Cunningham — superintendent of schools (1938 school board list).
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/officialcatholic1938unse (full text: https://archive.org/download/officialcatholic1938unse/officialcatholic1938unse_djvu.txt , Chicago recapitulation just before "ARCHDIOCESE OF CINCINNATI")
  • Confidence: Medium-High — the numbers read cleanly; the comparison is my arithmetic. Why enrolment fell is my inference (birth rate, tuition in hard times), not the source's.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the Depression is caused differently in the setting, but its effects on a parish school are the same).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Empty classrooms in a crowded parish are a GM's gift: a school wing closed since 1933, keys held by the pastor, where the order stores what it cannot put in a church.

The Chancery — 719 N. Wabash Avenue, open nine to noon

  • Where: Chancery Office, 719 N. Wabash Ave., behind Holy Name Cathedral (Near North Side); phone SUPerior 2315
  • What happened: The 1938 Directory gives the archdiocese's front door exactly: "Chancery Office: Chicago, Ill., 719 N. Wabash ave., open from nine A.M. to twelve M. every day, except Sunday. All official business should be directed to this office." The chancellor was Rt. Rev. Msgr. Robert C. Maguire (who also sat as the tribunal's promotor iustitiae and as treasurer of the Infirm Priest Fund); vice-chancellor Very Rev. Msgr. George J. Casey, J.C.D. Auxiliary Bishop Sheil, the vicar-general, kept his office at the same address. Nearby addresses in the same register: the Diocesan School Board and the Propagation of the Faith at 755 N. State; the cathedral rectory at 730 N. Wabash (phone SUPerior 8040); the cathedral school at 747 N. State.
  • The odd detail: The whole business of a million-Catholic archdiocese was open three hours a day, six days a week — a petitioner who came after lunch waited until tomorrow.
  • People: Robert C. Maguire — chancellor; George J. Casey — vice-chancellor and judge of the tribunal; Bernard J. Sheil — vicar-general.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/officialcatholic1938unse (Chicago section)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Any request an IHS agent makes "officially" — permission for an exorcism, access to a closed church, a sealed file — goes through 719 N. Wabash between nine and noon. What the chancellor does with it after noon is the question.

The Metropolitan Tribunal — the Church's own court in Chicago

  • Where: Chancery, 719 N. Wabash Ave.
  • What happened: The 1938 register lists the archdiocese's canon-law court (Tribunal Metropolitanum): Officialis (presiding judge) Msgr. George J. Casey, J.C.D.; vice-officiales Revs. Norbert E. O'Connell and John D. Fitzgerald; Promotor Iustitiae (the Church's prosecutor) Msgr. Robert C. Maguire; Defensores Vinculi (defenders of the marriage bond) Revs. Philip Furlong and E. M. Burke, J.C.D.; and a bench of a dozen Iudices Prosynodales (synodal judges). The same register lists a Consilium a Vigilantia (Council of Vigilance — the anti-Modernist watch committee every diocese had to keep after Pius X's 1907 encyclical) of one man, Rev. D. M. Thiele, and the Censores Librorum (see next entry).
  • The odd detail: Chicago's "Council of Vigilance" — an office created to hunt doctrinal error in the clergy — was one priest, Fr. Thiele, pastor on N. Claremont Avenue.
  • People: George J. Casey — presiding judge; Robert C. Maguire — prosecutor; D. M. Thiele — the Council of Vigilance.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/officialcatholic1938unse (Chicago section)
  • Confidence: High for the names and offices; the gloss on the Council of Vigilance's origin (Pius X, Pascendi, 1907) is general knowledge, not re-checked this session.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A real Church court with a real prosecutor and a one-man vigilance committee — IHS's natural cover inside the chancery. A priest summoned "to the Tribunal" is either in canonical trouble or being briefed; nobody outside can tell which.

The Censors of Books — who could say nihil obstat in Chicago

  • Where: Archdiocese of Chicago (names from the 1938 register)
  • What happened: Every Catholic book printed in the archdiocese needed a censor's nihil obstat before the archbishop's imprimatur. The 1938 Censores Librorum: Msgr. J. Gerald Kealy, Msgr. M. Klasen, Msgr. F. G. Ostrowski (pastor of St. Josaphat's), Rev. Samuel K. Wilson, S.J. (Loyola's president), Rev. John B. Furay, S.J., Rev. Matthew O'Neill, O.Carm., Rev. Reynold Hillenbrand (rector of the major seminary from 1936), Rev. Malachy Foley (rector of Quigley), Rev. John McMahon.
  • The odd detail: The men who licensed every Catholic book in Chicago included a Carmelite and two Jesuits — the censorship was spread across the orders, not kept in the chancery.
  • People: Samuel K. Wilson, S.J. — Loyola president; Reynold Hillenbrand — seminary rector; Matthew O'Neill, O.Carm. — Carmelite censor.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/officialcatholic1938unse (Chicago section)
  • Confidence: High for names (OCR-cleaned); Hillenbrand's rectorship date from general knowledge — UNVERIFIED this session.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A book that crosses a censor's desk and is refused leaves a record. The censors' refusals file is where IHS learns which Chicago Catholics are writing about things they should not know.

The Cathedral household — who slept at 730 N. Wabash in 1938

  • Where: Holy Name Cathedral rectory, 730 N. Wabash Ave. (the six-storey block of 1929 — R05); housekeeping sisters at 740 N. Wabash
  • What happened: The 1938 register lists the cathedral clergy (rector Msgr. Joseph P. Morrison; assistants George W. Moran, Edward V. Dailey — also the New World's editorial writer — David C. Fullmer, Joseph A. Wagner) and everyone else living in the rectory: the chancellor, the director of the Propagation of the Faith (Msgr. James J. Horsburgh), the rector of Quigley Seminary, three canon lawyers of the chancery, the director of the cathedral choristers (Rev. Edwin V. Hoover, Mus.D.), a Quigley professor, and the five priests of the Diocesan Mission Band (superior Rev. William E. Cousins), who travelled the archdiocese preaching parish missions. Ten Little Sisters of the Holy Family kept house. The cathedral's own grade school (301 pupils) and high school (99) stood at 747 N. State.
  • The odd detail: The priests who rode out to preach week-long missions in other parishes lived in the cathedral itself — a flying squad billeted at headquarters.
  • People: Joseph P. Morrison — cathedral rector; William E. Cousins — superior of the Mission Band; Edwin V. Hoover — choirmaster.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/officialcatholic1938unse (Chicago section, "CITY OF CHICAGO — Cathedral of the Holy Name")
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Mission Band is the perfect IHS cover — five priests with a standing reason to arrive in any parish for a week, preach hellfire, hear every confession, and leave. A second band of three Polish-speaking priests lived at the Felician convent, 3800 Peterson Ave.

The cardinal's house — 1555 North State Parkway (confirmed as his residence in the Church's register)

  • Where: 1555 N. State Pkwy, at the north end of the Gold Coast, facing Lincoln Park (cross-ref R19 and R09, which had the address from general knowledge)
  • What happened: The 1938 Directory gives Mundelein's residence as "Res., Chicago, Ill., 1555 N. State st." — so the address R09/R24 flagged as unverified is the Church's own. The house (built 1885 for Archbishop Feehan, per R19) was his town seat; his country seat was the villa at St. Mary of the Lake, where he died in 1939 (R05). The popular detail that the red-brick house has 19 chimneys is repeated widely but I could not reach a page that states it this session — UNVERIFIED.
  • The odd detail: The cardinal's front door faced the park and the lake; his office was a mile and a quarter south at 719 N. Wabash. Pacelli's overnight stay on 25 Oct 1936 (R24) was at one of the two houses — which one is still UNVERIFIED.
  • People: George Mundelein — resident 1916–1939.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/officialcatholic1938unse (Chicago section, the archbishop's entry)
  • Confidence: High for the address as his residence; the 19 chimneys UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Chimneys are ways in. A house with a legend of nineteen, and a GM who decides one of them does not connect to any fireplace.

Chaplains to the city's locked places — asylum, jail, penitentiary, sanatorium (1938)

  • Where: Cook County Jail and the House of Correction (26th St. & California Ave.); Dunning Asylum (Norwood Park); Joliet State Penitentiary; Kankakee and Manteno State Hospitals; Cook County Infirmary (Oak Forest); Municipal Tuberculosis Sanitarium (5601 N. Crawford); Cook County Hospital; the Juvenile Detention Home (Ogden & Roosevelt)
  • What happened: The 1938 register names who carried the sacraments into the public institutions: the County Jail was attended by "Father Ernest, O.F.M., of Corpus Christi Friary, 4920 S. Parkway"; the House of Correction and the Municipal Contagious Disease Hospital from St. Casimir's Church; Dunning Asylum by the Passionist Rev. Malachy Farrell, C.P.; Joliet Penitentiary by Rev. Eligius Weir, O.F.M.; Manteno by Rev. Terence J. Rice, C.S.V.; Kankakee from St. Viator's College; the Oak Forest poor-farm infirmary by three Franciscans; the TB sanitarium by two Viatorians; Cook County Hospital and the Juvenile Detention Home by the Jesuits of St. Ignatius College; Hines veterans' hospital by a Passionist.
  • The odd detail: The Jesuits of Holy Family/St. Ignatius on Roosevelt Road were the priests at the bedside in County Hospital's wards and at the juvenile lock-up next door — the city's poorest deaths and its youngest prisoners, one parish's job.
  • People: Father Ernest, O.F.M. — jail chaplain; Malachy Farrell, C.P. — Dunning chaplain; Eligius Weir, O.F.M. — Joliet chaplain.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/officialcatholic1938unse (Chicago section, "PUBLIC INSTITUTIONS")
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Every locked room in Cook County has a named priest with a key. The Dunning chaplain hears what the asylum's patients say at night; the Joliet chaplain hears the last confessions. IHS's best informants are these men.

The archdiocese's soldier-priests — chaplains to the Guard, the Army and the Navy (1938)

  • Where: Fort Sheridan; Illinois National Guard armories; the Naval Reserve
  • What happened: The 1938 register lists "Priests of the Archdiocese of Chicago serving as Chaplains": Rev. Benjamin J. Tarskey, U.S. Army, Fort Sheridan; Captain John P. Campbell, 124th Field Artillery, Illinois National Guard (living at Our Lady of Lourdes rectory, 4640 N. Ashland); Captain Martin Nealis, Illinois National Guard infantry (Our Lady of Solace rectory, 6214 S. Sangamon); Captain Francis J. Dolan, 202nd Coast Artillery (Anti-Aircraft); Lt. Cmdr. Thomas J. Burke, Naval Reserve (St. Hilary's, 5735 N. Fairfield); Lt. Col. Edward I. Dankowski, reserve chaplain (Bellwood); and Msgr. W. M. Foley, V.G., of St. Ambrose.
  • The odd detail: Parish curates who held captains' commissions — a priest who said the 6 a.m. Mass on Sangamon Street and drilled with an infantry regiment on the weekend.
  • People: John P. Campbell — Guard artillery chaplain; Martin Nealis — Guard infantry chaplain; Thomas J. Burke — Naval Reserve chaplain.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/officialcatholic1938unse (Chicago section)
  • Confidence: High for names and units as printed (the infantry regiment number is OCR-uncertain).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in the setting America never entered the Great War, so the older chaplains' war is the Southern War in Mexico (1916–17) and the occupation that followed, not France. A 1930s Guard chaplain who served in the occupation of northern Mexico or at Tampico would be a Catholic priest who ministered to an army occupying a Catholic country.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The soldier-priest is the IHS agent's natural bridge to the federal factions — and his memory of what the Army met in the Mexican hills is a mission seed.

Part 2 — Holy Family: the church the fire spared, and its ghost story

Holy Family Church and the seven lights — founded 1857; spared 8–10 Oct 1871 — LIVING MEMORY

  • Where: Church of the Holy Family, 1080 W. Roosevelt Rd (then 12th St.) at May St., Near West Side; St. Ignatius College next door
  • What happened: Founded in 1857 by the Dutch Jesuit Arnold Damen at Bishop O'Regan's request, dedicated 26 Aug 1860 (architect John Van Osdel); its 1874 tower (236 ft) was the tallest structure in Chicago until 1890, and by the 1890s it was the largest English-speaking Catholic congregation in the US, over 25,000 people, with a parish once reaching seven miles west to Austin. The O'Learys of 137 De Koven St. — whose barn was blamed for the fire — were parishioners (three children baptised there 1860–66). The fire started just across the parish line and the wind drove it east and north; the church and, the parish history claims, every parishioner's wooden cottage survived. Damen was preaching a mission at St. Patrick's in Brooklyn; the telegram reached him in the confessional; he prayed at the altar most of the night and vowed "for all time" to keep seven lights burning before the statue of Our Lady of Perpetual Help. Back in Chicago he charged the parish: "My vow must be kept … so long as this church stands." The candles proved too hard to keep lit at night and were replaced by gas jets shaped like candlesticks (later electric); they burn in the east transept today.
  • The odd detail: The 1923 parish history adds that Damen also held a novena "for the banishment of the disturbing echoes in the church" and set up a statue of the Virgin "for the protection of the east wall" — a Jesuit who prayed against sounds in his own building and posted a guardian on one wall.
  • People: Arnold Damen, S.J. (b. Leur, North Brabant, 20 Mar 1815 – d. Omaha, 1 Jan 1890) — founder; Catherine and Patrick O'Leary — parishioners; Br. Thomas M. Mulkerins, S.J. — parish historian (1923).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Family_Catholic_Church_(Chicago) ; https://holyfamilychicago.org/history ; Mulkerins, Holy Family Parish, Chicago: Priests and People (1923), pp. 102–105 and 136–137, full text https://archive.org/details/holyfamilyparish00mulk (the seven-lights passage quotes John Kelly in The New World, Oct 1921)
  • Image: the parish's photo albums (https://holyfamilychicago.org/the-side-altars) are the parish's own photographs — rights not stated; use as reference only.
  • Confidence: High for the church's history; the vow story rests on the 1921 New World article and on living parishioners' testimony printed in 1923 ("verified by many men and women still living").
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers: the period's reading (a pastor's vow answered), the folklore (the wind turned at the parish line), what is there (the seven lights are a ward, and Damen knew it — the echoes he prayed out of the nave and the wall he posted the Virgin on are the places something tried to come in). In the 1930s the lights are a Jesuit trust; an IHS agent would know which one has gone out.

The two altar boys — the Holy Family ghost story, and when it was first written down

  • Where: Holy Family Church — the two carved acolytes over the entrances to the sanctuary
  • What happened: Legend says (in the version printed in 1923): one stormy night two young boys rang the rectory bell and asked for a priest for an urgent sick call; the porter wanted them to wait until morning, but Damen, overhearing, went with them. They led him to a tumble-down house in a remote part of the parish and up to a garret, where a dying woman lay. She was astonished to see him — she had no one to send. Asked if she had sons, she answered: "None living … I had two boys who were acolytes of the Holy Family Church, but they are dead." She died before morning, and "the two statues, the story runs, were erected over the entrance to the sanctuary in memory of the incident."
  • The odd detail — the first attestation: The earliest written version I found is the parish's own 1923 centenary-era history, which calls it "one quite well authenticated tradition" and "one of many versions of this particular sick call story." It prints the testimony of a living parishioner, Mrs. John Griffin: "I heard that story some time in the seventies. I either heard Father Damen himself mention it, or heard that he mentioned it in his sermons from the pulpit." So the story was in oral circulation by the 1870s, possibly from Damen's own pulpit, and in print by 1923 — thirteen years before the setting's alpha. (I could not check whether an earlier printed version exists, e.g. in the New World or a Damen biography — UNVERIFIED.)
  • People: Arnold Damen, S.J. — the priest; Mrs. John Griffin — the 1923 witness; the two dead acolytes — unnamed in every version.
  • Sources: Mulkerins, Holy Family Parish, Chicago: Priests and People (1923), pp. 135–138 — https://archive.org/details/holyfamilyparish00mulk (read in full-text this session)
  • Confidence: High that the tradition is attested in 1923 and claimed for the 1870s; the event itself is folklore and is labelled so by its own source.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened (as a story).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The owner's three layers are already in the source: the Church's reading (Damen believed in the power of prayer), the folklore (the dead boys came for him), and room for what is actually there — dead acolytes who still serve at the altar of a church that kept a ward burning. An IHS priest in 1936 who hears the bell ring on a stormy night at 1080 W. Roosevelt answers it.

The altar carved by a cigar-store Indian maker — Anton Buscher, 1865

  • Where: Holy Family Church, main and side altars; Buscher's shop at 1123 S. May St.
  • What happened: The 1923 history (quoted on the parish site) says Anthony (Anton) Buscher, born in Baden in 1827, had tried farming in Kansas for six weeks, stopped in Chicago on his way back east, and set up at 1123 S. May St. carving wooden Indians for cigar-store signs. Damen saw the Indians being loaded into wagons, liked the work, and hired him to build the high altar: Gothic, 52 ft high, 30 ft 3 in wide, narrowing to a pinnacle one inch across; the altar stone rests on two brick columns built up from solid ground; the tabernacle "large enough to hold a dozen ciboria." Buscher carved its statues, St. Michael and the Guardian Angel in the vestibule, the confessionals' decoration and the pulpit; his nephew Sebastian carved the side-altar work (1873).
  • The odd detail: The same hands that made cigar-store Indians made the angels holding golden censers either side of the tabernacle.
  • People: Anton Buscher — carver; Sebastian Buscher — his nephew; Arnold Damen — patron.
  • Sources: https://holyfamilychicago.org/the-main-altar ; https://holyfamilychicago.org/the-side-altars (both quoting Mulkerins 1923)
  • Confidence: High (the parish's own text, from the 1923 book).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A carver whose first trade was figures that stood silent guard outside shops — and whose angels now stand guard over a tabernacle. A GM can let the Buscher statues move.

Part 3 — The seminary, the rite, and the order's tools

St. Mary of the Lake Seminary in the 1930s — the first pontifical faculty in America

  • Where: Mundelein, Lake County, IL (~40 miles NW of the Loop); the village was "Area" until 1924 — named, the seminary says, for a business school whose name stood for Ability, Responsibility, Endurance, Action
  • What happened: Site found 1918; lumberman Edward Hines gave $500,000; architect Joseph W. McCarthy (who designed 28 churches for Mundelein, 1916–45) drained the property and re-dammed the lake in 1920. Opened 5 Oct 1921 with 50 philosophy students and a Jesuit faculty under rector Msgr. J. Gerald Kealy (1921–36). In Sept 1929 Rome granted a five-year licence for a Pontifical Faculty of Theology; in Sept 1934 it became permanent — the first in the US under Deus Scientiarum Dominus — and the new auditorium brought the campus to 14 buildings. In 1936 Msgr. Reynold Hillenbrand became rector (liturgical movement, Catholic Action). The seminary also trained the priests of Springfield, Belleville, Rockford and Peoria. Mundelein died there, "in his home at the seminary," 2 Oct 1939, and lies behind the chapel's main altar (R05). The library is today's Feehan Memorial Library, which now keeps a museum of "hundreds of Church artifacts."
  • The odd detail: The Church's first American pontifical degree-granting faculty sat on a re-dammed lake in a village named after a business school's acronym.
  • People: J. Gerald Kealy — first rector, host of the 1926 Congress; Reynold Hillenbrand — rector 1936–44; Edward Hines — donor; Joseph W. McCarthy — architect.
  • Sources: https://usml.edu/history/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Saint_Mary_of_the_Lake
  • Confidence: High. (When the library building was completed and its 1930s holdings are UNVERIFIED — see Leads.)
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar; the pontifical licences come from a Rome that, in the setting, sits in a German-dominated Europe — the Congregation for Seminaries still functions.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A pontifical faculty can grant the Church's international degrees — the paper an IHS priest needs to be taken seriously in Rome. Mundelein is where the order would train its American novices without anyone asking why a seminary needs a lake, fourteen buildings and a cardinal's villa.

What a 1930s Chicago priest needed to perform an exorcism

  • Where: any parish; the permission came from the Chancery, 719 N. Wabash (above)
  • What happened: The rite itself was the one in the Rituale Romanum of 1614 (De exorcizandis obsessis a daemonio), unchanged in substance until 1999. Every priest had received the minor order of exorcist — the second of the four minor orders — on the way to ordination (the bishop handed him the book: "Receive, and commit to memory, and possess the power of imposing hands on energumens"). But by the Church's own discipline only priests used the power, and a solemn exorcism of a possessed person required the express permission of the bishop (under the 1917 Code, canon 1151 — the canon number is general knowledge, not re-checked this session). The Ritual's signs of possession: speaking or understanding unknown languages, knowledge of distant or hidden things, strength beyond one's age or condition, aversion to the holy. The 1909 Catholic Encyclopedia — the reference a 1930s priest owned — said possession had "become much rarer" in Christian countries and was mostly a missionary's problem, though "authentic cases of possession sometimes occur." In the region, the Earling case (Iowa, 1928; R05) was handled exactly this way: a Capuchin exorcist with the local bishop's leave.
  • The odd detail: Every priest in Chicago had been ordained an exorcist and was forbidden to use it without a letter from the chancery that was open nine to noon.
  • People: the Ordinary — Cardinal Mundelein, whose permission was required; Theophilus Riesinger, O.F.M. Cap. — the regional precedent (R05).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exorcism_in_the_Catholic_Church ; https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05711a.htm (Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909, "Exorcist")
  • Confidence: High for the Ritual, the minor order and the bishop's permission; canon 1151 by number UNVERIFIED here.
  • Divergence check: holds — this is Church discipline, not politics.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): This is the rulebook's IHS exorcist made real: the reason the order's exorcist "will want to know precisely how it got here" is that the cardinal's permission has to be asked for, in writing, and the answer is on file at 719 N. Wabash. IHS's advantage is that it can get the letter by nightfall.

The Catholic Order of Foresters — the Chicago-born fraternal insurance order (founded 1883)

  • Where: founded in Chicago, 1883 (today headquartered in Naperville, IL)
  • What happened: Disgruntled Catholic members of the (non-sectarian, Masonic-flavoured) Independent Order of Foresters split off to form two separate "Catholic Order of Foresters" — one in Massachusetts in 1879 and one in Chicago in 1883. The Chicago order became the national fraternal benefit society: life insurance for Catholic families, organised into local "courts," with "Forester" ritual and regalia in place of the lodges the Church forbade Catholics to join. (The Massachusetts sister order alone had 60,000 members in 1930.)
  • The odd detail: A Catholic "secret society" invented precisely so that Catholics would not join the real ones — the Church's answer to Freemasonry was an insurance company with a ritual.

  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Order_of_Foresters
  • Confidence: Medium — founding split confirmed; Chicago membership and the address of its 1930s headquarters UNVERIFIED (the order's own history page returned 404 this session).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened; holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Fraternal courts meet at night in parish halls with ritual, passwords and regalia — the one place a Catholic layman in 1930s Chicago learns what an oath feels like. IHS recruits its lay helpers from the Foresters' courts; the Golden Circle, which grew out of the nativist lodges (R10), knows how to counterfeit one.

Part 4 — The dead: the bishops' tombs, the Holy Souls and the relics

The bishop they left at Calvary — James Duggan, removed insane in 1869; and the Bishops' Mausoleum at Mount Carmel

  • Where: Calvary Cemetery, 301 Chicago Ave., Evanston (Duggan's grave until 2001); the Bishops' Mausoleum (formally the Mausoleum and Chapel of the Archbishops of Chicago), Mount Carmel Cemetery, Hillside (cross-ref R01, R09, which cover both cemeteries in general)
  • What happened: James Duggan (b. 22 May 1825) was Bishop of Chicago from 1859; he consecrated Calvary Cemetery himself on All Souls' Day, 2 Nov 1859. After the Second Plenary Council of Baltimore (Oct 1866) he began showing "mood swings, erratic behavior and signs of psychological stress"; in 1868 he dismissed four priests who had been his close advisers, and clergy wrote to Rome questioning his competence. On 14 April 1869 Pius IX ruled him no longer mentally capable and sent him to a sanatorium run by the Sisters of Charity in St. Louis. In Sept 1880, "sufficiently rational," he signed his resignation; he died in the same sanatorium on 27 March 1899 and was buried at Calvary. Meanwhile Archbishop James Quigley built the Bishops' Mausoleum at Mount Carmel (1905–12; exterior William J. Brinkmann, interior Aristide Leonori — marble, a domed tiled ceiling, crypts either side of the altar, mosaics of Celtic, Nordic and Slavic saints, the roof a stepped pyramid crowned by the Archangel Gabriel) and moved his predecessors' bodies into it; Quigley himself was laid there in July 1915. Duggan was not moved until 29 March 2001.
  • The odd detail: Through the whole of the 1930s Chicago's bishops lay together in Quigley's chapel on the hill at Hillside — except the one Rome had declared mad, who stayed alone in the lakeside cemetery he had consecrated on the Day of the Dead. (And Holy Name's ceiling was still bare: a cardinal's red galero is hung above the cathedral apse only after his death, "and stays there until it disintegrates" — Mundelein's, the first and now the centre of six, went up after October 1939.)
  • People: James Duggan — the mad bishop; Pius IX — who removed him; James E. Quigley — builder of the mausoleum; Aristide Leonori — its interior designer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Duggan ; https://drloihjournal.blogspot.com/2023/04/bishops-mausoleum-in-mount-carmel-cemetery-hillside-illinois.html ; https://www.chicagocatholic.com/chicagoland/-/article/2016/05/31/raising-the-hat (galeros)
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Bishops%27_Mausoleum_at_Mount_Carmel_Cemetery_Dec_2024.jpg — CC BY 4.0 (per R09; file page not re-read this session)
  • Confidence: High for Duggan's dates and the mausoleum; the date Mundelein's galero was raised is UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The period's reading: a sick man. The folklore: none yet — the GM can start it. Underneath: what did the bishop of Chicago see in 1866–69 that the Church needed him silenced, and why was he the one bishop not gathered in with the others? IHS would know which cemetery it keeps an eye on.

St. Odilo, Berwyn — the Shrine of the Poor Souls (founded 1928)

  • Where: St. Odilo Church, Clarence Ave. at 23rd St., Berwyn (today 2244 East Ave.); the parish began in a storefront at 22nd St. (now Cermak Rd.) and Clarence
  • What happened: In April 1927 Mundelein commissioned Fr. William J. Roberts to found a parish for fast-growing Berwyn; at Roberts's request it was dedicated to St. Odilo, the abbot of Cluny who began the feast of All Souls. First Mass 12 June 1927 in the storefront (seating about 200); cornerstone May 1928; the $150,000 "Northern Italian" building — church on the first floor, school above, hall in the basement — was dedicated by Mundelein on 4 Nov 1928, two days after All Souls' Day. At that first Mass the cardinal attached a 200-day indulgence, applicable to the souls in purgatory, to every visit to the church, founding the Shrine of the Poor Souls; the archdiocese's paper calls it "the nation's only shrine dedicated to the holy souls in purgatory." Today a side altar holds a book in which people write the names of their dead, remembered at the shrine Masses (whether the book existed in the 1930s is UNVERIFIED). In 1938 Roberts was still pastor, with one assistant, and B.V.M. sisters taught the school.
  • The odd detail: A church whose whole reason for being is the dead who have not yet got out — every visit is counted in days off purgatory, on behalf of someone else.
  • People: George Mundelein — founder of the shrine; William J. Roberts — founding pastor, 1927 to at least 1938; St. Odilo of Cluny (d. 1049) — patron.
  • Sources: https://www.saintodilo.org/history.html ; https://www.chicagocatholic.com/chicagoland/-/article/2019/11/06/berwyn-home-to-national-shrine-dedicated-to-holy-sou-1 ; Official Catholic Directory 1938 (https://archive.org/details/officialcatholic1938unse), Berwyn entry
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Berwyn is also where Capone's casino was raided by a minister in 1925 (R01). A shrine that exists to pray souls out is the first place a Chicago ghost would be drawn to — and the name-book is a register of the dead the living still care about. An IHS agent reading it would see a name written in a hand that stopped writing years ago.

The Helpers of the Holy Souls — nuns for the dead who nursed the living, S. St. Louis Ave. (1931–1938)

  • Where: Convent of the Helpers of the Holy Souls, 1800 S. St. Louis Ave. (West Side, North Lawndale; the 1938 OCR also reads "1300" in one place — number UNVERIFIED)
  • What happened: The Society of the Helpers of the Holy Souls (Auxiliatrices des âmes du purgatoire) was founded in Paris in 1856 by Eugénie Smet (Mary of Providence); seven Helpers sailed to New York in May 1892. The 1931 Directory lists the Chicago convent with its superior, Mother Mary of St. Catherine, "Supr. and Provincial" — the house was the seat of a province — and says the Helpers "take care of the sick poor in their own homes." In 1938 there were 17 professed sisters, and the register adds that the work was "gratuitous" and included religious instruction for adults and public-school children, children's clubs and sewing guilds. Their chaplain in both years was Rev. C. A. Poissant — the priest who had brought the first relic of St. Anne to Chicago in 1900 (next entry).
  • The odd detail: An order that offered all its work — nursing the poor, for nothing — on behalf of the souls in purgatory, and whose chaplain was the city's relic-bringer.
  • People: Eugénie Smet (Mary of Providence) — foundress; Mother Mary of St. Catherine — Chicago superior and provincial (1931); Cyril A. Poissant — chaplain.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_the_Helpers_of_the_Holy_Souls ; Official Catholic Directory 1931 (https://archive.org/details/officialcatholic1931unse) and 1938, Chicago "Religious Women" sections
  • Confidence: High for the register lines (the 1931 sister-count reads "414" in the OCR — almost certainly 14; not given as fact).
  • Divergence check: holds; the order's mother house is in Paris — in the setting, the capital of a defeated France paying reparations to Germany.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Sisters who enter the poorest West Side flats every day, uninvited by anyone but the sick, and pray for the dead as their rule — the best-placed witnesses in Chicago to a haunting in a tenement. The Kabbalahs' Chicago contacts (R34) and these sisters could be working the same Lawndale blocks.

The Shrine of St. Anne — Chicago's first shrine, and a bone relic from Apt (1900, 1916, 1937)

  • Where: St. Joseph's (French) Church and St. Anne's Shrine, 38th Pl. and California Ave., Brighton Park (2751 W. 38th Pl.); rectory on S. California; school at 2747 W. 38th Pl.
  • What happened: A French-Canadian parish of the 1880s (the parish's own history says 1880; first pastor Fr. Joseph LeSage); the first stone was blessed on 7 June 1891 and the church dedicated in May 1892. In January 1900 the second pastor, Fr. Cyril A. Poissant, "brought the first relic of the Saint from Monseigneur Marquis received from the Bishop of the Diocese of Nicolet, Quebec"; the first novena to St. Anne was held that July, and pilgrims came every year after. In April 1916 a bone relic was obtained from Apt, France, through Bishop Lenfant — "the largest after the one found in Beaupre Basilica." In 1937 Fr. Louis F. DeCelle built a new St. Anne sanctuary, and in May 1938 he added a novena to Our Lady of Sorrows. The 1938 register lists the parish as "St. Joseph's (French), St. Anne's Shrine," with DeCelle as pastor, two assistants and 331 pupils taught by Sisters of St. Joseph from Concordia, Kansas.
  • The odd detail: A saint's bone that left France in April 1916 — in the middle of the war that, in the setting, France went on to lose. (A 1900 Tribune report of a paralysed young woman, Matilda Cunnea, rising from her invalid chair after venerating the relic, with her wheelchair still displayed in the church, appears in a search summary of a Chicago Ancestors page I could not open — UNVERIFIED.)
  • People: Cyril A. Poissant — relic-bringer, later chaplain to the Helpers of the Holy Souls; Louis F. DeCelle — pastor who built the 1937 sanctuary; Joseph LeSage — first pastor.
  • Sources: https://oladyf.weebly.com/history.html (the consolidated parish's own history) ; Official Catholic Directory 1938, Chicago parish no. 127
  • Confidence: Medium-High (the parish's own history; the relic's identity as "bone" and its size ranking are the parish's claims).
  • Divergence check: holds — the April 1916 relic predates the break; its passage from wartime France across the Atlantic happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A relic that crossed an ocean at war and a pastor who rebuilt its sanctuary in 1937 — was the new shrine built because the old one had stopped working? IHS keeps a list of the relics in Chicago that do something; this is on it.

St. John Cantius — the Polish baroque church whose famous relic room is later than the 1930s

  • Where: St. John Cantius Church, Carpenter St. and Chicago Ave. (rectory 825 N. Carpenter St.), West Town
  • What happened: Founded in 1893 by the Congregation of the Resurrection; cornerstone laid July 1893; Adolphus Druiding's Polish-baroque church was dedicated by Archbishop Feehan on 11 Dec 1898. In 1918 it had about 23,000 parishioners and 2,500 children in its school. By the 1938 register the pastor was Rev. Theodore S. Klopotowski, C.R., with three Resurrectionist assistants, and the school, kept by School Sisters of Notre Dame at 1025 Fry St., had 679 pupils. The church keeps a nineteenth-century copy of the icon of Our Lady of Częstochowa. Its relic collection — "several hundred authenticated relics" on show in the north tower, about 1,800 in all by recent counts — was given mostly by priests and is associated with the Canons Regular who took over the parish in modern times; I found no evidence that it existed in the 1930s (dates UNVERIFIED).
  • The odd detail: The school fell from about 2,500 children in 1918 to 679 in 1938 — the biggest drop of any parish in this file.
  • People: Theodore S. Klopotowski, C.R. — pastor in 1938; Adolphus Druiding — architect.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St.John_Cantius_Church(Chicago) ; Official Catholic Directory 1938, Chicago parish no. 122
  • Confidence: High for the 1893–1938 facts; the relic collection's dates UNVERIFIED — a GM should not put the modern relic room in 1936.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): For a 1936 story, the relics belong somewhere else — St. Anne's shrine (above) is the documented one. Cantius is better used for its half-empty school wing (see "the schools shrink", Part 1).

Part 5 — The houses of the orders

The Poor Clare Colettines at 53rd and Laflin — an enclosed monastery founded from Cleveland (1893)

  • Where: Monastery of the Poor Clares, 53rd St. and Laflin Ave., Back of the Yards (south-west of the stockyards; the Franciscan friary of St. Augustine's was at 50th and Laflin)
  • What happened: The Colettine Poor Clares' first American house was in Cleveland (1877). Its German founders had fled to Holland after Bismarck's government disbanded their Düsseldorf convent in 1875, joining two Italian sisters, the Bentivoglios; Cleveland in turn founded houses in Chicago, Rockford, Oakland, Newport News and Brazil. The Chicago monastery on Laflin St. dates from 1893. The nuns took vows of poverty, chastity, obedience and enclosure; a small group of "extern" sisters dealt with the world outside. The 1931 Directory: "Public Chapel of Perpetual Adoration in connection. Sisters, 42; Novice, 1; Postulants, 5. Extern Sisters, 7; Postulants, 2. Rev. Mother M. Coletta, Abbess." A Franciscan at the friary, Rev. Giles Strub, O.F.M., was "Director of the League of Adorers at Poor Clares Monastery." By 1938: 35 sisters, 9 externs, 1 postulant, Rev. Mother M. Joseph superior. They left for Hickory Hills in the 1960s.
  • The odd detail: Forty-odd women who never came out, keeping the Sacrament watched day and night in a public chapel within a mile of the killing floors of the Union Stock Yards.
  • People: Mother M. Coletta — abbess (1931); Mother M. Joseph — superior (1938); Giles Strub, O.F.M. — director of the League of Adorers.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/p/poor-clare-colettine-religious (Encyclopedia of Cleveland History) ; https://www.chicagocatholic.com/chicagoland/-/article/2012/03/25/they-carry-on-800-years-of-prayer-and-work ; Official Catholic Directory 1931 and 1938, Chicago sections
  • Confidence: High for the dates and numbers; that Archbishop Feehan dedicated the building in person appears only in a search summary — UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the founders were refugees from Bismarck's Kulturkampf; in the setting the Protestant-led empire that expelled them won the war and now dominates Rome's Europe. A Colettine who remembers Düsseldorf reads the news from Berlin as a personal matter.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The IHS seat is in Cleveland, and Chicago's enclosed nuns came from Cleveland — the one religious house in Chicago with a direct line to the order's American home. Enclosed nuns see no one and pray all night; the externs carry messages.

The watchers — where the Sacrament was never left alone (1930s)

  • Where: the Poor Clares' public chapel, 53rd and Laflin (above); the Benedictine Convent of Perpetual Adoration, Mundelein (Park and Sayre Aves. as printed); St. Francis Hospital, 355 Ridge Ave., Evanston; St. James' Hospital, 1423 Chicago Rd., Chicago Heights

The Alexian Brothers' hospital — 1200 W. Belden Avenue, men only, nursed by the heirs of the plague-buriers

  • Where: Alexian Brothers Hospital, 1200 W. Belden Ave. (at Racine/Lakewood), Lincoln Park–Sheffield; also the American provincial house and novitiate of the order
  • What happened: The Alexians began in 14th-century Flanders as laymen who nursed the victims of the Black Death "and buried the deceased"; they were also called Cellites, from the cells they lived in. Their Chicago hospital (1866) was destroyed in the fire of 9 Oct 1871, rebuilt the next year, and moved in 1897 to Belden and Lakewood when the new elevated displaced it from Old Town. It was a hospital for men, nursed by brothers. The 1931 register: provincial Brother Frumentius Horn; 54 brothers, 18 novices, 22 postulants; a "Training School for Male Nurses" (40 pupils); 3,765 patients that year; a Precious Blood chaplain. By 1938: 60 professed brothers, 27 novices, 11 postulants; the school of nursing "exclusively for members of the Novitiate" (45 pupils); 3,116 patients. The 1931 and 1938 registers also list diocesan priests "absent" and living at the Alexian hospitals in Chicago and St. Louis (next entry). The sister hospital in St. Louis (founded 1869), which trained male nurses 1928–52 and took only men until 1962, is where, the hospital later claimed, part of the 1949 exorcism behind The Exorcist took place — outside the window, but it is the same order.
  • The odd detail: The body of John Dillinger was taken to this hospital from the Biograph alley on 22 July 1934 (R02, R17) — to brothers whose order was founded to carry out the dead.
  • People: Frumentius Horn — provincial (1931); Vigilius Weaver — local superior and master of novices (1938); John Blackledge, R.N. — director of the nursing school (1938).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexians ; https://www.sheffieldneighborhood.org/history ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St.Alexius_Hospital(Missouri) ; Official Catholic Directory 1931 and 1938, Chicago "Hospitals" sections
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.

Part 6 — The press and the Irish glass

The New World — the archdiocese's newspaper, 109 N. Dearborn St. (founded 1892)

  • Where: The New World Publishing Co., 109 N. Dearborn St., the Loop — the same address as the office of the Diocesan Catholic Cemeteries
  • What happened: Archbishop Patrick Feehan founded the paper with a Catholic Press Company capitalised at $20,000 from himself and about 40 pastors; he took the name from the coming World's Columbian Exposition's theme of America as the New World. The first issue (the archdiocese's own articles give both 8 and 10 Sept 1892) cost 5 cents, ran eight pages, and carried a pastoral letter, Fair stories and a report on Protestantism's spread in Scandinavia. Early editors were laymen: John Hyde, then William J. Dillon (1893–1902), who sided with labour. The 1938 register: "Official Catholic Newspaper of the Archdiocese of Chicago and Province of Illinois; Circulation, 53,134. Founded in 1892. Managing Editor, Rev. Timothy Rowan. Rev. Edward V. Dailey, Editorial Writer" — Dailey lived in the cathedral rectory (Part 1). The same register gives the Diocesan Catholic Cemeteries at 109 N. Dearborn too, under a priest superintendent (the name reads "William P. Casny" in the OCR — probably Casey; UNVERIFIED) and his assistant Rev. Joseph B. Heldmann. It was The New World that printed John Kelly's 1921 account of Damen's seven lights (Part 2).
  • The odd detail: The Church's newspaper and the office that sold its graves shared one Loop address.
  • People: Patrick Feehan — founder; Timothy Rowan — managing editor (1938); Edward V. Dailey — editorial writer (1938).
  • Sources: https://www.chicagocatholic.com/chicagoland/-/article/2017/09/06/a-look-back-at-our-history ; https://www.chicagocatholic.com/chicagoland/-/article/2017/01/15/sept-1892-first-issue-of-the-new-world ; Official Catholic Directory 1938, Chicago "Diocesan Publications" and diocesan offices

Old St. Patrick's and the MacSwiney window — a hunger-striker in 250,000 pieces of glass (1912–1922)

  • Where: Old St. Patrick's Church, Adams and Des Plaines Sts., West Loop
  • What happened: Founded Easter Sunday, 12 April 1846 as Chicago's first English-speaking parish; the Romanesque church of Milwaukee Cream City brick (architects Augustus Bauer and Asher Carter) was begun on 23 May 1853 and dedicated Christmas Day 1856 — one of the few buildings to survive the 1871 fire, the city's oldest standing church; its two octagonal spires (1885) are said to stand for the Eastern and Western Churches. From 1912 to 1922 Thomas A. O'Shaughnessy (1870–1956), inspired by the Celtic exhibit at the 1893 Fair and the Book of Kells, stencilled the interior in interlace and made 15 windows. The last was the central panel of the Faith, Hope and Charity triptych, completed in 1922 as a memorial to Terence MacSwiney, the Lord Mayor of Cork who died on hunger strike in Brixton Prison in 1920: more than 250,000 pieces of glass in 2,000 colours and no paint. It shows MacSwiney rising to heaven among angels, with the chaos of the world below in the side panels ("rising out of the flames of purgatory" in one description — UNVERIFIED). By the Depression the parish was a downtown church losing its people; by 1950 the building was in danger of demolition.
  • The odd detail: An Irish political martyr set in the window of a Chicago church two years after he starved to death — "this window explicitly aligns the church with Ireland and Irish nationalist activity."
  • People: Thomas A. O'Shaughnessy — artist; Terence MacSwiney — the man in the window.
  • Sources: https://www.oldstpats.org/history.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_A._O%27Shaughnessy ; https://www.creativecentenaries.org/blog/remembering-terence-macswiney-in-the-irish-diasporic-built-environment
  • Confidence: High for the church and the window's facts.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in the setting Ireland was freed by Berlin's peace, so MacSwiney's 1920 death in a British prison probably never happened. The owner must decide whose memorial the 1922 window is (a hero of a German-backed Irish freedom? the dead of 1916?) — and an Irish-Chicago church celebrating a freedom Germany delivered is itself a "patron of other people's revolutions" thread.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A quarter of a million pieces of glass without a brushstroke of paint — a window you could hide something in. IHS would know who paid for each of the fifteen windows, and why the last one took until 1922.
