Overview

73 entries across the ring, organised by city/region (the file's section order) and indexed by theme below. The strongest material for this setting is where the 1916 break bites: Akron had a real German–American airship partnership (Goodyear-Zeppelin, 1923/24–1940, with Zeppelin holding a third and twelve Zeppelin engineers in Akron) that built the Navy's two giant airships in the Airdock — a building that makes its own rain — and both ships were lost within two years. Milwaukee was a quarter German-born in 1900 and lost its German schools, stage and clubs only because America entered the war in 1917 — in the setting that backlash never happens, so the 1930s city would still be bilingual. Cleveland, the IHS seat, gives the setting's John Carroll University a startling real history: founded by the German Province Jesuits' Buffalo Mission, it built its new campus 1931–35, and during the Depression halt an old Jesuit seismologist lived alone in the unfinished Grasselli Tower with a dog and a canary, guarding the instruments of a continent-wide Jesuit earthquake network; two weeks before the campus opened, half a million Catholics came to Cleveland for a Eucharistic Congress and marched in the shape of a monstrance. Detroit supplies the decade's labour violence (Hunger March, Overpass, Flint), the Black Legion (already in the setting) with a Highland Park mayor and a milk inspector among its members, the Purple Gang's Valentine's Day tip, a prophet deported by train whose last letter came from Mexico, and Joe Louis versus Germany's Schmeling. The lakes add the autumn of 1929 (three ships lost in seven weeks, one on the night of Black Tuesday), a captain vanished from a locked cabin, and a stewardess's note from Isle Royale. Ontario adds the Christie Pits swastika riot (in a no-Nazi world, the swastika becomes an occult sign), the Dionne quintuplets behind one-way glass, a radio-preaching bandit, and Grey Owl. Cross-references: R02 (Dillinger, Little Bohemia, the St. Paul kidnappings), R05 (Coughlin, cults), R06 (Torso Murders). AKRON — the rubber city and the German airship yard

The Goodyear-Zeppelin Corporation and the Akron Airdock — 1923/24 to 1940/41

  • Where: Akron, Ohio — the Airdock at the Akron Municipal Airport (south-east Akron); Goodyear's offices on East Market Street.
  • What happened: Goodyear executive William C. Young visited Friedrichshafen in October 1922 to propose a joint venture to Hugo Eckener; the Goodyear-Zeppelin Corporation was formally created 1923–24, with Goodyear holding two-thirds and Luftschiffbau Zeppelin one-third, and Goodyear getting the use of Zeppelin patents. In November 1924 Dr. Karl Arnstein — Zeppelin's chief stress engineer, a Prague-born Jew — arrived in Akron as vice-president of engineering with a team of 12 hand-picked Zeppelin engineers; the Zeppelin captain Ernst Lehmann also went to Akron. To build the Navy's two giant rigid airships the company raised the Airdock (built 20 April – 25 November 1929, $2.2 million): 1,175 ft long, 325 ft wide, 211 ft high, 13 steel arches, 364,000 sq ft of floor with no interior supports — the largest such building in the world at completion, its design inspired by a 1913 Dresden hangar. Each end closes with two semi-spherical "orange-peel" doors of 600 tons each, running on 40 wheels on curved rails. The partnership frayed after 1939 and was dissolved in December 1940 (Goodyear's own history says 1941).
  • The odd detail: The building makes its own weather — when humidity is high and the temperature changes suddenly, condensation falls inside as a fine mist, "the illusion of rain," per the designer.
  • People: Karl Arnstein — Prague-born Jewish Zeppelin engineer, VP of engineering, designer of the Akron and Macon; Paul W. Litchfield — Goodyear president, champion of the rigid airship; Hugo Eckener — head of the Zeppelin company; Ernst Lehmann — Zeppelin captain sent to Akron; William C. Young — Goodyear man who proposed the deal in 1922.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodyear_Airdock ; https://www.airships.net/blog/goodyear-zeppelin-70-years/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodyear_Aerospace
  • Confidence: High on Airdock figures and the 2/3–1/3 split (one search summary claimed Zeppelin held 66% — that is contradicted by Goodyear Aerospace's article; use 2/3 Goodyear). Dissolution date differs by source (Dec 1940 vs 1941).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in a world where Imperial Germany won the Great War, the Zeppelin company is a victor's national champion, not a defeated firm hungry for foreign money; its 1/3 stake, the patent licence and a dozen German engineers inside a US Navy contractor would read as a German foothold in American military aviation. Whether the deal happens at all, and on whose terms, is the owner's call.
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Goodyear_Airdock_exterior.jpg (exterior) ; https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Macon_construction_struct.jpg (USS Macon under construction inside the Airdock, 1932 — likely US Navy, public domain; verify on file page)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Airdock as a German enclave in the Ohio heartland — twelve "Zeppelin men" with Berlin's blessing, a building with its own indoor rain, and a Navy that needs them. Section 13 would want eyes inside; the Golden Circle would want the patents.

USS Akron — christened 8 August 1931; lost 4 April 1933

  • Where: Built in the Airdock, Akron; lost in the Atlantic off the New Jersey coast.
  • What happened: Construction began on 31 October 1929 ⟨fixed: V_R07⟩. First Lady Lou Henry Hoover christened the Akron on 8 August 1931; first flight 23 September 1931. It carried F9C Sparrowhawk biplane fighters, launched and caught in flight on a trapeze. On 11 May 1932, mooring at Camp Kearny near San Diego, the ship rose uncontrollably; the mooring cable was cut and four sailors held on — one dropped at about 15 ft with a broken arm, Aviation Carpenter's Mate Robert H. Edsall and Apprentice Seaman Nigel M. Henton fell to their deaths, and Apprentice Seaman C. M. "Bud" Cowart lashed himself to the line and was hauled in an hour later; the scene was filmed and shown in newsreels nationwide. In a violent thunderstorm around 00:23 on 4 April 1933 the Akron struck the sea: 73 of 76 died, including Rear Admiral William A. Moffett, chief of the Navy's Bureau of Aeronautics. The Navy blimp J-3, sent to search, also crashed, killing two more.
  • The odd detail: The Akron carried an experimental "spy basket" — a subcloud observation car lowered on a cable so the hidden ship could see below the overcast; in trials it swung so violently it was called frighteningly unstable.
  • People: Lou Henry Hoover — christened her; Rear Adm. William A. Moffett — father of naval aviation, killed; Lt. Cdr. Herbert V. Wiley — executive officer, survived; Robert H. Edsall and Nigel M. Henton — fell at Camp Kearny; C. M. "Bud" Cowart — the man who hung on.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Akron_(ZRS-4) ; https://www.airships.net/us-navy-rigid-airships/uss-akron-macon/
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Lou Hoover is still First Lady in the setting: Hoover 1929–33). The German-built lineage BENDS as above.
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:USS_Akron_under_construction,_nov_1930.jpg (in the Airdock, Nov 1930) ; https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Akron_incident_11_may32.jpg (stills of Seaman Cowart on the line, Camp Kearny) ; https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:USS_Akron_over_Manhattan.jpg — US Navy photographs, public domain (verify on file pages)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A ship designed by German engineers, christened by the President's wife, that swallows an admiral and 72 men in a thunderstorm — and the rescue blimp falls too. The spy basket, dangling a lone observer below the clouds, is a perfect set-piece for something seen that no one believes.

USS Macon — christened 11 March 1933; lost 12 February 1935

  • Where: Built in the Airdock, Akron; based at Moffett Field, Sunnyvale, California; lost off Point Sur.
  • What happened: Jeanette Whitton Moffett, the admiral's wife, christened the Macon on 11 March 1933 — three weeks before her husband died in the Akron. First flight 21 April 1933 over northern Ohio with 105 aboard. Its home field in California was renamed for the dead admiral. On 12 February 1935 wind shear broke the upper fin's attachment (an unstrengthened ring, frame 17.5) and debris punctured the rear gas cells; the ship came down at sea. 81 of 83 survived: Radioman 1st Class Ernest Edwin Dailey jumped from too high, and Mess Attendant 1st Class Florentino Edquiba drowned going back for belongings. The wreck, with four Sparrowhawks, was found by the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute in February 1991 at about 1,500 ft.
  • The odd detail: The widow christened the ship; the ship's home field took the husband's name; the second giant was lost within two years of the first.
  • People: Jeanette Whitton Moffett — christened her; Ernest Edwin Dailey and Florentino Edquiba — the two dead (Edquiba a Filipino mess attendant — the Philippines still a US possession in the setting).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Macon_(ZRS-5) ; https://www.airships.net/us-navy-rigid-airships/uss-akron-macon/
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar; BENDS only through the German-engineering lineage.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Two sister ships from one German-built shed, both lost to "weather." A GM could make the Airdock's indoor rain and the ships' storms the same thing.

The Akron rubber strike — the first great sit-down — 14 February – 22 March 1936

  • Where: Goodyear Tire & Rubber, Plant 2, Akron; the picket line around the whole Goodyear complex.
  • What happened: Goodyear had been running four six-hour shifts and planned to switch to three eight-hour shifts without more pay, laying off about 700 (up to 1,200 under the plan). At 3:10 a.m. on 14 February 1936, 137 tire builders in Department 251-A of Plant 2 simply sat down at their machines. Management fired them; by the 17th all the plant's workers voted to strike and the whole complex shut. [UNVERIFIED — V_R07] Up to 10,000 picketed; the sheriff swore about 150 deputies, and a "Law and Order League" of roughly 5,200 citizens formed to break it. The United Rubber Workers (founded September 1935, president Sherman Dalrymple) won a settlement on 21 March — layoffs by seniority, the six-hour shift kept, regular management–union meetings; the cited page lists no formal recognition ⟨fixed: V_R07⟩; 5,000 marched to celebrate. No one died.
  • The odd detail: The tactic, according to the standard account, was invented at a union baseball game when players refused to play under a non-union umpire and sat down on the field.
  • People: Paul W. Litchfield — Goodyear president; Sherman Dalrymple — URW president; the 137 men of Dept. 251-A.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1936_Akron_rubber_strike ; https://www.zinnedproject.org/news/tdih/akron-rubber-strike/ (search summary only)
  • Confidence: High on dates/numbers; Medium on the baseball-game origin (the Wikipedia article states it; I did not read Brecher directly).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (labor unrest is American; no Red Scare in the setting, so the "communist agitator" press framing of the period would be weaker or aimed at German agents instead).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Same company, same winter, as the German-staffed Airdock. A "Law and Order League" of 5,200 vigilantes is exactly the kind of population a Black Legion recruiter works.

Alcoholics Anonymous is born in Akron — 12 May and 10 June 1935

  • Where: Mayflower Hotel, 263 South Main Street, Akron (opened 18 May 1931); Stan Hywet Hall, the Seiberling estate; Dr. Bob Smith's home and practice.
  • What happened: In spring 1935 Bill Wilson, a New York stock speculator in Akron on a business trip (the popular account says a failed proxy fight — UNVERIFIED), found himself at the Mayflower with his money running out and phoned from its lobby for help; the call reached Henrietta Seiberling, daughter-in-law of Goodyear founder Frank Seiberling and a member of the Oxford Group, a Christian fellowship. She introduced him to Dr. Robert Holbrook Smith, an Akron surgeon and drunk, on 12 May 1935. After a relapse at a convention Smith drank one beer on the morning of 10 June 1935 to steady his hands for an operation — his last drink ever; AA counts that day as its founding.
  • The odd detail: The telephone Wilson used is said to still be in the Mayflower's lobby; Seiberling's gravestone reads "Let Go and Let God."
  • People: Bill Wilson — New York speculator, co-founder; Dr. Bob Smith (1879–1950) — Akron surgeon, co-founder, "Prince of Twelfth Steppers"; Henrietta Buckler Seiberling (1888–1979) — Vassar-trained Oxford Group member who ran its "alcoholic squad."
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Smith_(doctor) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Buckler_Seiberling ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayflower_Manor_Apartments
  • Confidence: High on dates; Medium on the lobby-phone detail (Wikipedia's Mayflower article, uncited in my read).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (R05 covers religion and cults in depth; the Oxford Group — the Buchmanite movement — belongs there too.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A secret society of confession and surrender to a Higher Power, founded in a rubber baron's household in the year after repeal — the three-layers method fits: the period saw a temperance fellowship; folklore says a phone call saved a life; underneath, something else answered.

CLEVELAND — the IHS city

John Carroll University: German Jesuits, and the priest who lived in the tower — 1886; move 1931–1935

  • Where: Original campus: West 30th Street and Carroll Avenue (then Jersey Street), Cleveland's West Side; new campus: University Heights (then Idlewood Village), off the Van Sweringens' Fairmount Circle, under the Grasselli Tower.
  • What happened: St. Ignatius College opened 6 September 1886 with 76 students, founded at Bishop Richard Gilmour's request by Jesuits of the Buffalo Mission of the German Province of the Society of Jesus. It separated from St. Ignatius High School in 1902 and in 1923 renamed itself Cleveland University (May) and then John Carroll University (September), after America's first Catholic bishop. Land in Idlewood (a 45-acre site) was bought in 1923 ⟨fixed: V_R07⟩ as part of the Van Sweringen brothers' plan for schools on boulevards radiating from Fairmount Circle. The cornerstone was laid 5 July 1931; the Depression stopped work in 1932; building resumed in May 1935 and classes began on the new campus on 7 October 1935. The graduate school admitted women from 1934.
  • The odd detail: In April 1932, when construction halted, the aged seismologist Fr. Frederick L. Odenbach, SJ moved into the unfinished Grasselli Tower with his dog Hector and a canary to guard his instruments — he cooked there and slept in an Arctic sleeping bag, and stayed until his superiors called him back to the West Side shortly before his death on 15 March 1933. He had founded America's first Jesuit seismic observatory (1900) [UNVERIFIED — V_R07], invented the "ceraunograph" to record thunder and lightning (1899), and in 1909 proposed a cooperative seismograph network across Jesuit colleges in the US and Canada (the Jesuit Seismological Association followed in 1925) ⟨fixed: V_R07⟩.
  • People: Bishop Richard Gilmour — invited the Jesuits (already in the setting); Fr. Frederick L. Odenbach, SJ (1857–1933) — Rochester-born seismologist, the tower's hermit; Fr. Joseph Joliat, SJ — his successor at the observatory; Thomas A. Grasselli and three sisters — donated the tower's bells (cast in Baltimore) [UNVERIFIED — V_R07]; Van Sweringen brothers — planned the Fairmount Circle district.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/j/john-carroll-university ; https://case.edu/ech/articles/o/odenbach-frederick-l-sj ; https://www.jcu.edu/about/history ; JCU Magazine 2016 "Studying the Earth's movements," read as PDF at https://www.vaticanobservatory.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/JCU-Seismologists.pdf
  • Confidence: High (the Hector/canary/sleeping-bag detail is from JCU's own magazine).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the college was founded by German-province Jesuits; in a world where Germany won the Great War, German Catholic ties are a source of prestige or suspicion, and the order's American seat being "German-founded" is a live question. The move itself is domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): IHS's American seat was literally unfinished and guarded by one old priest, a dog and a songbird for a year — and the priest ran a continent-wide Jesuit instrument network recording tremors and lightning. A network of seismographs in every Jesuit college is exactly how an order of monster-hunters would listen for things moving underground.

The Cleveland Clinic disaster — 15 May 1929

  • Where: Main building of the Cleveland Clinic Foundation, Euclid Avenue at East 93rd Street (address from general knowledge; the ECH article does not give it — UNVERIFIED).
  • What happened: Nitrocellulose X-ray film stored in the basement, heated by an unguarded 100-watt bulb, decomposed and released yellow-brown poison smoke, chiefly phosgene. The fire department put out the fire but not the gas: 123 died (80 patients and visitors, 43 employees) and 92 were injured. Among the dead was Dr. John Phillips, one of the Clinic's founders. Lawsuits totalling $3 million settled for about $45,000; the disaster rewrote national rules for storing nitrocellulose film and pushed Cleveland to buy gas masks and start a municipal ambulance service.
  • The odd detail: People died walking out of a building that was barely burning — killed by the hospital's own archive of photographs of the inside of the body.
  • People: Dr. John Phillips — co-founder, killed.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/c/cleveland-clinic-disaster
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Five months before the crash — a basement full of X-ray images of the sick exhales a coloured cloud. Folklore layer: what was on those plates.

The Van Sweringen brothers' empire falls — 1930–1936

  • Where: Terminal Tower, Public Square (opened 28 June 1930); Shaker Heights; Daisy Hill estate, Hunting Valley.
  • What happened: Oris Paxton (1879–1936) and Mantis James (1881–1935) Van Sweringen built Shaker Heights, bought the Nickel Plate railroad in 1916, and by 1929 controlled a $3 billion, 30,000-mile railroad empire through stacked holding companies, plus stock in Goodyear, Midland Steel and White Motor. Their Terminal Tower complex opened in June 1930, as the crash was eating them. On 1 May 1935 they defaulted on $48 million in loans from J. P. Morgan & Co., which ordered the collateral auctioned in September; a friendly bid bought it back for just over $3 million. Mantis died 12 December 1935; Oris 22 November 1936.
  • The odd detail: The brothers never married, lived together at Daisy Hill, and died eleven months apart as their empire was auctioned.
  • People: O. P. and M. J. Van Sweringen — Cleveland's reclusive railroad brothers; J. P. Morgan & Co. — their lenders.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/v/van-sweringen-oris-paxton-and-mantis-james
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the crash causes differ in the setting, but the default lands on the record's date).
  • Also: The Terminal Tower (708 ft to the roof) was the tallest building in North America outside New York City from its completion until 1964; it opened to tenants in 1928 and the Union Terminal complex was dedicated in 1930 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_Tower).
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cleveland_-_The_Vacation_City.jpg (1930 travel brochure with the newly dedicated Terminal Tower — check licence on file page)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Van Sweringens laid out the Fairmount Circle where John Carroll stands — two inseparable bachelors who owned a quarter of the continent's rails, then lost it in one year and died one after the other. A vampire-society layer writes itself.

Eliot Ness, Cleveland's Safety Director — sworn in 11 December 1935

  • Where: Cleveland City Hall; the Flats and Kingsbury Run shantytowns.
  • What happened: Mayor Harold H. Burton recruited Ness, the Treasury agent of Capone fame, and at 32 he became the city's youngest-ever Director of Public Safety. His graft purge indicted nine high-ranking police officers for soliciting bribes; six were convicted. He reorganised traffic and Cleveland won the National Safety Council's "Safest Large City in America" in 1938 and 1939. During the Torso Murders he ordered the shantytowns evacuated and burned (1938), which made him unpopular. A 1942 hit-and-run he tried to hide, and a lopsided loss for mayor in 1947, ended his career; he died 16 May 1957.
  • The odd detail: The man who took Capone's books spent his Cleveland years hunting a killer he never caught.
  • People: Eliot Ness — Safety Director; Harold H. Burton — mayor who hired him.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/n/ness-eliot
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar; note that in the setting Capone's fall is the Secret Service's doing, so Ness's Treasury past may be rewritten as Secret Service cover. ⟨fixed: factions⟩
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Ness as a Section 13 asset parked in the IHS city. The Torso Murders are already in the setting (owner's research folder; R06 covers the killings) — this entry is only Ness's side.

The Harvard Club raid — 10 January 1936

  • Where: Harvard Club, Harvard Avenue, Newburgh Heights (addresses 3111 and 4209 Harvard Ave.), just outside the Cleveland city line.
  • What happened: The Harvard Club took 500–1,000 gamblers a night from across the country — craps, slots, roulette, all-night poker — under operators including James "Shimmy" Patton, Arthur Hebebrand and Daniel T. Gallagher. Safety Director Eliot Ness led the celebrated raid that closed it on 10 January 1936. It reopened the next month with more gambling and a fleet of limousines giving free rides from downtown, survived scandals and grand juries, and only closed in April 1941 after Judge Frank J. Lausche deputised a police captain as an officer of the court to enforce his order.
  • The odd detail: Raided shut, it came back within a month offering free limousine pickup.
  • People: Eliot Ness; James "Shimmy" Patton — operator; Frank J. Lausche — the judge who finally closed it.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/h/harvard-club
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A casino just over a city line, fed by limousines, that cannot be kept closed — a GM's favourite kind of door.

The Great Lakes Exposition — 27 June 1936 – 15 September 1937

  • Where: 135 acres of lakefront landfill from Cleveland Municipal Stadium (West 3rd St.) to East 20th St., south to St. Clair Avenue.
  • What happened: Built by 3,000 workers in 80 days with $1.5 million in private money (organisers Frank J. Ryan, Lincoln G. Dickey, Dudley S. Blossom) for Cleveland's centennial, it ran 108 days in 1936 and 121 in 1937, drawing about 7 million. Roosevelt opened it remotely from Washington. Exhibits: "The Romance of Iron and Steel," the Hall of Progress, a 12,000-seat Radioland, a replica Globe Theatre, Streets of the World (150 buildings, 30-plus countries), Admiral Byrd's Antarctic ship City of New York, the decommissioned submarine S-49, $3 blimp rides; in 1937 Billy Rose's Aquacade with Eleanor Holm and Johnny Weissmuller. Admission 50 cents.
  • The odd detail: It boasted a greater intensity of light per acre than any American exposition before it, and ran midget and nude shows now considered offensive.
  • People: Lincoln G. Dickey — Public Hall commissioner, organiser; Dudley S. Blossom — chairman; Billy Rose — showman.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/g/great-lakes-exposition
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar; the "Streets of the World" foreign villages BEND (which nations sent what, and whether Germany's is the grandest, is the owner's call).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The fair ran both summers of the Torso Murders, a mile from Kingsbury Run — the brightest acre in America beside the darkest ravine. A blimp ride over it on a Goodyear ship ties Cleveland to Akron.

DETROIT, DEARBORN AND FLINT — the motor city

The Detroit River funnel: Windsor whisky, the bridge and the tunnel — 1920–1933

  • Where: The Detroit River between Detroit and Windsor/Walkerville, Ontario; the Ambassador Bridge (opened 1929); the Detroit–Windsor Tunnel (opened 3 November 1930).
  • What happened: Canada's provincial prohibitions never banned manufacturing liquor for export, so Canadian distillers could legally make what American smugglers illegally carried; the Windsor–Detroit crossing became the industrial-scale route, with a widely repeated claim that 75% of all liquor smuggled into the US in Prohibition came through Detroit. Hiram Walker's distillery (founded 1858; the company town of Walkerville incorporated 7 April 1890 with Walker as first mayor) made Canadian Club; in 1926 the Walker family sold it to Harry C. Hatch for $15 million. Pilot Jack Boettner parachuted to safety; a mechanic's parachute caught fire, and another man hung fifty feet below the burning gondola on tangled lines. It was the worst airship accident in the US to that date; Chicago wrote new aviation rules and built the Chicago Air Park, later Midway Airport.
  • The odd detail: An airship from Akron fell into a bank in Chicago's financial district — the ten dead were clerks at their desks.
  • People: Jack Boettner — Goodyear pilot, survived.
