  • Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent)

Overview

Cleveland in the 1930s was a city of about 900,000 (1930 census, general knowledge) split by the Cuyahoga's industrial valley, with its Catholic life organised into dozens of national parishes under a Bavarian-born organiser-bishop, Joseph Schrembs, and its Jesuits spread between an old Gothic house on the West Side and a half-built tower in the Van Sweringens' suburb. This file goes beyond what R05, R06, R07, R13 and R16 already hold. The strongest finds for the IHS city: the Guelph Treasure — a German royal church treasure, relics still inside its trap-door altar, bought by the Cleveland Museum of Art in 1931; the Hamann-Todd collection — more than 3,000 human skeletons gathered at Western Reserve by T. Wingate Todd through the Torso years (he died four months after the last bodies); Dayton Miller's "ether wind" measured from Case toward the constellation Dorado; Frank Milano fleeing to Veracruz in 1935 (Veracruz is under American receivership in the setting — a hard bend); St. Alexis Hospital was founded with Bishop Gilmour's support ⟨fixed: V_R25⟩, where the Torso suspect Sweeney trained; Rabbi Abba Hillel Silver's history of messianic calculation (a natural Kabbalahs contact); the Cleveland Agreement of 1915 and the Czech, Slovak, Hungarian, Slovene and Polish neighbourhoods whose old countries, in the setting, never broke free of Vienna and Berlin; Franklin Castle occupied by German cultural clubs from 1921; the stone Guardians of Traffic carved in Little Italy, over the spot where Rose Wallace's skeleton was found; and the Cultural Gardens, a valley of national shrines below the seminary. Occult and spiritualist Cleveland proper remained thin — Chronicling America was rate-limited (HTTP 429) all session — so that thread is mostly in Leads.

Part 1 — The Church in the IHS city

Bishop Joseph Schrembs, the German-born organiser — Bishop of Cleveland 8 Sept 1921 – 2 Nov 1945

  • Where: Chancery and Cathedral of St. John the Evangelist, E. 9th St. and Superior Ave., downtown Cleveland.
  • What happened: Born 12 March 1866 at Wutzlhofen by Regensburg, Bavaria; trained at St. Vincent Archabbey (Pennsylvania); ordained 1889; vicar general of Grand Rapids from 1903; consecrated auxiliary bishop and then named the first bishop of the new Diocese of Toledo in 1911; bishop of Cleveland from 8 Sept 1921. During the war years he was one of four bishops on the administrative committee of the National Catholic War Council, and he lobbied Rome successfully to let the body continue in peacetime (it became the National Catholic Welfare Council, whose permanent committee he joined); he helped found the National Councils of Catholic Men and of Catholic Women. In Cleveland he expanded Catholic Charities, consolidated the boys' orphanages at Parmadale, began the Rose-Mary Home, moved St. Mary Seminary, and opened a Sisters' College (1928 at the cathedral per ECH's cathedral article; 1931 per its biography). He "actively supported the trade-union movement, and used radio to evangelize and instruct." Rome gave him the personal title of archbishop in 1939; diabetes brought a coadjutor, Edward F. Hoban, in 1942. He is buried in a crypt beneath the altar of St. John Cathedral.
  • The odd detail: The bishop of the IHS city spent the Great War years persuading Rome to keep an American bishops' committee alive after the emergency ended — a man who knew how to turn a temporary war body into a permanent institution.
  • People: Joseph Schrembs — bishop; Edward F. Hoban — coadjutor from 1942, successor.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/s/schrembs-joseph ; https://case.edu/ech/articles/s/st-john-cathedral
  • Confidence: High (ECH). The Sisters' College date conflicts inside ECH (1928 vs 1931).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the National Catholic War Council was a response to America entering the Great War (1917); in the setting America never entered it, so Schrembs's national career needs another engine (the Southern War / occupation chaplaincy in Mexico is the obvious one). His Bavarian birth reads differently in a Germany-won world.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A bishop who talked Rome into keeping a "wartime" committee permanent is exactly the man who would be the IHS's public face in Cleveland without ever being a member — or the member who built the order's American bureaucracy. His crypt under the cathedral altar is a place the order would guard.

Bishop Richard Gilmour, the Scots convert who fought the county — bishop 14 Apr 1872 – 13 Apr 1891 (living memory; already in the setting)

  • Where: Diocese of Cleveland; St. Alexis Hospital and St. John Hospital (both founded under him).
  • What happened: Born 24 September 1824 at Dumbarton, Scotland, into a Scotch-Presbyterian family that moved to Nova Scotia and then Pennsylvania, Gilmour converted to Catholicism in 1842, was ordained 30 August 1852, and ran a model school at St. Patrick's in Cincinnati, where he translated a Bible history (papal commendation) and wrote the readers used across Catholic schools. Consecrated bishop of Cleveland 14 April 1872, he founded and subsidised the Catholic Universe newspaper in 1874 "to defend Catholic rights," and fought a six-year court battle (1877–1883) against the Cuyahoga County auditor's attempt to tax parochial school buildings. St. Ann's Asylum & Maternity Home, St. Alexis Hospital (erected 1884 at 5163 Broadway Ave., the city's second Catholic hospital) and St. John Hospital were founded with his support ⟨fixed: V_R25⟩, and (per the St. Ignatius history) he invited the Buffalo Jesuits who opened St. Ignatius College in 1886. He died at St. Augustine, Florida, on 13 April 1891.
  • The odd detail: St. Alexis Hospital (1884) was founded with Gilmour's support ⟨fixed: V_R25⟩ — the Broadway-district hospital where, forty years later, Dr. Francis Sweeney did his residency on the edge of Kingsbury Run (R06). The bishop who brought the Jesuits also backed the Torso suspect's training ground.
  • People: Richard Gilmour — bishop; the Buffalo Mission Jesuits — his invitees.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/g/gilmour-richard ; https://case.edu/ech/articles/s/st-ignatius-high-school ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland_Torso_Murderer ("In 1929, Sweeney was a surgical resident at St. Alexis Hospital in the Kingsbury Run area.")
  • Confidence: High on Gilmour and on the St. Alexis–Sweeney link (re-read this session).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Gilmour is already in the owner's Senate-hearing lore; the real man was a litigator who spent six years in court against the county to keep Church buildings out of the state's hands — the right temperament for the bishop who signed the 1866-era alliance's Cleveland end.

The Cathedral of St. John the Evangelist, as it stood in the 1930s — built 1848–52

  • Where: Erie St. (E. 9th St.) and Superior Ave., downtown Cleveland — two blocks from Short Vincent and a short walk to City Hall and the lakefront.
  • What happened: Cornerstone laid 22 October 1848; consecrated as cathedral 7 November 1852; designed by Patrick Charles Keeley in "Ornamental Gothic"; altar and statues imported from France; steeple added 1879; interior refurbished 1888, with a school building of 1888 (closed 1943). In the 1920s Schrembs decided to keep the cathedral downtown rather than move it east with the city's money. The building every 1930s Clevelander knew was the old brick Gothic church with its tall tower: in 1946–48 George W. Stickle refaced it in Tennessee sandstone, removed the old tower, and enlarged the sanctuary to seat 1,500.
  • The odd detail: The cathedral the 1930s knew no longer exists in its own shape — the tower was taken down after the war — so a GM can put anything in that tower.
  • People: Patrick C. Keeley — architect; Joseph Schrembs — kept it downtown; George W. Stickle — the postwar rebuilder.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/s/st-john-cathedral
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The bishop's crypt beneath the altar (Schrembs, 1945) and a Gothic tower that vanished in 1946 — the IHS's downtown door, a bell tower overlooking the lakefront where the Torso victims of August 1938 were dumped.

St. Mary Seminary moves to Ansel Road — built from 1924 (Spanish mission style)

  • Where: Superior Ave. and Ansel Rd. (1227 Ansel Rd.), on the East Side by Rockefeller Park.
  • What happened: Founded 1848 by Bishop Amadeus Rappe in a converted stable at Bond (E. 6th) St. and St. Clair, the seminary moved in 1850 into the former Spring Cottage Bath House at Lake and Hamilton, and rebuilt there in 1859. In 1924 construction began on a Spanish mission-style building at Superior and Ansel. In 1922 Schrembs restricted it to undergraduate work and sent his theology students to Mt. St. Mary Seminary in Cincinnati — a policy reversed after seven years. It was also known at times as "Our Lady of the Lake." The Ansel Road building was vacated in 1990 and became transitional housing for women in 2019.
  • The odd detail: Cleveland's priests were trained in a Spanish-mission building on the Great Lakes — and for most of the 1920s the diocese's future priests did their theology out of town.
  • People: Amadeus Rappe — founder; Joseph Schrembs — moved it and split its training.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/s/st-mary-seminary
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The seminary's other name, "Our Lady of the Lake," and the seven years when Cleveland sent its seminarians away — a window in which the IHS could have run its own quiet formation in the diocese.

St. Ignatius, the Jesuits' West Side house — 1886 onward

  • Where: W. 30th St. at Carroll Ave., Ohio City, West Side (a principal five-storey Gothic building; later wings including Loyola Hall and Carroll Gym; campus grew to 15 acres).
  • What happened: Founded 1886 at Bishop Gilmour's invitation by Jesuits from Buffalo; a combined high school and college "until 1902, when the latter assumed a separate existence." The college became Cleveland University and then John Carroll (1923) and left for University Heights in 1935 (R07, R05); the high school stayed on the old site, which it still occupies — it chose not to follow the city's Catholics to the suburbs. From 1971 it has been governed by a lay board rather than the Jesuits.
  • The odd detail: For the whole decade the IHS seat (in the setting) had two addresses — a Gothic pile in the West Side immigrant district and a half-built tower in the Van Sweringens' suburb (R07's Fr. Odenbach lived in that tower in 1932–33).
  • People: Bishop Richard Gilmour — invited them; the Buffalo Mission of the German Province — founders.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/s/st-ignatius-high-school
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the German-province founders BEND, see R07).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The old West Side house is where the order keeps what it cannot move to a campus full of students — the archive, the prisoners, the relics. The new campus is the public face.

The Guelph Treasure comes to Cleveland — exhibited 1930–31; nine pieces bought 1931

  • Where: Cleveland Museum of Art, University Circle (Wade Park), a mile from Little Italy and Lake View Cemetery.
  • What happened: In 1929 Ernest Augustus, Duke of Brunswick, head of the House of Hanover, sold 82 pieces of the Guelph Treasure — the medieval church treasure of Brunswick Cathedral, gathered by the Welf dynasty — to four Frankfurt dealers (Saemy Rosenberg, Isaak Rosenbaum, Julius Falk Goldschmidt, Zacharias Hackenbroch) for 7.5 million Reichsmarks. The dealers toured it through the United States in 1930–31, and the Cleveland Museum of Art bought nine pieces, more than any other buyer. Among them: the Portable Altar of Countess Gertrude (c. 1045; gold, cloisonné enamel, porphyry, gems, pearls, niello over wood; accession 1931.462, gift of the John Huntington Art and Polytechnic Trust), inscribed "Gertrude offers to Christ, to live joyfully in him, this stone that glistens with gems and gold." The treasure included arm reliquaries (one of St. Blaise), crosses and dome reliquaries. The 42 unsold pieces went in 1935 for 4.25 million Reichsmarks, in a deal in the Netherlands, to agents of Hermann Göring, and are now in Berlin's Bode Museum.
  • The odd detail: The Gertrude altar has a trap door in its base that opens on small relics of saints wrapped in silk — a working reliquary, relics still inside, in a Cleveland museum from 1931.
  • People: Ernest Augustus, Duke of Brunswick — the seller; Saemy Rosenberg, Isaak Rosenbaum, Julius Falk Goldschmidt, Zacharias Hackenbroch — the Frankfurt dealers; Countess Gertrude of Brunswick — the 11th-century donor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guelph_Treasure ; https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1931.462
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the 1929 sale grew out of the Hanovers' postwar losses and the 1935 sale out of Nazi Germany (Göring); in a Germany-won world the Duke of Brunswick (the Kaiser's son-in-law) is not broke, the treasure probably never leaves, and there is no Göring. If the owner keeps the Cleveland purchase, it needs another reason — a German sale made on purpose.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Saints' relics wrapped in silk behind a trap door, bought by a Protestant museum trust in the IHS's own city in 1931 — the order would want them, and a victorious Berlin might want them back. A heist, a custody dispute, or a ward the museum never knew it owned.

The Cleveland Agreement — 22–23 October 1915 (living memory)

  • Where: Bohemian National Hall, 4939 Broadway Ave., "Little Bohemia," Cleveland.
  • What happened: Delegates of the Slovak League of America (Ivan Daxner, Albert Pavol Mamatey) and the Czech National Association (Ludvík Fisher, Josef Tvrzický-Kramer) met in the freethinkers' hall on Broadway and signed a five-point programme: independence for the Czech and Slovak lands, a federal union with Slovak autonomy (its own legislature and administration), free elections, democratic government, and amendment only with both parties' consent. Mamatey was elected chairman and Emanuel Voska vice-chairman. It was superseded by the Pittsburgh Agreement of May 1918, and the real Czechoslovakia was proclaimed 28 October 1918. About 360 Czech and Slovak volunteers from the Cleveland area joined the Czechoslovak Legions.
  • The odd detail: One of the founding documents of a European country was signed in a Cleveland dance-and-lecture hall on Broadway — by Americans, about a state that did not yet exist.
  • People: Albert Pavol Mamatey — Slovak League chairman; Emanuel Voska — Czech-American organiser (and, in the real record, Allied intelligence man); Tomáš Masaryk — the exile leader whose plan it served.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland_Agreement ; https://case.edu/ech/articles/c/czechs
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the 1915 meeting happened (pre-1916), but in the setting Austria-Hungary survives and Czechoslovakia never exists. Cleveland's Czech and Slovak halls in the 1930s are full of men who signed a plan that failed — émigré politics against Vienna (and Vienna's patron, Berlin).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A failed nation's paperwork, still kept in a Broadway hall; its legionnaires home in Cleveland with rifles and grievances. Useful for a "Germany as patron of other people's revolutions" inversion — here Berlin is the power the revolutionaries lost to.

"Little Bohemia" and the freethinkers — Cleveland's Czechs, 1870s–1930s

  • Where: Broadway from E. 37th St. to Union Ave. ("Little Bohemia"); also W. 41st St. (Denison–Clark), Quincy Ave./E. 80th, Buckeye Rd., lower Fleet Ave.
  • What happened: From 696 families (3,252 people) in 1870 to about 10,000 by 1890, with the biggest wave 1900–14. By the late 1880s "more than half" of Czech-Americans had left the Church; each settlement had a church and a freethinkers' hall facing it — St. Wenceslas (founded 1867, rebuilt 1892 at Broadway and E. 37th), St. Procop (1874, West Side) and Our Lady of Lourdes on one side; Bohemian National Hall and Česká Síň Sokol Hall (freethinker) on the other, plus the Catholic Karlin Hall. Sokol gymnastic units from 1879; a socialist weekly (Americké Dělnické Listy, 1909); a Catholic paper (American); ten of the Cleveland Orchestra's 57 charter members were Czech. Monsignor Oldřich Zlámal, pastor of Our Lady of Lourdes, pushed Cleveland Czechs into politics.
  • The odd detail: The schism "forced every Czech-American to make a choice" — church or hall — and the neighbourhood was laid out that way, building facing building.
  • People: Msgr. Oldřich Zlámal — Our Lady of Lourdes; Joseph Martínek — socialist editor.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/c/czechs
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — old-country politics (no Czechoslovakia; the Habsburgs still rule).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Cult versus population": the freethinkers are a large, secular, anticlerical population the IHS cannot recruit from and tends to suspect — the right place to hide a real cult that looks like one more reading society.

The Hungarians of Buckeye Road — "the American Debrecen," 1890s–1930s

  • Where: Buckeye Rd. (from Woodland Ave./E. 79th, pushing out to E. 125th by 1930), with a secondary West Side community.
  • What happened: 9,558 Hungarians in 1900; 43,134 in 1920 (18% of the foreign-born). The city boasted North America's first Hungarian churches: First Hungarian Reformed (1891), St. Elizabeth of Hungary Roman Catholic (1892) and St. John's Greek Catholic (1892); by the early 1920s there were eleven Hungarian churches of six denominations plus three Hungarian Jewish congregations. Six Hungarian-language papers by 1920; Szabadság reached a daily circulation of 40,612 in 1940. A statue of Louis Kossuth, leader of the 1848 revolution against the Habsburgs, has stood at University Circle since 1902. The United Hungarian Societies date from 1902.
  • The odd detail: The nickname "American Debrecen" (after Hungary's Calvinist capital) — which, ECH notes drily, "did not hold up statistically."
  • People: Louis Kossuth — revolutionary, honoured in bronze.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/h/hungarians
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in the setting the Dual Monarchy survives; Buckeye Road's old country is still the Habsburg kingdom, and a Kossuth statue is a standing rebuke to a living empire allied with Berlin.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Hungarian consular politics in Cleveland — loyalists and Kossuth men — and a Greek Catholic parish whose priests could marry, something the Latin IHS finds awkward.

The Slovenes of St. Clair Avenue — "the 3rd-largest Slovene city in the world" (1910)

  • Where: St. Clair Ave. from E. 30th to E. 79th St. at its 1920s–30s peak; smaller colonies in Newburgh, Collinwood, Euclid, West Park.
  • What happened: The 1910 census counted 14,332 Slovenes in Cleveland — ECH calls it then the third-largest Slovene city on earth and the largest Slovene settlement in America. Parishes: St. Vitus on St. Clair (organised 1893), St. Lawrence in Newburgh (1901), St. Mary in Collinwood (1906). The Slovenian National Home on St. Clair (built 1924) seated more than 1,000. Sokol from 1897, more than 40 singing societies over the years, and papers from Narodna Beseda (1899) to Ameriška Domovina. Frank J. Lausche — the judge who finally closed the Harvard Club (R07), later mayor, governor and senator — and the writer Louis Adamic came out of this world.
  • The odd detail: A Slovenian neighbourhood big enough to rank among the world's Slovene cities — in a country most Americans could not have found on a map.
  • People: Frank J. Lausche — Slovene-American judge/politician; Louis Adamic — writer.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/s/slovenes
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — no Yugoslavia in a Germany-won world; Slovenia stays a Habsburg crownland, and St. Clair's politics (loyalist, pan-Slav, socialist) follow from that.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A thousand-seat national hall where the old country's quarrels are argued in a language the police do not speak — a meeting place nobody outside notices.

Warszawa, Kantowo and the Poles' parishes — 1873–1930s

  • Where: Warszawa (Fleet Ave./E. 65th, later Slavic Village) around the steel mills; Kantowo (Tremont); Poznań (E. 79th and Superior); Józefatowo (E. 33rd and St. Clair); Barbarowo (Denison Ave.).
  • What happened: From 77 Poles in 1870 to 35,024 in 1920 and a peak of 36,668 foreign-born in 1930. St. Stanislaus (parish 1873, church completed 1881) is the mother parish; daughter parishes include Sacred Heart (1889), St. Hyacinth (1907), St. John Cantius (1897, Kantowo), St. Casimir (1893), St. Josaphat (1908), and Corpus Christi (1936). In 1892 Fr. Anton Kolaszewski was removed from St. Stanislaus and in 1894 set up his own Immaculate Heart of Mary parish; its members were excommunicated until he resigned in 1908; the breakaway Polish National Catholic Church opened its first Cleveland parish in Kantowo in 1914. By 1919 Poles were over half the workforce at U.S. Steel's American Steel & Wire. The Polish Falcons' Nest 141 recruited volunteers "to fight with the Allies in World War I." [UNVERIFIED — V_R25] By 1930 the two dailies split over Piłsudski (Wiadomości Codzienne for, Monitor Clevelandski against).
  • The odd detail: 67 Polish saloons and 32 Polish grocers by 1900 — and a parish whose people were excommunicated for fourteen years for following their own priest.
  • People: Fr. Anton Kolaszewski — the rebel pastor; Stella Walsh (Stanisława Walasiewicz) — Olympic champion sprinter of the Polish Falcons (1932 gold for Poland), who lived and worked in Cleveland.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/p/poles
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — "fighting with the Allies" and the Piłsudski quarrel belong to the real Poland of 1918; in the setting Poland is whatever Berlin made of it at Brest-Litovsk (a German-client kingdom is the natural reading — owner's call).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Kolaszewski schism is a model for a parish that goes over to something else and takes its congregation with it; the Polish National Catholics are a church the IHS does not control.

St. Theodosius, the onion-domed cathedral of Tremont — consecrated 20 July 1913

  • Where: 733 Starkweather Ave., Tremont (above the Flats, beside the Kantowo Poles).
  • What happened: The parish was organised in 1896 by Carpatho-Rusyn immigrants — Cleveland's first Eastern Orthodox parish. Architect Frederick C. Baird designed the church (groundbreaking 1911, consecrated 20 July 1913, $70,000) from photographs of Moscow's Cathedral of Christ the Saviour, with one large, four medium and eight small copper onion domes for Christ and the twelve apostles; the iconostasis icons came from Russia. It hosted three All-American Sobors of the Russian Orthodox church in America — in 1919, 1934 and 1946. Its long-serving pastor was Fr. Jason Kappanadze (his likeness went into 1953 murals).
  • The odd detail: A copy of Moscow's great cathedral, built from photographs, stood in Tremont in the 1930s — and the Moscow original was blown up in 1931 on Stalin's orders, leaving Cleveland's copy as the one still standing.
  • People: Frederick C. Baird — architect; Fr. Jason Kappanadze — pastor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Theodosius_Russian_Orthodox_Cathedral
  • Confidence: High on the church; the 1931 demolition of the Moscow cathedral is general knowledge, not from the page read.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — with no Soviet Union the Moscow cathedral is never demolished and the Russian church in America keeps its tie to a (German-dominated) Russia; the 1934 sobor debates a different question.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): An Orthodox cathedral, outside Rome's reach, three blocks from the Catholic Jesuits' first West Side streets — a second "church that hunts" with its own saints and its own reasons, or a sanctuary the IHS cannot enter.

Big Italy, Little Italy and the stonecutters of Lake View — 1880s–1930s

  • Where: Big Italy on Woodland and Orange Aves. from E. 9th to E. 40th; Little Italy at Mayfield and Murray Hill Rds. (E. 119th–E. 125th), against the bluff of Lake View Cemetery; Collinwood; two West Side colonies.
  • What happened: Italian-born Clevelanders passed 32,000 by the late 1920s, mostly from the Mezzogiorno; Little Italy's families came largely from Ripalimosani, Matrice and San Giovanni in Galdo (Campobasso). Little Italy grew around the marble works that served Lake View Cemetery — Joseph Carabelli, a stonecutter who arrived in 1880, founded the Lakeview Marble Works — and in 1911 it was 96% Italian-born. Churches: St. Anthony (1887, Ohio's first Italian parish), Holy Rosary (1892, Little Italy), St. Marian (1905), St. Rocco (1922), Holy Redeemer (1924), Mt. Carmel West (1926). By 1920 Italians were 80% of Cleveland's barbers and 70% of its cooks. In 1935 the Italian ambassador, Augusto Rosso, dedicated a Sons of Italy temple. The Mayfield Road Mob took its name from Little Italy's main street (formed c. 1913).
  • The odd detail: Little Italy exists because of the cemetery — its men carved Cleveland's gravestones, and the neighbourhood sits pressed against the graveyard wall.
  • People: Joseph Carabelli — stonecutter, Lakeview Marble Works; Augusto Rosso — Italian ambassador, 1935.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/l/little-italy ; https://case.edu/ech/articles/i/italians ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayfield_Road_Mob
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar; Italy's own politics (Mussolini, the ambassador's temple) depend on the setting's Italy (owner's call).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The men who carve the graves live against the cemetery wall and run the neighbourhood's rackets — stone, silence and the dead are one trade here.

Lake View Cemetery — founded 1869; the 1930s city of the dead

  • Where: Between Euclid Ave. and Mayfield Rd. at the city's eastern edge (Cleveland/Cleveland Heights), above Little Italy.
  • What happened: The Lake View Cemetery Association formed in July 1869 under Jeptha H. Wade and bought nearly 200 acres for $73,000; first lots were sold July 1870. The Garfield Monument (George H. Keller) opened on Memorial Day 1890; the Wade Memorial Chapel (1901) has a Tiffany interior. Buried here: President James A. Garfield, John D. Rockefeller (a trustee), Mark Hanna, Newton D. Baker, Charles F. Brush, Charles Chesnutt, and both Van Sweringen brothers (1935, 1936). The Collinwood School Fire — 4 March 1908 (living memory)

Part 4 — Science, bones and books

Dayton C. Miller measures the ether wind — Case School, 1900–1933

  • Where: Case School of Applied Science, University Circle (where, by general knowledge, Michelson and Morley ran their famous null experiment in 1887 — not from the page read); Miller's own runs at Mount Wilson, California, 1921–26.
  • What happened: Miller, head of physics at Case from 1893 to 1936, spent decades re-running the Michelson–Morley experiment to detect the "ether" through which light was thought to travel — more than 326,000 turns of the interferometer, over 5,200,000 readings. He reported a small positive drift (0.12 ± 0.01 fringe) and an apparent absolute motion of the Earth of about 227 km/s toward the southern constellation Dorado, and published "The Ether-Drift Experiment and the Determination of the Absolute Motion of the Earth" in Reviews of Modern Physics in 1933. Einstein said positive results would overturn relativity but suspected temperature errors; Miller replied: "The trouble with Professor Einstein is that he knows nothing about my results." He also invented the phonodeik (1908) to photograph sound waves, collected flutes [UNVERIFIED — V_R25], and died in Cleveland on 22 February 1941.
  • The odd detail: A Cleveland physicist spent the 1920s and 1930s insisting the Earth was being blown through space by an invisible wind from the direction of Dorado — and published it in the leading physics review.
  • People: Dayton C. Miller — physicist; Albert Einstein — his sceptic; Edward Morley — his early partner.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dayton_Miller
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Einstein's standing in a Germany-won world is the owner's call — he stays German).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers: the period's reading (a respected crank against Einstein); the folklore (an "ether" that carries more than light); what is there — an instrument in Cleveland that measures a current nobody else can see. The IHS's seismographs listen underground; Miller's interferometer listens to the sky.

Part 8 — Gardens, temples and haunted ground around the city

The Cultural Gardens — a chain of national gardens in Rockefeller Park, 1926–1940

  • Where: Along East Blvd. and today's Martin Luther King Jr. Blvd. through Rockefeller Park (Doan Brook), between University Circle and the lake — below St. Mary Seminary.
  • What happened: After the Shakespeare Garden (1916), journalist Leo Weidenthal proposed a garden for each nationality; with Charles J. Wolfram and Jennie K. Zwick he founded the Civic Progress League (1925), which became the Cultural Garden League (1926). Dedications: Hebrew (5 May 1926 — the poet Hayim Nahman Bialik planted three cedars of Lebanon); German (2 June 1929 — a replica of Ernst Rietschel's Goethe–Schiller monument, first set up in Wade Park in 1907); Italian (12 Oct 1930); Slovak (23 Oct 1932); Hungarian (21 Oct 1934); Polish (28 Oct 1934); Czech (6 Oct 1935); Lithuanian (11 Oct 1936); Rusin (25 June 1939); Irish (29 Oct 1939); Greek (2 June 1940). City manager William R. Hopkins backed it; the WPA built much of it.
  • The odd detail: Each nation got its garden in a long chain down one valley — Czech, Slovak and Hungarian gardens a few hundred yards apart, dedicated in the 1930s by communities whose old countries (in the real record) had just torn up the Habsburg map.
  • People: Leo Weidenthal — founder; Charles J. Wolfram — German-American co-founder; Hayim Nahman Bialik — the Hebrew poet who planted the cedars.
  • Sources: https://case.edu/ech/articles/c/cleveland-cultural-garden-federation ; https://www.clevelandmemory.org/gardens/index.html
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in the setting there is no Czechoslovakia or independent Poland/Lithuania of the Versailles kind; which nations get gardens, and whose consul cuts the ribbon, is the owner's call. The German garden (1929) is the victor's garden.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A valley of national shrines, each with its own statues and soil — a map of Europe laid out in Cleveland, which a summoner could read as a ritual diagram. The IHS seminary sits on the hill above it.

The Kirtland Temple — the Mormon temple 20 miles east — dedicated 27 March 1836

  • Where: Kirtland, Lake County, Ohio, about 20 miles east of Cleveland.
  • What happened: Begun 5 June 1833 [UNVERIFIED — V_R25] (cornerstone 23 July), built for $40,000, dedicated 27 March 1836 in a seven-hour service for about 1,000 people. On 3 April 1836, by Latter Day Saint belief, Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery saw Jesus Christ "standing upon the breastwork of the pulpit," then Moses, Elias and Elijah, who restored "priesthood keys." The third floor held the "School of the Prophets" and Smith's office. In the 1930s it belonged to the RLDS Church (now Community of Christ), which sued for title in 1880 (dismissed) and held it by adverse possession by 1901. Its exterior was originally bluish-grey, not white.
  • The odd detail: A building in rural Ohio whose pulpit, by its believers' account, was stood upon by Christ, Moses and Elijah in one week of 1836 — owned in the 1930s by a church that won it by squatting.
  • People: Joseph Smith; Oliver Cowdery.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirtland_Temple
  • Confidence: High on the record; the visions are the church's own account (label as belief).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Catholic order's American seat has a non-Catholic "house of visions" 20 miles away — a place where something appeared and gave keys. The IHS would keep a file on it.

Squire's Castle — the gatehouse and the false ghost — built c. 1895–97; park land from 1925

  • Where: 2844 River Rd., Willoughby Hills (North Chagrin Reservation), east of Cleveland.
  • What happened: Feargus B. Squire — a Standard Oil executive and former mayor of Wickliffe — bought about 525 acres around 1890 and built a Romanesque Revival gatekeeper's house of Euclid bluestone (c. 1895–97) for an estate he never finished. The Cleveland Park Board bought most of the land in 1925 and started calling it "Squire's Castle"; it later removed the upper floors and filled the basement. Legend says Louisa Squire fell down the stairs during a storm and died there, and haunts it; in fact "Louisa Squire did not die in the building."
  • The odd detail: The "castle" was only ever the gatehouse to a mansion that was never built — a door with no house behind it.
  • People: Feargus B. Squire — Standard Oil man; Louisa Squire — the legend's ghost.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squire%27s_Castle
  • Confidence: High; the ghost is folklore (and the age of the legend is not established — it may postdate the 1930s).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A gatehouse to nowhere in the Metroparks woods: the owner can decide what it was the gate to.

"Gore Orphanage" and the Wilbers' séances — Vermilion, Swift Mansion burned 6 December 1923

  • Where: Gore Orphanage Rd., near Vermilion, Lorain County, west of Cleveland.
  • What happened: The Swift Mansion (built 1840–42 by Joseph Swift) passed to the Wilber family, who held séances there; four Wilber children died of diphtheria (not at the house). Nearby stood the Light of Hope Orphanage — its printing building burned on 22 November 1910 with no one hurt — and the road took "Orphanage" from it ("Gore" was a surveyor's term for a narrow wedge of land). The empty Swift Mansion burned on 6 December 1923. Legend says children died in an orphanage fire there and their screams can be heard at night; historians say no "Gore Orphanage" building ever existed and the story borrowed the 1908 Collinwood School Fire's dead. Teenagers have been "legend-tripping" to the site since at least 1905.
  • The odd detail: The one real spiritualist fact under the legend — a family holding séances in the old mansion — has been forgotten, while an invented fire has become famous.
  • People: Joseph Swift — builder; the Wilber family — séance-holders.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gore_Orphanage
  • Confidence: High on the history; the legend labelled as legend.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened (legend-tripping continued through the 1930s).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The owner's three layers in one place: the period's story (a burned orphanage), the folklore (screaming children borrowed from Collinwood), and what is there (whatever the Wilbers called up in their séances, in an empty house that burned in 1923).

Tremont — the first "University Heights" and the first "Cleveland University" — 1851 onward

  • Where: The South Side bluff above the Flats (Lincoln Park; Starkweather Ave.; St. Theodosius, St. Augustine, St. Michael the Archangel, Pilgrim Congregational).
  • What happened: The neighbourhood was successively named Cleveland Heights (1851), University Heights, Lincoln Heights (1896), "the South Side" (the 1930s name) and Tremont (1970s). In 1851 prominent citizens founded a Cleveland University there; it lasted only until 1853, and its buildings became the Humiston Institute and the Western Reserve Homeopathic Hospital. Irish and Germans came in the 1860s, Poles in the 1890s (Kantowo), Greeks and Syrians in the 1900s — "more than 30 nationalities." Population peaked at 36,686 in 1920. Its churches include St. Theodosius (see above), St. Michael the Archangel (1888), St. Augustine (1896) and Pilgrim Congregational (1893).
  • The odd detail: The Jesuit college took the name "Cleveland University" for four months in 1923 (R05) and moved in 1935 to a suburb called University Heights — both names had already been used, seventy years earlier, by a failed university on the Tremont bluff.

The Clinic fire, closer up — 15 May 1929 (deepens R07 and R13)

  • Where: The Cleveland Clinic's original four-storey building, Euclid Ave., Cleveland — still standing as the Clinic's "Building T," with a small memorial exhibit in its lobby.
  • What happened: Beyond R07/R13: the smouldering nitrocellulose X-ray film gave off phosgene, carbon monoxide, nitric oxide and methyl chloride; a "yellowish-brown gas" filled the building and victims' "faces turned yellowish-brown within minutes." Some died at once, others hours or even days later. Water did not help — nitrocellulose "continues to burn even while immersed in water," so hosing it made more gas. Policeman Ernest Staab carried out 21 victims, went home apparently well, collapsed while working on his lawn, and was reported dead in the newspapers — but lived and served another 25 years. Cleveland then issued gas masks to its firemen and proposed a city ambulance service.
  • The odd detail: A policeman the papers buried who went back to work for a quarter-century — and a building still in daily use by the hospital.
  • People: Ernest Staab — the policeman who rescued 21.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland_Clinic_fire_of_1929
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The victims who died days later, after walking out — the owner's layer can make some of them not quite dead, and Staab the one man who knows.

The 1935 Eucharistic Congress — what the record actually says (reconciles R05 and R07)

  • Where: Cleveland Municipal Stadium; Public Auditorium; Cleveland.
  • What happened: ECH's own article gives the dates as 23–27 September 1935 (R05 says 23–26). A choir of 3,000 local parishioners sang Mass in Public Auditorium on the morning of 24 September; 50,000 filled the stadium for the evening speakers; the midnight Mass, moved from Public Hall to the stadium, drew "nearly 125,000" (R05's "about 150,000" is not in ECH). On the last day 20,000 marchers — fraternal and religious societies, "the safety forces," students [UNVERIFIED — V_R25] — formed the monstrance on the field. Pius XI spoke by radio; James Farley represented Roosevelt.
  • The odd detail: "The safety forces" marched in the living monstrance — Cleveland's police and firemen, in the autumn Ness's appointment was being prepared (sworn in December 1935), were part of the figure drawn for the sky. [UNVERIFIED — V_R25]
  • People: Patrick Hayes — cardinal; Amleto Cicognani — apostolic delegate; John T. McNicholas — archbishop of Cincinnati; Joseph Schrembs — host.
