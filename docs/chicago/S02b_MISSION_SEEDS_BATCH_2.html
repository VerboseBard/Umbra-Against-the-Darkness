S02b — Fifteen more mission seeds (batch 2), from R18–R35

  • Lane: S02b — mission seeds numbered 31–45, built from chapters R18–R35 of the 2026-09-26/27 Chicago & Great Lakes sweep and their fact-checks (V_R*)
  • Date: 2026-09-30
  • Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (mission-seed writer, second batch)
  • Standing: PROPOSAL, NOT CANON — every seed in this file. Nothing here has been ruled by the owner. Real anchors are taken from the chapters as fact-checked; where a checker corrected a fact (⟨fixed …⟩) the corrected fact is used, and nothing tagged [UNVERIFIED …] is used as an anchor. Where two chapters disagree, the seed says so in a ⟨note⟩ instead of choosing silently.
  • Status: COMPLETE — 15 seeds and a summary table (see the end). Not yet audited: no V_S02b exists.
  • Companion to: S02_MISSION_SEEDS.md (seeds 1–30). The closing table of S02 was read so nothing is repeated; this batch fills its gap notes (1931; Toledo; Buffalo/Niagara; the Ontario shore).

Overview. Fifteen seeds, each built the owner's way — the real record kept exactly, the occult layer put underneath. They cover 1930–1939 with five seeds opening in 1931 (S02 had none), every Great Lakes city S02 left uncovered (Toledo, Buffalo/Niagara, Toronto on the Ontario shore) plus Detroit, Cleveland and Milwaukee and the Chicago hinterland (Geneva, Franklin Park, Manteno, Peru, Ind.). Five seeds are built on the newest chapters: R32 (famous visitors: Einstein, Rasputin's daughter), R33 (the Secret Service's own cases), R34 (Jewish Chicago — the ground under the Kabbalahs), R35 (Catholic Chicago — the ground under IHS) and R24 (the autumn-winter 1936 almanac: Granville, the Casino Club's December Ball, the Halloween-eve moon). Every faction sends at least three of the fifteen and is at odds in more; the Secret Service and Section 13 are kept separate rivals throughout; every threat appears at least twice (the Golden Circle as unease, never revelation, §33).

How to read a seed

Same as S02. Real anchors first (chapter + entry, dates, names and addresses as the fact-checked chapter gives them); the three layers of truth (what the papers said · what the folklore says · what is actually there); who sends the team (two factions, at odds); three clue sites (one carries a cost, §29: rolls price clarity and time, never access); the clock (a real date where the record gives one); the divergence underneath; another road (no seed requires one Specialty — the guy, the air, the ground); one twist.

What is record and what is invention: the Real anchors are the record. Everything under what is actually there, the contents of the clue sites, the twists and anything marked proposal is invention for play. Where a seed attributes a secret to a real person, that is the owner's method, not a claim about the person. Three standing courtesies in this batch: (1) real private people who were victims or defendants (the Marchand case, the Ross kidnapping, the Milwaukee bombers, the dead of Granville and Manteno) are never made the villains; (2) the Kabbalahs are forbidden to one institution — they do the work the Church forbids its own — and are never "dark arts" (R34 note; seeds 36, 38, 40, 41, 44); (3) the real Jewish, Catholic and Seneca communities in these seeds are the population, never the cult.

Seed 31 — The High Level — proposal, not canon

Pitch: A retired grocer hands $37,000 across a Toledo table for a share in Mexican oil that does not exist, a $3 million bridge goes up over the Maumee in two years and twenty-seven days, a bootlegger who will not sell is killed — and every ledger the team opens balances to the same three names.

Real anchors

  • The Anthony Wayne Bridge, Toledo — R20 Part 10 (checked in V_R20). The "High Level Bridge" over the Maumee River: built in two years and 27 days, a record for suspension bridges then; $3 million; two 154-ft, 83-ton girders hoisted in 1930 before watching crowds; opened in 1931 before 40,000 people; a proposal to name it for President Warren G. Harding was dropped after a public outcry, and it was named for General Anthony Wayne; five years later Toledo had to issue more bonds to pay for the steel. (The exact opening date is not in the chapter — a lead for a Toledo paper.)
  • The Licavolis take Toledo — R26 §B. Thomas "Yonnie" Licavoli and his brother Peter ran liquor across the Detroit River from Canada; after Thomas came out of a Canadian prison in 1930 the brothers pushed into Toledo, where bootlegger Jack Kennedy resisted; the gang war ended with Kennedy's murder in July 1933 (day not given). Thomas was convicted in 1934 of conspiracy in the killings of Kennedy and three others and sent to the Ohio Penitentiary for life; Peter stayed in Detroit "as a Partnership man".
  • "Yellow Kid" Weil's Toledo table — R33 §D (V_R33-checked family). April 1931: held for taking $37,000 from Matthews Reinart, a retired Indianapolis grocer, who was met at Martinsville by a "J. A. Kirk", introduced to a "G. A. Mayo" who had "made millions in Mexican oil", saw his stake grow to $300,000 on paper, and handed over $37,000 "collateral" in Toledo. Weil, five months out of Leavenworth in Aug 1930, told a Chicago lineup in Jan 1931: "Sure, I am a con man — the best. But I've only taken from those who can afford the education."
  • The 1931 Lake Erie sea serpent — R22 (Top 10 #6; the entry's title runs "July 1931 → March 1935"): ten witnesses and an alligator head, then a "captured" python blessed by a Cleveland museum curator, then exposed as a carnival snake in Tampa. (Used only as the papers' own three layers; not re-opened.)

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a con man's sting, a record-building bridge, a bootleg war ended by a conviction; a summer of sea-serpent stories on the lake.
  • What the folklore says: none recorded for the bridge. Proposal: the Toledo riggers who hauled the two 83-ton girders in 1930 said the span was built to a plan the city engineer never drew, and that the name was fought over because a name on a bridge is a claim.
  • What is actually there: the Golden Circle's northern money. Its map is Havana-centred and the Great Lakes are its cold edge; Toledo is the Lake Erie rail-and-ore hub. "G. A. Mayo" is a Circle bagman working the Circle's oldest trick — paper fortunes that end in a real payout in the occupation zone; the money Weil's marks lose buys the Licavolis their Toledo war and, five years on, the bond interest on the steel. The Circle is never seen — only the arithmetic, and every column adds up to the same three names (§33: unease, not revelation).

Who sends the team

  • The Secret Service — forged and swindled paper is its old trade; Weil's $37,000 crossed a state line, and its file notes that one of Weil's "worthless" leases paid out (R33 hook, proposal). Callaghan's Chicago office (R33) forwards the Reinart file.
  • FBI Section 13 — the Licavoli war is the Detroit funnel's Bureau case; before May–June 1934 its men can neither carry guns nor arrest without borrowing a local policeman (R18 Top 10 #2), so it wants Toledo's police owing it — and wants the Service's file first.

Three clue sites

  1. The Anthony Wayne Bridge (R20) — the Harding-name fight was the old story; proposal: in this setting the fight was over President Leonard Wood (died on the operating table, Boston, Aug 1927). The outcry's petition is in one hand; the copyist works for a bond house.
  2. The hotel table where Reinart handed over $37,000 (the hotel is not named in R33; take it from the Indianapolis Times of 29 Apr 1931). Costed clue: the clerk who took the "collateral" envelope will talk — but his price is the team's own name on the register, which Licavoli's men read every morning (heat: they know who is looking).
  3. The river run (R26) — the Licavolis' Canadian route; the Coast Guard's night log; an ice road in winter.

The clock — Start: Apr 1931, Weil held and Reinart's $37,000 traced to Toledo. Escalation: the bridge opens in 1931 before 40,000 (the crowd is the cover for a hand-off). Real deadline: July 1933 — Jack Kennedy's murder, when the Circle's man stops needing a war. After: 1934, Licavoli convicted (the Circle sheds him); five years after the bridge, the extra bonds.

The divergence underneath — BENDS twice. The Harding fight cannot happen (no President Harding); the substitute is President Wood (R20's own suggestion, endorsed by V_R20). "Mexican oil" is not a crook's fantasy: the Mexican north is American-ceded, Tampico is held, and colonial speculation is the very boom the setting blames for the Crash (R33).

Another road — The guy: a Toledo ironworker who was on the 1930 girder hoist. The air: a Great Lakes radio operator who kept the Detroit River log. The ground: the Ohio Penitentiary — Licavoli's cell after 1934 (he will talk about the money, never about the Circle). A Doctor is not needed; a forger's eye is.

Twist — "J. A. Kirk" and "G. A. Mayo" were never Weil's characters. Weil bought them — the two names arrived from the buyer of his stings and he thought them a fine touch. The Circle uses the best con man in the Midwest as a laundry and lets him keep 10 percent; he is the only man in the file who has seen the Circle's face, and he has been told that if he tells the team, the Service's file will note that one of the leases was real.

Seed 32 — Niagara Square — proposal, not canon

Pitch: A Buffalo sculptor's wife is killed by a hammer and a chloroform rag after ouija-board séances, two women take the fall for a circle nobody ever tried — and the tower on Niagara Square rising over the trials is dedicated on the day the working is meant to close.

Real anchors

  • The Marchand case, Buffalo, 1930–31 — R22 (Marchand entry, with V_R22). Mrs. Clothilde Marchand, wife of sculptor Henri Marchand (making Seneca dioramas for the Buffalo museum), killed with a hammer and a chloroform-soaked rag at 576 Riley St., Buffalo. Nancy Bowen, a Seneca woman of 60, confessed; Lila Jimerson, 35, a Marchand model and lover, was accused. The UP headline of 8 Mar 1930: "OUIJA BOARD SEANCES LED TO ARTIST WIFE MURDER, INDIAN WOMAN CONFESSES." The first trial was a mistrial; a new jury was sworn in February 1931. Verdicts (confirmed, V_R22): Bowen pleaded guilty to manslaughter, one to ten years, serving about a year in the Erie County jail; Jimerson was acquitted at her second trial. Per V_R22, a ouija board spelled "Clothilde" and an address, in letters signed "Mrs. Dooley". ⟨note: R22 gives the murder date as 30 March 1930 ⟨fixed: V_R22⟩ yet prints a UP headline of 8 March 1930 — both cannot be right; take the date from a Buffalo paper before using it.⟩ (The chapter's "grave-dirt ring" and "paper-doll effigy" are [UNVERIFIED — V_R22] and are not used.)
  • Buffalo City Hall — R20 (Part 10). 65 Niagara Square; begun 16 September 1929, finished 10 November 1931, dedicated 1 July 1932 for Buffalo's centennial; 32 storeys, 398 ft, $6,851,546.85; polychrome terra-cotta chevrons, bands of American Indian motifs broken by stone eagles; friezes by Albert Stewart and René Paul Chambellan; a 28th-floor observation deck over Lake Erie and, on clear days, "the mists of Niagara Falls".
  • The Welland Ship Canal — R20/R30. The fourth canal opens 6 August 1932, 137 workers dead in the building.

The three layers

  • What the papers said: "modern police dealt with primitive belief" — the press's own framing; an artist's affairs called "a professional necessity".
  • What the folklore says: none for the circle. R22's real question: who taught a Buffalo sculptor's circle to use a ouija board?
  • What is actually there: a demon-summoning circle of the sculptor's own patrons — museum-and-studio people, not Seneca — used the séance table to spell a name and an address, then let two exploited women carry the blame. The working is not finished; the letters signed "Mrs. Dooley" were instructions. The Seneca are the population, not the cult: Bowen and Jimerson were used, and the team's first job is to keep the Guild from hunting a "witch" the press invented.

Who sends the team

  • FBI Section 13 — the papers' word "witchcraft" reaches its Buffalo clerks in March 1930; the Niagara corridor is federal ground; its mandate is to read what the newspapers make quaint. Before mid-1934 it must borrow a Buffalo detective for every arrest (R18).
  • The Hunter Guild — reads the same headlines as a bounty notice. It wants the "Indian witch" and has an offer out. It is the least safe to tell (§32), and it is wrong.

Three clue sites

  1. 576 Riley St. (R22) — the house; the ouija board in evidence with the court clerk. Costed clue: touching the board — 1 Sanity — and it spells an address in the team's own hand.
  2. The Buffalo museum's Seneca diorama room (R22) — Marchand's models; who signed the loan forms for the life-casts; who chaired the trustees' committee that paid for them.
  3. City Hall, the 28th-floor deck (R20) — and the building commission's field office, in the tower's first shell from 1929. The letters were typed on the commission's machine.

The clock — Start: Feb 1931, the second jury sworn. Escalation: 10 Nov 1931, the tower finished. Real deadline: 1 July 1932 — the dedication for Buffalo's centennial. The working closes when the flag goes up on a building covered in ward-like carved motifs (R20 hook, proposal). After: 6 Aug 1932, the Welland Canal opens — the corridor's water, the second working.

The divergence underneath — Holds (domestic calendar). Proposal: in the setting Buffalo is a border city of a Dominion drifting toward Washington (Canada under a bankrupt Britain), and the Niagara corridor's smuggling and power run under one of the costliest city halls in America (R20).

Another road — The guy: the Erie County jail matron who kept Bowen's year. The air: none — the trial's transcripts. The ground: Chambellan's and Stewart's studios: sculptors know exactly who commissioned the carvings and to what geometry.

Twist — "Mrs. Dooley" was real and was in the courtroom every day: the sculptor's patroness, a museum trustee, seated in the front row of both trials. She never wrote a word; she had a secretary. The secretary, Marchand's own housekeeper, still works for the trustee and is afraid — of the trustee, not of the police.

Seed 33 — Paid in Shares — proposal, not canon

Pitch: In the worst year of the Depression Toronto builds a hockey palace in five and a half months and pays its workers in stock — and the men who take shares in it are buying a claim on something older than the building.

Real anchors

  • Maple Leaf Gardens — R20 Part 10 (V_R20 ⟨fixed⟩). Conn Smythe's contract was let 30 May 1931; construction began at midnight on 1 June 1931 ⟨fixed: V_R20⟩; the arena opened 12 November 1931, five and a half months later; cost about C$1.5 million; land from Eaton's for $350,000; Thomson Brothers bid just under $990,000; unions took shares in Maple Leaf Gardens Ltd. in part instead of wages; 12,473 seats, 14,550 with standing room; Carlton Street at Church Street.
  • The murdered keeper of Gibraltar Point — R22. Keeper John Paul Radelmüller died violently on 2 January 1815 at Gibraltar Point Lighthouse, Toronto Islands. Lore says soldiers from Fort York, wanting bootleg beer, quarrelled with him and cut up the body; in 1893 keeper George Durnan dug near the light and found part of a jawbone and coffin fragments. Recent research (Eamonn O'Keeffe) confirms a murder, names the accused as John Henry and John Blueman of the Glengarry Light Infantry, and doubts the dismemberment. Called "Toronto's most enduring ghost story" (when the ghost was first reported: not stated on the page).
  • The Prince Edward Viaduct — R20. Opened 18 October 1918; about $2.48 million; Works Commissioner R. C. Harris insisted on a lower deck for rapid transit that did not exist — empty until the Bloor–Danforth subway used it in 1966.
  • The lake in the ledger — R30: Ben Kerr, "King of the Lake Ontario rum-runners", vanished on the ice in February 1929 and was found in the thaw near Colborne.

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a young owner's miracle of Depression building, a payroll met in paper and stock.
  • What the folklore says: legend says a keeper was murdered in 1815 for beer and his jawbone came up in 1893 — a keeper digging for his predecessor.
  • What is actually there: a ghost bound by debt. Radelmüller did not die over beer; he was killed so the light would be dark on a January night. The Hunter Guild's Toronto lodge dug in 1893 not to find him but to bind him, and the Gardens' midnight groundbreaking is the second binding — a room the Guild has rented from the dead. The union men paid in shares have been made, without knowing it, shareholders in a ghost.

Who sends the team

  • The Hunter Guild — it sends because a bound thing is loosening: the Toronto lodge's binder died in the spring; the Chicago Guild is sending outside hands (the compromise §32: a Guild that hires its own out).
  • IHS — stands aside from a door to the dead (§32); its man in the archdiocese wants the keeper released, not bound, and will say so only once.

Three clue sites

  1. Gibraltar Point Lighthouse, Toronto Islands (R22) — the 1893 dig's spoil-heap, still lipped with coffin nails. Costed clue: standing the keeper's watch through one January night costs 1 Sanity, and it answers with the two accused men's names.
  2. Carlton and Church, the Gardens' site (R20) — the foundation men's payroll book; who chose midnight; and the small block of union shares no union owns.
  3. The Prince Edward Viaduct's empty lower deck (R20) — reached by a maintenance ladder; proposal: the Guild's lodge meets here, on a railway deck with no trains, because the dead keeper cannot cross running water under it (R22: Hull-House kept water on its attic stairs against a ghost — the period rule).

The clock — Start: midnight, 1 June 1931, the first shift. Apparent deadline: none. Real deadline: 12 November 1931 — opening night, 14,550 in the building at full roar; the binding is meant to close on the crowd. After: the next thaw — the lake keeps its own ledger (Ben Kerr came up in the thaw of February 1929, R30).

The divergence underneath — BENDS lightly (R20): Toronto in a world where Britain lost; the Guild's Toronto lodge is a Dominion outpost with no Crown to answer to, so a Roosevelt-founded order may hold real estate there openly.

Another road — The guy: a Maple Leaf Gardens carpenter paid in shares. The air: the lighthouse's own radio log. The ground: Toronto's Palace of Purification (R20, 1932–41) — the water-works nobody mentions, running under the same lake. A Holy Man at the keeper's window does more than any gun.

Twist — The Guild man on the site is the one who owns the shares — a fully paid block, the "founder's share" — and is himself the Guild lodge's second binder: he believes the ghost is the only thing keeping the Leafs' arena from burning and has been told, by the Guild, that if the building empties it will burn.

Seed 34 — Eight Days Without Money — proposal, not canon

Pitch: On 14 February 1933 Michigan's banks close, shopkeepers talk about food coupons — and an old French-speaking Ste. Anne's parishioner sees a red-faced dwarf on the beach where nobody has seen him since 1812.

Real anchors

  • The Union Guardian run and the eight-day holiday — R26 §C (V_R26 checked). Michigan declared a banking holiday on 14 February 1933 after "troubled Detroit banks were unable to secure loans from the RFC" (Federal Reserve History); Governor William Comstock's eight-day holiday was the domino for Roosevelt's national bank holiday of 6 March 1933. Reuters, from Detroit on 15 Feb: "A serious run on the Union Guardian Trust bank, of which Henry Ford and his son Edsel Ford are directors, precipitated the Michigan State bank crisis"; a clearing-house scheme limited withdrawals to five per cent; shopkeepers discussed food coupons; the governor blamed Ford's refusal to subordinate his $7,500,000 deposit — then retracted it the same day; $40 million was rushed from the New York and Chicago Federal Reserve banks by midnight. The Guardian Building: 500 Griswold Street, "Cathedral of Finance", 1929.
  • The Nain Rouge — R22 (Hamlin, printed 1883/84; V_R26: earliest source Hamlin, no recorded 1930s sighting). In 1701 the fortune-teller Mère Minique told Cadillac: "Appease the Nain Rouge (Red Dwarf). Beware of offending him." In 1704 Cadillac met it on the beach and struck at it; it then marked Bloody Run (1763), the 1805 fire, and a foggy morning before Hull's surrender (1812) — after which the written legend has it retire. So any 1930s sighting is a return.
  • Le Lutin — R22: the horned goblin that rides Grosse Pointe habitants' horses by night; the proof is a broken holy-water font and a dropped thorn stick; farmers marked their horses with a cross (Hamlin, 1883).
  • The Cleveland echo — R25: Union Trust and Guardian Trust "died" on Saturday 25 February 1933, eleven days after Detroit's.

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a run started by the Fords' refusal; a governor who blamed a man in the morning and retracted by evening.
  • What the folklore says: the harbinger appears before a disaster; it quit in 1812; Cadillac's line is still the city's unpaid debt.
  • What is actually there: the fae. Cadillac did not appease it; the city has been paying instead — the Guardian Group's "loans", secured on nothing, have been a nightly tithe since 1929. When money stops for eight days, the tithe stops — and a fae host that is owed collects in what money cannot buy.

Who sends the team

  • The Hunter Guild — its Detroit lodge wants "the Red Dwarf" hunted before the holiday ends. The Guild is wrong about what it can be hunted with (§28: cold iron, and a compact).
  • The Secret Service — Treasury is the holiday's owner; it wants the banks reopened, no rumours in the queues, and a "monster hunt" in Woodward Avenue is a run in the making.

Three clue sites

  1. The Guardian Building, 500 Griswold (R26) — the night-watchman's book; the vault door left ajar at 3 a.m., as if someone were being let out.
  2. Grosse Pointe barns and garages (R22, R19) — the polo pony found foaming at dawn, the racing car with a hot engine and an empty tank: "Le Lutin come again" (R22 hook).
  3. Ste. Anne's parish, the old French quarter — the one old parishioner who saw it. Costed clue: to hear her, you must promise something aloud — and the fae hear promises: the team is now in the dwarf's debt for whatever they say.

The clock — Start: 14 Feb 1933, the holiday declared. Apparent deadline: the holiday's eighth day. Real deadline: 25 Feb 1933 — Cleveland's Union Trust and Guardian Trust fail (the tithe has moved a lake east); then 6 Mar 1933, the national holiday: the tithe is owed everywhere.

The divergence underneath — BENDS: in the setting the Depression was set off by colonial speculation and German sanctions, not the record's causes (R26); Detroit's banks would have failed on car exports shut out of Europe and colonial paper — but the Michigan holiday of 14 Feb and the national one of 6 Mar still land where the record puts them.

Another road — The guy: a French-speaking Ste. Anne's sexton. The air: the Reuters wire itself — read the clipping. The ground: Belle Isle's Snake God legend (R22) points the same way. A Holy Man with a holy-water font in the Hamlin manner is the period-attested rule.

Twist — The Nain Rouge was never the disaster's harbinger. It was the Guardian Group's creditor. The run began because the officers' nightly payment failed the night the RFC refused the loan — and the man who kept the ledger of the payments is the Ford Motor Company's lawyer on the Guardian board, who is now negotiating the return of the deposit and the dwarf's terms.

Seed 35 — The Portable Altar — proposal, not canon

Pitch: A Cleveland museum buys the largest share of a Brunswick cathedral's treasure — and a small gold altar in its gallery has a trap door in the base that opens on the bones of saints wrapped in silk, which someone has begun to open at night.

Real anchors

  • The Guelph Treasure comes to Cleveland — R25 (checked in V_R25, "All verbatim"). In 1929 Ernest Augustus, Duke of Brunswick, head of the House of Hanover, sold 82 pieces of the medieval church treasure of Brunswick Cathedral (gathered by the Welf dynasty) to four Frankfurt dealers — Saemy Rosenberg, Isaak Rosenbaum, Julius Falk Goldschmidt, Zacharias Hackenbroch — for 7.5 million Reichsmarks. The dealers toured it through the United States in 1930–31; the Cleveland Museum of Art bought nine pieces in 1931, more than any other buyer. Among them: the Portable Altar of Countess Gertrude (c. 1045; gold, cloisonné enamel, porphyry, gems, pearls, niello over wood; accession 1931.462, gift of the John Huntington Art and Polytechnic Trust), inscribed "Gertrude offers to Christ, to live joyfully in him, this stone that glistens with gems and gold." On the bottom of the altar is a trap door that opens on small relics of saints wrapped in silk. The treasure also held arm reliquaries (one of St. Blaise). The 42 unsold pieces went in 1935 for 4.25 million RM, in the Netherlands, to agents of Hermann Göring — now in Berlin's Bode Museum.
  • Cleveland's ground — R25: the Museum on University Circle (Wade Park); the Terminal Tower, dedicated 28 June 1930; Franklin Castle, 4308 Franklin Blvd., meeting house of German cultural clubs from 1921; Millionaires' Row, Euclid Avenue — "of the 40 great houses … 7 remained" by 1937; St. Ignatius, the Jesuits' West Side house (1886). R26: the German consul in Cleveland presented Henry Ford with a German Eagle on 30 July 1938.
  • IHS's American seat — BRIEF: John Carroll University, outside Cleveland.

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a Protestant museum trust's coup — nine pieces, more than any other buyer.
  • What the folklore says: none recorded. Proposal: museum guards say the small gold altar is the only object in the gallery they will not dust, and the Jesuits at St. Ignatius say the relics in it are awake.
  • What is actually there: vampires on Euclid Avenue — an old-money coterie whose members sit on the Huntington Trust — bought the altar to keep the relics behind glass, in a gallery where consecrated things are only "art". A vampire can walk past a reliquary in a museum; it cannot walk past one in a church. Someone has been opening the trap door at night and putting something in.

Who sends the team

  • IHS — saints' relics sitting in Protestant hands, in its own city. It wants the altar returned to a church — quietly.
  • FBI Section 13 — at odds: it will not have a museum scandal, and it has a file on the German consul; a "German sale made on purpose" (see below) is a Berlin matter, and the Bureau would rather the altar stay where the consul can be watched.

Three clue sites

  1. The Cleveland Museum of Art, the treasure gallery (R25) — the altar itself, accession 1931.462. Costed clue: Psychometry on the silk inside the trap door (1 Mana + 1 Sanity) shows the last hand to open it wearing a signet — and a small, dark, dry thing being added, wrapped as carefully as any saint.
  2. The German consulate, Cleveland (R26; address not in the chapter) — the consul who would present a German Eagle to Ford in 1938 already knows the treasure's shipping papers.
  3. Millionaires' Row and the Terminal Tower's Union Terminal (R25) — the last of the great houses, and the underground station where a coterie's members change trains without being seen.

The clock — Start: 1930, the touring exhibition opens. Real deadline: the 1931 accession year — when the altar is catalogued as art, the museum's title (and the vampires' safety) closes. After: 1935, the other 42 pieces sail to Berlin; 1937, Millionaires' Row is nearly razed and a coterie has to move.

The divergence underneath — BENDS (R25). The 1929 sale grew from the Hanovers' postwar losses and the 1935 sale from Nazi Germany (Göring); in a Germany-won world the Duke of Brunswick (the Kaiser's son-in-law) is not broke and there is no Göring. Proposal: the sale is made on purpose — Berlin chose to seed a relic-cache in the IHS's own city, and a victorious Reich wants it back at a time of its choosing.

Another road — The guy: a museum night-guard from the Guelph exhibition. The air: none. The ground: the Van Sweringens' Terminal Tower and its underground (R25, R20); a Holy Man at the altar with the trap door open does more than the whole treasure.

Twist — The thing being added to the trap door is a vampire's own relic — the coterie's founder, the first of them, laid to rest in the only place a Cleveland vampire can be sure no priest will ever look: inside a saint's altar. IHS has been walking past it at every Mass it attends.

Seed 36 — Twelve-Oh-One — proposal, not canon

Pitch: Milwaukee kept its lagering cellars for thirteen dry years on cheese, soap and chocolate. At 12:01 a.m. on 7 April 1933 the doors open — and something in one cellar has been waiting since 1920.

Real anchors

  • Beer Night — R27 §9 (V_R27-checked). The Cullen–Harrison Act made 3.2% beer legal from 7 April 1933; "throngs gathered outside breweries and taverns." Seed 43 — Well Number Four — proposal, not canon

Pitch: The state knew for eight years that Manteno's well drank from a sewer. The first case on 8 July 1939 fell ill on a ward where a patient had been drawing that same well, in chalk, for weeks.

Real anchors

  • The Manteno typhoid, from the state's own report — R31 (V_R31 #1 CONFIRMED, exact: the Illinois Department of Public Health's 1945 report, NLM copy). Manteno State Hospital, Kankakee County, about 40 miles south of Chicago, "100 buildings virtually comprise a small municipality"; about 6,200 people. The first case fell ill on 8 July 1939; cases peaked on 15 August among employees and 23 August among inmates; the outbreak ran into October: 453 cases, 60 deaths. The cause was Well No. 4, one of four wells a few hundred feet apart, drawing from creviced limestone and cased with 14-inch pipe to a depth of only 19 feet, barely a foot into rock; a sanitary sewer ran about 35 feet away, and sewage had backed up 30 inches deep in a manhole 80 feet from the well. Typhoid organisms were cultured from the well on 22–31 August. The activated-sludge plant, built in 1930, was designed for 3,000 and was taking the waste of 6,200. The Department of Public Health had warned in written reports in 1931, 1935 and 1937 that the limestone was dangerous and that officials were not exercising "reasonable care". Chlorinators went in 19 and 26 August; by 5 September everyone had had three doses of vaccine; films, dances, church services and all gatherings were stopped, visitors banned; two cottages, Clouston and McDowell, became typhoid wards; nine state nurses arrived 20 September; an amoebic dysentery case followed 13 October; cost $120,000. The wards' excreta were boiled down in 90-gallon cauldrons until only a paste remained, and the paste was thrown on the fire and burned.
  • The asylum as door — R31: the "insane cop" — John Howe, a state highway policeman indicted in 1930 on four holdup charges, committed to Kankakee instead of Chester, escaped "soon afterward" and hired at $175 a month in 1936, exposed in the Tribune of 22 January 1940; outcome UNVERIFIED. (The chapter's "patients as servants in the village" and "village's own well" passage is [UNVERIFIED — V_R31] and is not used.)
  • The locked places' chaplains — R35 §8: the Church's own list of chaplains to the asylums, jails and sanatoria in 1938.

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a state's neglect — warnings in three separate years, a plant built for half the people it served.
  • What the folklore says: none recorded. Proposal: the patients called the well by a name the staff never used, and would not drink from it on a Friday.
  • What is actually there: the fae. A well cased only 19 feet into limestone is a threshold, and the hospital's 6,200 guests are under the oldest law there is. The state's three warnings each ended with a quiet payment from the patients' unclaimed effects; the boiling and burning of the paste was, to the watching patients, a rite — and to one of them, a correct one. The 60 dead are a tithe, and the well's court is in no hurry.

Who sends the team

  • IHS — the Church's chaplains sit in state hospitals (R35); Catholic patients died by the dozen, and the order has a list of parish sacristies in the locked places.
  • The Hunter Guild — at odds: it wants to salt the well and burn the wards — a plan that kills the living. Its price is the state's silence.

Three clue sites

  1. Well No. 4 (R31) — a cased hole with a sewer 35 feet off; chalk drawings on the pump-house wall. Costed clue: a Woodsman (You don't lose them) reads the limestone's cracks; going down with a lantern costs 1 Sanity and the light is not the lantern's.
  2. Clouston and McDowell cottages (R31) — the typhoid wards; the nurses' rosters of 20 September; the ward where the first case was.
  3. The Department of Public Health's file room, Springfield — the 1931, 1935 and 1937 warnings; each one's marginal note, in a different hand, ending the same way.

The clock — Start: 8 July 1939, the first case. Escalation: 15 Aug (employees' peak); 19 and 26 Aug (chlorinators). Real deadline: 23 August 1939 — the inmates' peak; the well's count must reach its number. After: 5 Sept (vaccine), 13 Oct (amoebic dysentery), October (the outbreak ends).

The divergence underneath — Holds (domestic calendar). Proposal: in the setting the state hospitals were quietly expanded by the Southern War's casualties — Manteno's 6,200 includes veterans nobody is counting.

Another road — The guy: a Kankakee County well-driller who set the 1930 casing. The air: the state's own report (archive.org), read for what it does not say. The ground: the limestone. A Doctor, obviously; a Holy Man at a ward's window is what the chaplains have.

Twist — A ward nurse hired in June 1939 — invention, not any real staff member — reported the chalk drawings, and is the first case. She is a changeling, a patient placed on staff as the well's hand, and she has been helping the state keep its promises for nine years by not telling it what they were.

Seed 44 — Levitation — proposal, not canon

Pitch: A millionaire's estate on the Fox River hired a Harvard physicist to tune a levitation machine decoded from Sir Francis Bacon — and the man who paid for it dies on 17 May 1936, which is when a working lapses.

Real anchors

  • Riverbank, Geneva, Illinois — R29 (V_R29 checked, all CONFIRMED). Col. George Fabyan's 325-acre estate: a Japanese garden, a private zoo, a Roman-style pool, a lighthouse, a Dutch windmill moved in 1914; Riverbank Laboratories founded 1913. Elizabeth Wells Gallup's Baconian cipher team looked for Francis Bacon's hidden messages in Shakespeare; William Friedman and Elizebeth Smith met there and married. In 1913 Fabyan hired Harvard's Wallace Clement Sabine to help tune an acoustical levitation machine built according to specifications decoded from a Sir Francis Bacon work; Sabine convinced him it would not work. Agnes Meyer Driscoll was on staff; the NSA has a 1993 plaque crediting Riverbank's cryptologic training. Fabyan died 17 May 1936 (pleurisy); widow Nelle died 1939; the county bought the estate for $70,500.
  • The Cliff Dwellers' founder digs for crosses — R19 (V_R19 #13 CONFIRMED): Hamlin Garland (Pulitzer 1922; psychic research from 1891) spent the 1930s digging for a medium's buried crosses; Forty Years 1936; Buried Crosses 1939 "prove the legitimacy of psychic mediums"; died 4 March 1940, ashes at West Salem. The club: the ninth-storey penthouse over Orchestra Hall, 220 S. Michigan.
  • The Secret Service's own founder — BRIEF: Col. William P. Wood, 1865; the old guard's founding is a cipher story too.

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a rich man's eccentric laboratory that trained the country's best codebreakers.
  • What the folklore says: a millionaire who believed Shakespeare was Bacon and built the proof; Geneva says the lighthouse light burns when no one has lit it (proposal).
  • What is actually there: demon summoners' text — the "Bacon work" was not Bacon's; it is a Renaissance summoner's grimoire in a period cipher, and the decoding was read aloud in a laboratory by trained acousticians. The levitation machine worked, once; Sabine's report that it did not work is a lie told to keep it from being repeated. The working is bound to its patron: when Fabyan dies, it lapses — and something that was being held in the air comes down.

Who sends the team

  • The Kabbalah of David — the cipher's tongue is what matters: a summoner's text in a period cipher is a Kabbalist's proper reading, and the Church forbids its own scholars to read it (R34 note).
  • The Secret Service — at odds: Riverbank's cipher school is the country's own codebreaking origin, and the Service wants the estate's papers sealed before the county buys it.

Three clue sites

  1. The Riverbank lighthouse and windmill (R29) — the two oddest buildings on the grounds; the lighthouse's lamp. Costed clue: reading the cipher key aloud — 1 Sanity per line, and it takes three to finish; one reading is free and tells you it is a summons, not a code.
  2. The Cliff Dwellers, 220 S. Michigan (R19) — Garland's members and his buried crosses; the Chicago end of the estate's correspondence.
  3. The Riverbank Laboratories' acoustic room (R29) — the levitation machine's chamber; the plaster still holds a low note.

The clock — Start: spring 1936, Fabyan failing. Real deadline: 17 May 1936 — his death. After: 1939, Nelle dies; the county buys the estate for $70,500 and the public gets the lighthouse.

The divergence underneath — BENDS lightly (V_R29 divergence sweep): Riverbank's cipher school trained the army in a Great War America entered; in the setting America never went, so whom Riverbank trained for is the owner's call. Proposal: it trained the Army's Southern War codebreakers — the occupation's ciphers.

Another road — The guy: an ex-Riverbank student from the Friedman class. The air: a Circuit Man (Dead Frequencies) hears the low note in the plaster. The ground: the Fox River bank. An Arcanist can Unpick the working once the text is in hand.

Twist — Sabine did tune it, and Fabyan's real secret was that it was wrong: the machine was built to specifications decoded backwards, and the thing it holds aloft is not Bacon's summons but its reflection — which is lighter, and older, and can be asked to leave.

Seed 45 — The Typewriter Box — proposal, not canon

Pitch: A retired greeting-card man of 72 is taken from Franklin Park, the ransom goes in a typewriter box, and the killer's own partner dies beside him in a hole in the Wisconsin woods — because the hole was not the killers' to dig.

Real anchors

  • The Charles S. Ross kidnapping — R19 §6 (V_R19 #28 CONFIRMED; ⟨fixed: V_R19⟩). Charles Sherman Ross, 72, a retired greeting-card executive, was taken on 25 September 1937 from Franklin Park, Cook County, by John Henry Seadlund and James Atwood Gray. The family paid $50,000 on 8 October near Rockford. On 10 October, at the hideout dugout near Spooner, Wisconsin, Seadlund killed both Ross and his own partner Gray ("We struggled around … but I got my finger on the trigger and pulled it"). Seadlund was taken in Los Angeles on 14 January 1938; he had buried $32,645 of the ransom in a typewriter box; he pleaded guilty ⟨fixed: V_R19⟩ and was electrocuted at the Cook County Jail on 14 July 1938, the last person executed in Illinois on a federal warrant. Hoover (K. Edgar in the setting) called him "the nation's cruelest criminal"; K. Edgar Hoover's FBI was the federal hunter, its Section 13 if the file turns strange, and the Secret Service is a separate, rival agency.
  • The Lindbergh Law's seven-day clock — R18 Top 10 #3 ⟨fixed: V_R18⟩. From May 1934 a kidnapping is presumed interstate (federal) on day eight; the 24-hour rule is a 1956 amendment and does not exist in the 1930s. From 25 Sept 1937 that clock runs out on Saturday 2 October 1937.
  • The rich's daily fear — R19: by 1934 the Lindbergh, Urschel, Hamm, Bremer and Boettcher cases had made kidnapping the rich's daily fear; the Hearst Sunday feature described J. P. Morgan at a flower show (the seed's setting colour).

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a cruel, lucky kidnapper; a $50,000 ransom; an execution the following summer.
  • What the folklore says: none recorded for Spooner. Proposal: the northwoods' loggers say the country around the Yellow and Namekagon rivers is not empty, and that Christmas Eve brings something down from it.
  • What is actually there: werewolves — a Wisconsin northwoods pack whose ground the kidnappers dug into. Seadlund and Gray did not choose the woods; the woods chose them — a gang of two dug a dugout where a pack's boundary is, and the pack cleaned up its own edge: Gray, the second-rate partner, shot beside the victim. Seadlund lived because he was the useful one and left with a shape in the dark he never named. Ross, a man with no enemies, was witness to what the pack does with its own and could not be allowed to leave.

Who sends the team

  • FBI Section 13 — the Bureau's case, with a difference: a file that stops at "we struggled around … I got my finger on the trigger" and does not say who dug the hole.
  • The Hunter Guild — at odds: it sells "protection" to the rich (R19's kidnap-fear entry) and would rather the pack stay as a subscription business; a solved Spooner is a lost client.

Three clue sites

  1. Franklin Park, Cook County (R19) — the abduction site; the neighbours who saw a second car after Seadlund's.
  2. The Rockford drop, 8 October (R19) — the Bureau's dossier: the ransom bills were marked; Seadlund did not spend them.
  3. The Spooner dugout (R19, Wisconsin northwoods) — costed clue: a Woodsman (You don't lose them) reads the ground — the pack's boundary is a ring of stakes cut by no logger; crossing it at night costs 1 Sanity and a night's tracking.

The clock — Start: 25 September 1937. Real deadlines: Saturday 2 October 1937 (day eight: the Lindbergh Law's presumption of federal jurisdiction), then 8 October (the drop) and 10 October (Ross and Gray killed at Spooner). After: 14 January 1938, Seadlund taken in Los Angeles; 14 July 1938, executed — the pack's debt is paid when he dies.

The divergence underneath — BENDS (R19): the Bureau's case in the setting is K. Edgar Hoover's — and the Service, which is not Hoover's, is the rival that wants the ransom's serial numbers for its own reasons (R33: "Know Your Money").

Another road — The guy: a Spooner logger who cut the road to the dugout. The air: the ransom drop's telephone call, traceable to a single pay-phone near Rockford. The ground: the Yellow River country, a Woodsman's whole trade.

Twist — Seadlund's confession was the pack's bargain, offered in the Cook County Jail cell and kept to the day: "I got my finger on the trigger and pulled it" is the only story the pack lets him tell. The typewriter box was never for money — it was the pack's price for his life, buried where they can find it.
