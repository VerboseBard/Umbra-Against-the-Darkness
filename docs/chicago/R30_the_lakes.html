Overview

Between 1925 and 1940 the Great Lakes were still a working sea with a sea's dead. The autumn of 1929 alone took five ships in seven weeks on Lake Michigan and Superior — Andaste (Sept 9–10, never found), Milwaukee (Oct 22, all 52), Wisconsin (Oct 29, the night of Black Tuesday), Senator (Oct 31, 268 new Nash cars, sank in eight minutes in fog) and Kiowa (Nov 30) — and the Andaste coroner's jury, unable to say where or why she sank, told Congress the lakes needed radios and rescue cutters; the cutter Escanaba arrived at Grand Haven in November 1932 as the answer. The decade also killed the Chicago excursion boat Favorite in sight of the North Avenue beach (1927, 27 dead), the Material Service Corporation's motor freighter off Calumet with fifteen men (1936), and the last commercial schooner on the lakes, Our Son (1930, everyone saved); the Cleveland sand-sucker Sand Merchant (October 1936) is a lead I could not source this session. The Lighthouse Service was still a civilian service until 1 July 1939, when Roosevelt's reorganisation folded it into the Coast Guard; the Coast Guard itself spent 1925–33 fighting a "rum war" on the Detroit River and the lakes (the named shootings are standard history but not confirmed here — see Leads); Lake Ontario's rum king Ben Kerr was found dead in the ice in 1929 with no cause ever settled; the Great Lakes Naval Training Station at North Chicago — a WWI creation that trained 125,000 men in 1917–18 — was closed to a maintenance status on 1 July 1933 and reopened 1 July 1935 only after Illinois lobbying (this BENDS: no American Great War means no 1917 expansion). The fourth Welland Canal opened 6 Aug 1932 after 137 workers died building it. Goodrich, the Chicago lake-passenger line since 1856, went bankrupt in 1933 and the whaleback Christopher Columbus was cut up at Manitowoc in 1936. The ore trade nearly stopped in 1932. Under all of it is the lore — the lake that never gives up its dead, the three sisters, the Witch of November, the Christmas Tree ship — and a set of genuinely unanswered things: where the Andaste lies, what note the captain really wrote, what became of the crews nobody found.

A. The autumn of 1929 and the other wrecks, 1925–1940

The Senator sinks in eight minutes with 268 new Nash cars — 31 October 1929

  • Where: Lake Michigan, 16 miles north-east of Port Washington, Wisconsin; wreck in 450 ft of water (43°20′08″N 87°34′11″W).
  • What happened: In heavy fog on the morning after Black Tuesday's aftermath, the steel package freighter Senator (built 1896), carrying 268 Nash automobiles valued at $251,000 from Milwaukee to Detroit, collided with the ore carrier Marquette (Captain W. F. Amesbury, iron ore from Escanaba for the Indiana steel mills). The Senator went down in eight minutes. Of her 28 crew, nine died (the Wikipedia article's own figures waver between seven and nine), including her master, Captain George Kinch. A tug pulled 15 men out of the water. The wreck's exact position was found by Paul Ehorn and Rob Polich in 2005; it went on the National Register on 12 April 2016.
  • The odd detail: Two ships full of the decade's money — 268 unsold new cars and a hold of iron ore — meeting in fog two days after the market broke, and the car-ship being the one that sank. It was the third Lake Michigan loss in nine days (after the Milwaukee on the 22nd and the Wisconsin on the 29th).
  • People: Capt. George Kinch — master of Senator, lost; Capt. W. F. Amesbury — master of Marquette.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Senator_(1896)
  • Confidence: High on date, cargo, place, captain; Medium on the death toll (7 vs 9 in the same source).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): 268 new automobiles on the lake floor in 450 feet of water is a cache nobody could reach in 1929. Something that does not need air could. A Chicago Nash dealer who "lost" cars on the Senator files an insurance claim for cars that were never aboard — the Secret Service's counterfeit-and-fraud men pull the manifest and find the numbers are off by one car, and that one is parked outside a Gold Coast house.

The Andaste coroner's jury, the plank note, and the cutter they bought — 24 September 1929 → 23 November 1932 (deeper cut; the loss itself is in R07)

  • Where: Grand Haven, Michigan (inquest); wreckage field south-west of Holland; bodies from Holland north to Grand Haven; the ship was bound for Chicago's Calumet River with 2,000 tons of Grand River aggregate under charter to the Construction Materials Company of Chicago.
  • What happened: The semi-whaleback Andaste (Capt. Albert L. Anderson of Sturgeon Bay, 25 aboard including a 14-year-old steward's boy, Earl Zietlow) passed the Grand Haven piers at 9:03 p.m. on 9 September 1929 into a gale that reached 60 mph by 1 a.m. She had no radio. Wreckage was sighted on the 12th; 16 of the 25 bodies came ashore, 11 of them in life-jackets. On 24 September the coroner's jury ruled that she "had sunk for unknown reasons at an unknown location" — and issued three recommendations: radios on all large lake vessels, a central marine office on each lake to report missing ships, and proper rescue facilities on each lake. Congress funded six Coast Guard cutters for the lakes; the Escanaba was commissioned at Grand Haven on 23 November 1932 as the direct result. A "splintered plank or piece of board siding" was found bearing the words "Worst storm I have ever been in. Can't stay up much longer. Hope to God we are saved. A.L.A." — the captain's initials; its authenticity was disputed at the time, sceptics arguing a master of Anderson's experience would not have written so incomplete a note. The Michigan Shipwreck Research Association began a formal search in December 2006 and recovered an original name board in 2010; the hull has never been found.
  • The odd detail: The verdict itself — a lake so close to Chicago that a ship can vanish on it "at an unknown location" for "unknown reasons" — and a message that may or may not be the captain's, scrawled on a plank.
  • People: Capt. Albert L. Anderson — master, Sturgeon Bay; Earl Zietlow — 14-year-old steward's assistant, lost; the Grand Haven coroner's jury (24 Sept 1929) — unnamed in the source.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Andaste (which cites michiganshipwrecks.org's Andaste page and the Sandusky Register AP report of 14 Sept 1929)
  • Confidence: High on the inquest, note text and Escanaba link; the note's authenticity is genuinely unsettled in the record — that is the point.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A plank note in a dead man's initials, disputed by the men who knew him, is exactly the "three layers of truth" object: the period reads it as a hoax, the folklore reads it as a last message, and what is actually there is a message written after the ship went down. The Grand Haven jury's three recommendations mean that from 1932 there is a federal cutter on Lake Michigan whose reason for existing is a ship no one can find — an FBI Section 13 liaison aboard the Escanaba is a natural player posting.

Our Son, the last commercial schooner on the lakes, goes down in a gale — 26 September 1930

  • Where: Central Lake Michigan, off Sheboygan, Wisconsin; bound for the Central Paper Company mill at Muskegon.
  • What happened: The three-masted schooner Our Son, built in 1875, was by 1930 one of the very last sail-powered vessels still earning freight on the Great Lakes — in practice the last working commercial schooner. On 26 September 1930, loaded with pulpwood for Muskegon under Captain Fred Nelson with a crew of seven, she was caught by storm winds that left her helpless and filling. The lake carrier William Nelson, herself "barely owning steerageway" in the seas, worked alongside and took off every man before the schooner sank. Nobody died.
  • The odd detail: The age of sail on the lakes ended on a specific date in a specific storm with a rescue by a steamer that shared the captain's surname — and the old man's ship sank under him only after the last man was off.
  • People: Capt. Fred Nelson — master and owner of Our Son (his age, reported in popular accounts as about 70, is UNVERIFIED here); Capt. Charles Mohr — master of the William Nelson (UNVERIFIED; not in the page read).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Son_(schooner)
  • Confidence: High on date, place, crew, rescuer; Medium on the captains' details beyond the page.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The last sailing ship on the lake and the last of the old sailors' knowledge going with her — a Hunter Guild veteran who learned his trade on schooners is now the only man in Chicago who remembers what the lake used to be told. The Kabbalahs might note the date: the equinox week.

The George M. Cox wrecks on the Rock of Ages on her maiden cruise — 27 May 1933

  • Where: Rock of Ages reef, 3.5 miles west of Isle Royale, Lake Superior; the lighthouse stands on a rock 117 ft tower, focal height 130 ft.
  • What happened: The 270-foot steamer George M. Cox (built 1901 as the Puritan, bought and renamed by promoter George M. Cox) was on her first cruise for her new owner, with special guests aboard, when she struck the reef in heavy fog although the crew heard the foghorn. Keeper John Soldenski of the Rock of Ages Light towed the life-rafts to his lighthouse; 127 passengers and crew spent the night crowded into the tower and on the surrounding rocks. No one was killed. The hull stayed on the rocks until an October storm broke her up; she sank into 10–100 ft beside two older wrecks, the Cumberland (1877) and the Henry Chisholm (1898).
  • The odd detail: A brand-new venture's first night out ends with a hundred and twenty-seven people sleeping on a bare rock in Lake Superior under a lighthouse, in the same week Chicago's fair opened (the fair opened the same day, 27 May 1933).
  • People: George M. Cox — owner-promoter; John Soldenski — keeper, Rock of Ages Light.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_George_M._Cox (redirects to USS Puritan (ID-2222)); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_of_Ages_Light
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A promoter renames a ship after himself and she dies on her first voyage: the sailors' rule against renaming (see section H) played out in the record. What was the "special guest" list? A vampire's yacht party stranded on a rock till dawn is a scene that writes itself.

The America sinks at Isle Royale with everyone saved — 7 June 1928

  • Where: North gap of Washington Harbor, Isle Royale, Michigan (Lake Superior).
  • What happened: The Booth Fisheries Company's steamer America (built 1898 by Detroit Dry Dock) ran the Duluth–Isle Royale–Thunder Bay round with passengers, mail, packages and the fishermen's catch. Leaving Washington Harbor in the small hours of 7 June 1928 she hit a submerged reef; the captain tried to beach her but she struck a second reef and settled in the gap. All 16 passengers and 31 crew got into the boats. She lies bow in 2 ft and stern in 85 ft of water, the most-dived wreck in the park.
  • The odd detail: The island's only link to the mainland sank in its own front door; the fishing families' mail and supply line was gone in one night. (A popular story that a dog chained aboard was the only casualty is NOT in the page read — UNVERIFIED.)
  • People: the master — unnamed in the page read.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_America_(1898)
  • Confidence: High on the facts stated; the dog is unverified.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Isle Royale in the 1930s is a place of Scandinavian fishing families, a lighthouse crew that once ran down to a single can of tomatoes, and wrecks stacked on the same reefs decade after decade. A place that eats boats at its doorway is a place the Hunter Guild would send someone to look at.

The Material Service goes down off Calumet with fifteen men — 29 July 1936

  • Where: Lake Michigan, north-east of Calumet Harbor, off North Township, Lake County, Indiana (41°44′20″N 87°30′14″W) — within sight of the Chicago–Indiana line.
  • What happened: The Material Service was a purpose-built, steel, diesel, self-unloading "workbarge" (239.6 ft, 1,077 GRT, hull #253, built 1929 by Leathem D. Smith at Sturgeon Bay), owned by the Leathem Smith-Putnam Navigation Company and under ten-year lease to the Material Service Corporation of Chicago — Henry Crown's sand-and-gravel company. She was designed low with a lowerable A-frame to pass under the Chicago River bridges, carrying sand from the lake to the river docks. On 29 July 1936, in the record heat summer, she sank off Calumet with the loss of fifteen lives. (The page read gives no survivor count, captain's name, cause or inquiry finding — those are leads.)
  • The odd detail: A vessel built to duck under Chicago's bridges dying on the open lake within sight of the steel mills; fifteen dead in Chicago's own harbour approach in mid-summer, in the same month the heat killed hundreds on shore (see R13).
  • People: Henry Crown — Material Service Corporation, Chicago (lessee).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Material_Service
  • Confidence: Medium-High on the facts above; cause, names and survivors UNVERIFIED here (see Leads).

The Tashmoo, Detroit's "White Flyer" — breaks loose in a gale (8 Dec 1927) and dies at Amherstburg (18 June 1936)

  • Where: Detroit River — Belle Isle Bridge (1927); Sugar Island to Amherstburg, Ontario (1936).
  • What happened: The sidewheel excursion steamer Tashmoo (launched 30 Dec 1899 at Wyandotte; 320 ft; 20+ knots; nicknamed the "White Flyer" and, for her glass, the "Glass Hack") ran Detroit–Port Huron with a stop at Tashmoo Park for the White Star Line. On 8 December 1927 she snapped her moorings in a gale and drifted up the Detroit River, hit a ferry and struck the Belle Isle Bridge before tugs got lines on her; she was repaired. On 18 June 1936, leaving Sugar Island with an evening excursion, she struck a submerged rock; the captain ran for Amherstburg, Ontario, landed everyone at the dock, and she settled in 18 ft of water there. The hull was scrapped. She was inducted into the National Maritime Hall of Fame in 1985.
  • The odd detail: A ship that once ran away by herself up the Detroit River in a December night, and nine years later chose to die at the dock after everyone had walked off. (The 1936 passenger count — often given as about 1,400 — is NOT in the page read; UNVERIFIED.)
  • People: the White Star Steamship Company of Detroit — owners.

The Manasoo rolls over in Georgian Bay; six men on a raft for sixty hours — 15 September 1928

  • Where: Georgian Bay, Lake Huron, off Griffith Island (wreck half a mile off, 210 ft down).
  • What happened: The Owen Sound Transportation Company's Manasoo (built 1888 as the Macassa, renamed 1928) left Manitowaning at noon on 14 September with 19 crew, two passengers, 115 cows and one bull. In a storm on the 15th she listed to port, and as the captain tried to beach her she rolled over. The one lifeboat launched capsized; a life-raft broke free and six men, Captain McKay among them, got onto it. Chief Engineer Thomas McCutcheon died of exposure on the raft. After more than sixty hours the Canadian Pacific steamer Manitoba found them. Sixteen of the 21 people aboard died. The wreck was found on 30 June 2018 by Jerry Eliason, Ken Merryman and Cris Kohl — with a 1927 Chevrolet coupe still inside.
  • The odd detail: The cattle. A ship rolling over with a hundred and sixteen head aboard; and a ninety-year-old wreck opened to show a 1927 Chevrolet parked in the dark.
  • People: Capt. McKay — survived on the raft; Thomas McCutcheon — chief engineer, died on the raft.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Manasoo
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Canadian shore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Sixty hours on a raft in Georgian Bay with a dying man is a story a survivor tells in a Chicago bar in 1935 — and what the other five agreed never to say about the second night. Werewolf-adjacent: something had to be done with the cattle.

The whaleback Henry Cort on the Muskegon breakwater; the Escanaba's rescue; a Coast Guardsman lost — 30 November 1934

  • Where: North side of the Muskegon channel breakwater, Lake Michigan.
  • What happened: The whaleback freighter Henry Cort (built 1892 at Superior as the Pillsbury; sunk once by collision in Lake Erie in 1917 and raised; stranded on Colchester Reef in 1927; holed by ice at Ecorse in 1933) ran aground on the Muskegon breakwater in a 45-mph gale on 30 November 1934. The cutter Escanaba (see C) and the Muskegon Coast Guard got all 25 crew off by line. One Coast Guardsman was washed off the breakwater and drowned in the rescue. The December storms broke the Cort in two; she was scrapped in 1935.
  • The odd detail: A ship that had already died twice and been brought back — 1917, 1927 — and the only man who died at her third death was one of the rescuers.
  • People: the drowned Coast Guardsman — unnamed in the page read (popular accounts name him; UNVERIFIED here).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Henry_Cort ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USCGC_Escanaba_(WPG-77)

The City of Bangor and the 248 Chryslers driven off across the ice — 30 November 1926 → spring 1927 (living memory)

  • Where: Keweenaw Point, Lake Superior, near Copper Harbor, Michigan.
  • What happened: Carrying 248 new Chrysler automobiles from Detroit to Duluth, the City of Bangor (Capt. William J. Mackin) struck the rocks off the Keweenaw on 30 November 1926; 18 cars spiked to the deck slid overboard. The 29-man crew got ashore the next morning and set out on a 15-mile walk to Copper Harbor, lost their way, and spent a night in the open with a fire, no shelter, no food and inadequate clothing before the Eagle Harbor Coast Guard lifeboat found them; several were hospitalised with frostbite. That winter salvors built an ice ramp and drove the Chryslers off the wreck across the frozen lake to Copper Harbor; in spring they were trucked to Calumet, loaded on trains and returned to Detroit — 202 were sold. The same storm put the Thomas Maytham, carrying grain, aground on the other side of the peninsula. (The Canadian freighter Altadoc wrecked at the same point on 8 December 1927; noted in the Lake Superior wreck list, not detailed in the page read.)
  • The odd detail: A convoy of brand-new cars driven off a shipwreck over Lake Superior ice by men in overcoats — a scene from a dream, on the record.
  • People: Capt. William J. Mackin — master.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_City_of_Bangor ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_shipwrecks_in_Lake_Superior
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1929, holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): 202 cars sold as new in 1927 that had spent a winter on a wreck in the lake: any 1930s Chicagoan might own one. What rode across the ice with them?

The Hennepin, the world's first self-unloader, sinks under tow from Chicago — 18 August 1927 (living memory)

  • Where: Lake Michigan off South Haven, Michigan (42°27′39″N 86°41′47″W), about 200 ft down.
  • What happened: Built in Milwaukee in October 1888 as the George H. Dyer, burned in 1901 and rebuilt by the Lake Shore Stone Company with a conveyor belt, she became the world's first self-unloading ship (1,600 tons, quarry stone from Wisconsin). The Construction Materials Corporation bought her in 1923 to haul Grand River aggregate to Chicago; by 1927 her hull was so tired the engine was pulled and she was towed as a barge. On 18 August 1927, under tow of the tug Lotus out of Chicago for Grand Haven, a squall opened her seams at about 10:30 a.m.; the pumps lost, the crew went over to the tug at 2:30 p.m., and she sank about 6 p.m. Michigan Shipwreck Research Associates found her in 2006 while looking for another ship, and knew her by a capstan stamped "G.H. Dyer".
  • The odd detail: The same Chicago aggregate company lost the Andaste two years later on the same run — two of its boats on the bottom between Chicago and Grand Haven.
  • People: Construction Materials Corporation, Chicago — owner (also the Andaste's charterer).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Hennepin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_shipwrecks_in_Lake_Michigan
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1929, holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A Chicago sand company whose boats keep sinking on one lane — the Hennepin (1927), the Andaste (1929) — and a third firm's Material Service (1936) on the Calumet approach. Somebody in Chicago's building trades is paying a toll to the lake, or is being made to.

The Lakeland and the opened seacocks — 3 December 1924 (living memory)

  • Where: Lake Michigan, about 9 miles off Sturgeon Bay, Wisconsin, in 205 ft.
  • What happened: The steel freighter Lakeland (built 1887 at Cleveland as the Cambria, the first 300-footer on the lakes) left Chicago for Detroit with 22 Nash and Kissel automobiles and one Rollin aboard. At about 11:30 a.m. on 3 December 1924 she "sprang a serious leak"; Captain John McNeely headed for shallow water and she sank; the whole crew was saved. Sixteen insurance companies, facing $450,000 in claims, sent divers down — who found every seacock open. The Thompson Transportation Company was sued for deliberate scuttling; the case was tried twice and settled out of court on the third attempt, on terms favouring the owners. Divers rediscovered her in 1960 with the cars still in the hold.
  • The odd detail: The seacocks. A ship sunk on purpose, with the evidence still bolted to the bottom of Lake Michigan for anyone who could get down 205 ft.
  • People: Capt. John McNeely — master; Thompson Transportation Company — owner.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Lakeland
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1929, holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Secret Service's fraud men know the Lakeland case as the model for scuttling-for-insurance in the Depression; when the Material Service goes down in 1936 with no cause, that is the first thing they think of — and the wrong thing.

The Rosinco, the Chicago Yacht Club's flagship, hits "an underwater obstruction" twelve miles out — 19 September 1928 (living memory)

He died in 1968 with the work unfinished; Joe Grimm edited it as Windjammers: Songs of the Great Lakes Sailors (Wayne State University Press, 1 February 2002; Lee Murdock transcribed the music; Michigan Notable Book 2003).
  • The odd detail: The setting's own decade is the exact moment the last men who had sailed the lakes under canvas were dying in charity wards — and one professor was going bed to bed with a notebook.
  • People: Ivan H. Walton — U-M folklorist, d. 1968; Joe Grimm — editor.
  • Sources: https://www.wsupress.wayne.edu/books/detail/windjammers
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Walton is the period's ready-made investigator NPC: an academic with a notebook of what the old sailors would only say when dying. His field notes (real, at the Bentley Library) are the setting's grimoire of the lakes.

The keeper who hanged from a tree at Big Bay Point (legend from a real death) — 1901; haunted since

  • Where: Big Bay Point Light, Lake Superior, north-west of Marquette (built 1896, $25,000; automated 1941; deactivated 1961).
  • What happened: William Prior, the first keeper, lost his son Edward (his assistant) to a leg injury; in 1901 Prior disappeared and was found some eighteen months later hanging from a tree about a mile from the light. Legend since says suicide or murder, a red-haired ghost in the mirrors, doors banging at night. The light was manned through the 1930s, automated 1941, and is a bed-and-breakfast today. Folklore built on a documented death.
  • The odd detail: Eighteen months hanging a mile from the house before anyone found him — on a coast where men walked to the light every day.
  • People: William Prior — keeper; Edward Prior — his son.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bay_Point_Light
  • Confidence: High on the death; the haunting is legend.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The 1930s keeper at Big Bay knows the story and keeps a lamp lit in the boathouse; a Section 13 file on lighthouse suicides (Grosse Point's "Sheridan", Big Bay's Prior) is a pattern someone in Washington has already noticed.

Stannard Rock, "the loneliest place in the world" — the stag station 24 miles out — 1882 onward

  • Where: Stannard Rock reef, 24 miles off the Keweenaw, Lake Superior — the most distant lighthouse from shore in the United States.
  • What happened: Built 1877–83 for $305,000 (first lit 4 July 1882), Stannard Rock was a "stag station" — men only — with keepers landed in March to chip one to two feet of ice off the doors with sledgehammers and taken off in early December. Illness or fire could leave them alone for days or weeks; in the first three seasons four keepers resigned and three transferred. (In 1961 the gasoline and propane store exploded during automation, killing one keeper and stranding three on the pier for three days.)
  • The odd detail: The keepers' own name for the post — "the loneliest place in the world" — on a reef in the lake that keeps its dead.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stannard_Rock_Light
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened; keepers there all decade.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Four men, nine months, no women, no shore, on a rock in the lake — either the safest cell in America for a thing that must not be near people, or the place it was let out.

The Armistice Day storm — 11 November 1940 (one year past the window; note only)

  • Where: Lake Michigan, off Pentwater and Ludington, Michigan.
  • What happened: The blizzard of 11 November 1940 sank three freighters on Lake Michigan — William B. Davock, Anna C. Minch and the Canadian Novadoc — and two smaller boats; 66 sailors died on the lake (the fishing tug Three Brothers of Pentwater took the Novadoc's survivors off — standard accounts, NOT on the page read; UNVERIFIED). It closed the decade's lake history exactly one year after the setting's window.
  • The odd detail: The Witch of November, on the anniversary of the peace the setting never had.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armistice_Day_Blizzard
