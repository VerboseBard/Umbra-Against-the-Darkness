1. The clubs — the unmarked doors

The Casino Club — "the most absolutely, utterly excruciatingly exclusive club in Chicago" — 167 → 195 E. Delaware Place, 1914 / 1928

  • Where: Gold Coast; first clubhouse 167 E. Delaware Place (opened December 12, 1914), then 195 E. Delaware Place from 1928 (the low building that now sits in the shadow of the Hancock tower); one block from the Drake
  • What happened: Founded 1914 as a society dancing-and-dining club run by women: Mrs. Joseph G. Coleman president, Mrs. Howard Linn vice-president, Robert G. McGann secretary, Robert H. McCormick treasurer, with Mrs. John Alden Carpenter (Rue Winterbotham Carpenter, later of the Arts Club), Arthur Aldis, Harold A. Howard, Eames MacVeagh and Honoré Palmer among the governors ⟨fixed: V_R19⟩. The first house — pink-and-white stucco over tile, Directoire interiors, an 80-by-40-foot dance hall, card rooms, dressing rooms, kitchen — was designed by Ernest Walker, Harold Howard and Mrs. Carpenter herself and opened with 400 members. In 1916 members made a silent film, Cousin Jim, for charity. When the Palmer estate sold the ground in 1927–28 the building came down and the club moved to a new marbled, pastel Art Deco house at 195 E. Delaware. The annual December Ball — first Friday of December, 250 to dinner and 400 more for dancing — was the fixed point of the society year; during Prohibition the club was raided for serving liquor (Chicagology, from Tribune files; date of the raid not given). On repeal night 1933 the Casino was one of the society rooms that drank legally at midnight (see R16).
  • The odd detail: A club founded, designed and governed by women, with a bylaw that a non-member may enter only once a month and never unescorted — and a Prohibition raid on the most respectable address in the city.
  • People: Mrs. Joseph G. Coleman — first president; Rue Winterbotham Carpenter — founder, designer, later Arts Club president; Honoré Palmer — governor, Bertha's son; Michael Kilian — the Tribune writer who coined the "excruciatingly exclusive" line (May 5, 1993)
  • Sources: https://chicagology.com/skyscrapers/skyscrapers118/ (Tribune articles Dec 7 1913 – Dec 13 1914, Feb 13 1927, May 5 1993 reproduced) ; https://chicagology.com/notorious-chicago/prohibition/ (repeal night, read for R16)
  • Confidence: High on founding, addresses, officers and the December Ball; Medium on the Prohibition raid (mentioned without a date).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The vampires who "conspire in high society" need a room with a once-a-month guest rule and a door that opens only to a member: this is it. The December Ball on the first Friday of December is a fixed date a module can hang a scene on — and in the alpha's year, 1936, that Friday is December 4, three weeks before the eclipse.

The Union League Club — the Loop's clubhouse of loyalty, 65 W. Jackson Boulevard, 1879 / new house 1926

  • Where: 65 W. Jackson Boulevard, the Loop (the 1926 Mundie & Jensen building on the site of Jenney's original)
  • What happened: Founded December 19, 1879, out of the Civil War Union League of America, "to encourage loyalty to the Federal Government, defend the Union, inculcate good citizenship" and police elections; in its first year it had two presidents at once (James B. Bradwell and Lewis Larned Coburn). Members included Daniel Burnham, Marshall Field, Louis Sullivan, William Le Baron Jenney and Charles G. Dawes (a former president in the setting). The club was the engine behind the Chicago Crime Commission (c. 1919–20) and its Public Affairs Committee; the present 22-storey clubhouse — meeting rooms, guest bedrooms, five dining rooms, pool and gymnasium — was dedicated in 1926 with a history titled The Spirit of the Union League Club, 1879–1926. Its art collection was large enough for the Tribune to call it "the other art institute in Chicago."
  • The odd detail: A private club whose stated purpose was policing the Republic — election watchers and the Crime Commission's paymasters — with bedrooms upstairs and Monets on the walls.
  • People: Charles G. Dawes — member (a former U.S. president in the setting); James B. Bradwell and Lewis Larned Coburn — the two simultaneous first presidents
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_League_Club_of_Chicago
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly (presidents) — the club holds as recorded ⟨fixed: V_R19⟩ (its founding loyalty to "the Union" makes it the natural enemy of a Golden Circle; Dawes as an ex-president member is a setting colour).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): If the Golden Circle is the Confederacy's ghost, the Union League is the club that was founded to hunt it; Section 13's Chicago liaison eats lunch here. The Crime Commission's files on Capone were assembled in this building.

The Cliff Dwellers — Hamlin Garland's "kiva" over Orchestra Hall, 220 S. Michigan Avenue, 1907/1909

  • Where: The eighth floor and ninth-storey penthouse above Orchestra Hall, 220 S. Michigan Avenue (leased 1908; inaugural ceremonies January 6, 1909); earlier meetings in the Fine Arts Building
  • What happened: Founded 1907 as the Attic Club by the novelist Hamlin Garland (Pulitzer 1922 for A Daughter of the Middle Border) "to encourage, foster and develop higher standards of art, literature and craftsmanship"; renamed the Cliff Dwellers January 18, 1909 — after Henry Blake Fuller's novel of that name, though Fuller refused to join, or after the cliff-dwelling Indians of the Southwest, for a club "perching on high ledges." Its presidents in this decade were the Chicago Symphony's conductor Frederick Stock (1927–28), the ex-mayor Carter Harrison IV (1929) and the architect Thomas E. Tallmadge (1932–33); women were barred until 1984. Garland himself had moved to Hollywood in 1929 and spent the decade on the other thing he had pursued since 1891: psychical research, published as Forty Years of Psychic Research (1936) and The Mystery of the Buried Crosses (1939), the latter written to "prove the legitimacy of psychic mediums." He died March 4, 1940.
  • The odd detail: The founder of the city's arts club spent the 1930s digging for buried crosses on the instructions of a medium — while his club dined nine floors above Michigan Avenue with the Symphony's conductor in the chair.
  • People: Hamlin Garland — founder, novelist, psychic researcher; Frederick Stock — CSO conductor, club president 1927–28; Carter Harrison IV — five-time mayor, president 1929; Thomas E. Tallmadge — architect, president 1932–33; Henry Blake Fuller — namesake, refused membership
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliff_Dwellers_Club ; https://www.cliff-chicago.org/history ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlin_Garland
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A penthouse "kiva" of architects, writers and musicians whose founder believed the dead speak — the Cliff Dwellers is where a Hunter Guild recruiter finds the educated dilettante who has already seen something. Garland's buried crosses (a real 1930s hunt for relics located by a medium) are a ready-made lead for a Kabbalah or IHS module.

The Onwentsia Club — "a meeting place in the country of sporting braves and squaws," Lake Forest, 1895

  • Where: Green Bay Road, Lake Forest (Lake County); the North Shore's premier country club
  • What happened: Established 1895 (the first nine holes by Charles B. Macdonald that year, the second nine 1898 by H. J. Tweedie and the Foulis brothers); hosted the 1906 U.S. Open (won by Alex Smith) and hired Willie Anderson, by then a four-time U.S. Open champion (the only man to win three in a row), as its professional in 1906 ⟨fixed: V_R19⟩. Its name was explained by the club as an Indian phrase for "a meeting place — in the country — of sporting braves and squaws." Lake Forest's own Wikipedia history calls it, in one writer's words, "the premiere social and sporting club in the Midwest"; the Armours, Swifts, McCormicks and Ryersons whose estates ring it (see section 4) were its members. The South Shore Country Club — the lakefront palace that closed its door to Jews in the 1930s — 71st Street and South Shore Drive, 1905 / 1916

  • Where: 7059 S. South Shore Drive, South Shore (now the South Shore Cultural Center)
  • What happened: Founded 1905 as the South Side's suburban counterpart to the Loop clubs; Marshall & Fox's first Mediterranean Revival clubhouse (1906) was replaced by their grander one in 1916 — ballroom, theatre, dining rooms, stables and a riding ring, a nine-hole golf course, tennis, a bowling green, and a private beach on the lake. Built as a Protestant-only club, it later admitted Irish Catholics; Jews were kept out from the 1930s (in 1967 the club considered opening membership to Jews "for the first time since the 1930s" and African Americans "for the first time ever," and declined; members left as the neighbourhood integrated, the club liquidated in 1973 and was sold to the Park District in 1975 for $9.775 million) ⟨fixed: V_R19⟩. Garrard B. Winston of 1508 N. State Street was a member here as well as at the Saddle and Cycle. Its horse shows and its beach were the South Side's society summer.
  • The odd detail: The decade this file covers is the decade a Chicago club that had admitted Jews decided to stop — the period's own social categories, hardening as the money got scarce.
  • People: Marshall & Fox — architects (also of the Drake); Garrard B. Winston — banker-member
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Shore_Cultural_Center ; Wikipedia search snippet for Garrard B. Winston (https://en.wikipedia.org/w/api.php?action=query&list=search&srsearch=%22Saddle%20and%20Cycle%22%20Chicago&format=json)
  • Confidence: High on dates and rules; the exact street number is from the present Cultural Center address.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A door that shut in the 1930s is a decision someone made; a module can ask who on the board wanted the Jewish families out and why — the Kabbalahs would have noticed first.

The Chicago Athletic Association — the Doge's palace on Michigan Avenue and the Cubs' "C" — 12 S. Michigan Avenue, 1890 / 1893

  • Where: 12 S. Michigan Avenue, facing Grant Park, the Loop
  • What happened: Founded 1890; Henry Ives Cobb's Venetian Gothic clubhouse (façade by Louis Christian Mullgardt), modelled on the Doge's Palace, opened in 1893 for the fair year. Its football team played West Point under lights at the 1893 fair (won 14–0 in a 40-minute game) with Yale's Pudge Heffelfinger and Princeton's Knowlton Ames. William Wrigley Jr. was a longtime member; the club's red encircled "C" became the Cubs' emblem in 1917. A men's club until 1972 (wives could dine as guests), it closed in 2007 and is now a hotel.
  • The odd detail: The baseball logo half the city wears began as a private club's monogram — a small daily emblem of who owned what.
  • People: Henry Ives Cobb — architect (also of the Palmer castle); William Wrigley Jr. — member
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Athletic_Association
  • Confidence: High.

The Woman's Athletic Club — seven floors of marble for the society women, 626 N. Michigan Avenue, opened April 24, 1929

  • Where: 626 N. Michigan Avenue at Ontario Street, Near North Side (the north tower of the Magnificent Mile's early stretch)
  • What happened: Founded 1898 — "the first athletic club for women in the United States" — the club opened Philip B. Maher's seven-storey French-style building on April 24, 1929 (Tribune, April 23, 1929: "opens its doors tomorrow with reception for the membership"): a Georgian living room the width of the second floor, a swimming pool, "modernistic" dining rooms, a top-floor ballroom, a library with a Lely portrait, a card room, a "personal service" floor of massage, shampoo and manicure, and bedrooms so suburban members could stay in town. The design committee were Mrs. Hudson (chairman), Mrs. Brower, Mrs. William Sherman, Mrs. Frank P. Graves, Mrs. Nelson Pelouze and Mrs. M. Clement. It was thus six months old when the market broke.

The Three Arts Club — ninety-two rooms of art students on Dearborn, 1300 N. Dearborn Street, 1912 / 1915

  • Where: 1300 N. Dearborn Street at Goethe, Gold Coast
  • What happened: Founded 1912 on the model of New York's Three Arts Club as "a safe and congenial home" for young women studying music, painting and drama; Holabird & Roche's three-storey Mediterranean building (built 1914, opened 1915) had 92 residence rooms round a courtyard. Over its life more than 13,000 women lived there (residential use ended 2004). Its patrons were the Gold Coast's matrons; its residents were the girls from downstate who came to the Art Institute and the conservatories.

The Chicago Yacht Club and the 1937 Mackinac gale — Monroe and Belmont harbors, 1875 / July 1937

  • Where: Chicago Yacht Club stations at Monroe Harbor (foot of Monroe Street) and Belmont Harbor; the race runs 333 miles from the Chicago Harbor Lighthouse to Round Island Channel off Mackinac Island
  • What happened: The club dates from 1875; the first Race to Mackinac was 1898 (five boats), not held again until 1904, with a trophy bought in 1906, skipped 1917–20 and run every year since 1921 — "the oldest annual freshwater distance race in the world" ⟨fixed: V_R19⟩. Its worst weather came in "the blows of 1911, 1937 and 1970." In July 1937 the fleet met "the fiercest gale in the history of the event": the Chicago yacht Rubaiyat, skippered by Nathaniel Rubinkam, commodore of the Columbia Yacht Club, finished shortly after dawn on July 27 after 62 hours on a storm-swept lake, having also won in 1936; Bob Benedict's Southern Cross was second, 1 hour 50 minutes back [UNVERIFIED — V_R19]; the race organiser records winds up to 75 mph and "only 8 out of 42 boats finished," the Coast Guard rescuing the crew of Reverie after they were swept overboard (a 1937 wire report is quoted as 35 of 43 failing "because of heavy seas, broken masts and other damage") ⟨fixed: V_R19 — https://www.cycracetomackinac.com/history⟩.
  • The odd detail: In the summer of 1937, 34 of 42 rich men's yachts were driven out of the race by 75-mph winds, heavy seas and broken masts, and only eight reached the island ⟨fixed: V_R19⟩ — a whole society weekend wrecked on a freshwater sea.
  • People: Nathaniel Rubinkam — commodore, Columbia Yacht Club, owner of Rubaiyat; Bob Benedict — Southern Cross
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Yacht_Club ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Yacht_Club_Race_to_Mackinac ; https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84026749/1937-07-27/ed-1/?sp=24 (Washington Times, July 27, 1937)
  • Confidence: High.

2. The season — debutantes, the balls, opera nights (first entries; more below)

Ravinia closes — the North Shore's summer opera dies of the Depression — 1931 / revived 1936

  • Where: Ravinia Park, Highland Park (Lake County), on the Chicago & Milwaukee Electric line; 36 acres
  • What happened: Opened August 15, 1904, as an amusement park built by the A. C. Frost Company to sell rides on the electric interurban (a baseball diamond, an electric fountain, a refectory). In the 1920s the Chicago businessman Louis Eckstein turned it into "the summer opera capital of the world," staging full seasons with Chicago Opera and Metropolitan stars — Yvonne Gall, Edward Johnson, Giovanni Martinelli — as the North Shore estates' own summer season. The cost of the productions broke it: Ravinia closed in 1931 and stayed dark four summers, until North Shore residents raised the money and the Chicago Symphony made it its summer home in 1936.
  • The odd detail: For four summers of the decade the rich had no summer opera — the one fixed evening of the Lake Forest calendar simply stopped, and the park sat empty on the interurban line.
  • People: Louis Eckstein — publisher-patron who ran the opera seasons; Giovanni Martinelli — Met tenor who sang there
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravinia_Festival
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): An opera pavilion dark from 1931 to 1936 on the edge of the estates is a stage nobody is watching; what was staged there in the dark summers is a North Shore module's question.

Chicago's opera after Insull: two companies in four years, and a fifteen-year-old's debut — 1933–1939

  • Where: Civic Opera House, 20 N. Wacker Drive
  • What happened: Insull's Chicago Civic Opera folded in 1932 (see R08). The Chicago Grand Opera Company took the house for 1933–35 and went bankrupt; the Chicago City Opera Company was "organized from the remaining assets" and gave four seasons, 1935 to 1939, "before it too succumbed to financial difficulties," to be succeeded by the Chicago Opera Company (1940–46). On December 6, 1936 — the alpha's month — the MGM starlet Betty Jaynes made her debut with the City Opera at fifteen. Opening night at the opera remained society's biggest single evening of the year; the boxes were the Social Register in tiers.
  • The odd detail: The city's opera was reorganised out of bankruptcy twice in the decade; the boxholders stayed the same while the company's name changed under them.
  • People: Betty Jaynes — 15-year-old debutante of the stage, December 6, 1936
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_City_Opera_Company
  • Confidence: Medium-High (the Grand Opera Company's founders and backers not read this session — UNVERIFIED).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Opening night 1936 in the Throne is the one night every society vampire must be seen; a debut by a child prodigy on December 6 is a lure or a sacrifice, depending on who bought her contract.

Mrs. Insull's comeback: The School for Scandal for charity — June 1925 (living memory of the Insull years)

  • Where: Chicago (the charity revival; theatre not confirmed this session), then Broadway, October 1925
  • What happened: Samuel Insull's wife, the former Broadway actress Gladys Wallis (born Margaret Anna Bird; on stage with W. H. Crane's company from 1892; married Insull May 22, 1899, at 24 to his 41), returned to the stage in a Chicago charity revival of Sheridan's The School for Scandal in June 1925, then played the same part on Broadway that October. The revival — the utilities king's wife playing Lady Teazle for charity — was the sort of event society bought boxes for; Orson Welles's Citizen Kane borrowed the pattern of a magnate promoting his wife's stage career. She outlived the crash and her husband, dying September 23, 1953.
  • The odd detail: The richest man in Chicago's wife, in her fifties, playing an eighteenth-century flirt for charity while his holding companies were being built on paper.
  • People: Gladys Wallis (Mrs. Samuel Insull) — actress; Samuel Insull — husband, see R08
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Insull
  • Confidence: High on dates; the Chicago theatre UNVERIFIED (the Studebaker is remembered).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A charity revival is where society meets the stage; the cast list of June 1925 is a list of people who owed the Insulls a favour by 1932.

Brush (the arc light), Marcus Hanna, Amasa Stone and John Hay — and the swindler Cassie Chadwick, who claimed to be Carnegie's natural daughter — was in decline by the 1920s; "during the Great Depression, many mansions were converted into rooming houses," and by mid-century the street was commercial. Charles F. Schweinfurth had designed at least fifteen of its houses. Eight survive; the Mather and Howe mansions are now Cleveland State's.
  • The odd detail: In the 1930s a Cleveland transient could rent a room in the house Rockefeller's neighbours built — the ring's Prairie Avenue.

Gwinn and Bratenahl — Cleveland's lakefront enclave and the Mather house that copies the White House — 1902 / 1907–08 / 1929–1935

  • Where: Gwinn, 12407 Lake Shore Boulevard, Bratenahl, Ohio; the village of Bratenahl on Lake Erie east of Cleveland
  • What happened: Bratenahl was formed in 1902 (incorporated 1903) when Liberty E. Holden, Samuel Mather and Frederick Goff refused annexation with Glenville; the Cornings, Hannas, Boltons, Mathers, Goffs, Ingalls, Haskells, Grassellis, Coits and Cunninghams built along tree-lined Lake Shore Boulevard, having first summered there "in elaborately furnished tents." William G. Mather's Gwinn — a 16-room Italian Renaissance and neoclassical house built 1907–08 on six acres for $1 million, its north front a copy of the White House portico dropping 28 feet by terraced steps to the lake; Charles A. Platt (also of Villa Turicum) was its designer through 1931 — became, after Mather married the widow Elizabeth Ring Ireland in 1929, the ring's great party house: "Elizabeth made large impact on Gwinn by hosting fundraising events and fabulous parties," and in 1935 she had Ellen Biddle Shipman redesign the walled garden. The Mathers lived there until 1951 and 1957.
  • The odd detail: A copy of the White House on Lake Erie, whose second mistress arrived in 1929 and threw the Depression's best parties on the terrace.
  • People: William G. Mather — iron-ore magnate; Elizabeth Ring Ireland Mather — hostess; Charles A. Platt — architect; Samuel Mather — his half-brother, Bratenahl founder

Grosse Pointe's two palaces — Edsel Ford's Cotswold manor and Anna Dodge's $4 million Versailles — 1926–29 / 1931–34

  • Where: Edsel and Eleanor Ford House, 1100 Lake Shore Drive, Gaukler Pointe, Grosse Pointe Shores, on Lake St. Clair; Rose Terrace, Grosse Pointe Farms (demolished 1976)
  • What happened: Albert Kahn built the Fords' house from 1926 (the shell in a year; the family in by 1928; interiors to 1929) with Jens Jensen's long-meadow landscape: the Gallery panelled in sixteenth-century linenfold oak from demolished English manors, a staircase from Lyveden, a dining room from New Place at Upminster, a library from Deene Park; in 1930 Clara Ford gave her granddaughter Josephine a child-sized playhouse with working electricity and plumbing. Across the Pointes, Horace Dodge's widow Anna Thompson Dodge had Horace Trumbauer replace her 1912 Kahn house with a Louis XV château, 1931–34, at $4 million: 75 rooms, over 42,000 square feet, 15 fireplaces, 40 French doors, a 60-by-36-foot music room with the old house's organ, a bureau made for Catherine the Great and four of Marie Antoinette's chairs.
  • The odd detail: In the worst years of the Depression Detroit's two auto widows-and-heirs were importing whole English rooms and a French queen's chairs — one house Tudor, one Bourbon, a mile apart.
  • People: Edsel and Eleanor Ford; Anna Thompson Dodge — Horace Dodge's widow; Albert Kahn, Horace Trumbauer — architects; Jens Jensen — landscape
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edsel_and_Eleanor_Ford_House ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rose_Terrace_(Grosse_Pointe_Farms,_Michigan)

Detroit's clubs — the Athletic Club's palazzo and the Yacht Club that went broke — 241 Madison, 1915 / Grosse Pointe Shores, 1929

  • Where: Detroit Athletic Club, 241 Madison Street; Grosse Pointe Yacht Club, Grosse Pointe Shores on Lake St. Clair
  • What happened: On January 4, 1913, Henry Bourne Joy of Packard and 108 leading citizens re-founded the 1887 Detroit Athletic Club as the auto men's house, and Albert Kahn gave them a Roman palazzo (the Palazzo Borghese for the body, the Farnese's windows for the fourth-floor Grill Room) in 1915. The Grosse Pointe Yacht Club — an ice-boat club of 1910, a yacht club from 1918 — opened Guy Lowell's Mediterranean clubhouse with its Venetian campanile on July 4, 1929 (Lowell had died at sea before finishing the plans; the budget overran; the big dining room seated 250) — and the Depression foreclosed it "despite attempts from the club to solicit more members"; the members bought it back from creditors at the start of the war.
  • The odd detail: A yacht club opened four months before the crash and lost its own clubhouse; the auto barons kept their palazzo.
  • People: Henry B. Joy — Packard, DAC re-founder; Albert Kahn, Guy Lowell — architects
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit_Athletic_Club ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grosse_Pointe_Yacht_Club

Milwaukee's brewers at home — the Pabst mansion as the archbishop's house, Villa Terrace, and "Uihlein Hill" — 1908 / 1924 ⟨fixed: V_R19⟩ / 1930s (Pabst also in R07)

  • Where: Pabst Mansion, 2000 W. Wisconsin Avenue (archbishop's residence 1908–75); Villa Terrace, 2220 N. Terrace Avenue; the Schlitz brewery on Third Street and the Uihleins' Hillside neighbourhood; the 1920s estates at River Hills
  • What happened: Captain Frederick Pabst's Flemish Renaissance house (George Bowman Ferry, 1890–92, with a mahogany men's parlour and a white-enamel ladies' parlour) was sold to the Archdiocese in 1908; "for the next 67 years, five archbishops as well as many priests and sisters lived at the Pabst mansion," the conservatory rebuilt as the archbishop's chapel — so through the decade the beer king's house was the Catholic Church's. Schlitz in the dry years — the beer that made candy, and the family that came back first — Milwaukee, 1920–1933

  • Where: The Schlitz brewery on Third Street, Milwaukee; the short-lived chocolate-and-candy division on N. Port Washington Road (now Glendale)
  • What happened: Through Prohibition the Uihleins' Schlitz made "malt syrup, bakery products, and sodas, among other items," and for a while chocolate and candy at a separate plant; the family — William J. Uihlein had brought America's first pure yeast culture from Copenhagen in 1883; Joseph Uihlein Sr. ran a refrigerator-car line in the 1890s — kept the company through three generations to 1976. After repeal in April 1933 Schlitz "returned to its position as a national brewing leader," the largest brewer in America by 1947. The family's money later built the Performing Arts Center and the Schlitz Audubon nature reserve on land it had held since the 1880s.
  • The odd detail: The beer barons of the ring spent the dry decade selling malt syrup (the home-brewer's raw material, sold with a straight face) and chocolate — and were richer at repeal than before.
  • People: the Uihlein brothers and sons — Schlitz owners
  • Sources: https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/schlitz-brewing-company/
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (repeal December 1933 is in the setting's calendar).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Malt syrup "for baking" kept every speakeasy in the Midwest supplied; the ledgers of who bought it by the ton in Milwaukee are the Outfit's supply chain written down by respectable men.

Marshall Field Jr. shot — at home on Prairie Avenue, or at the Everleigh Club — November 22, 1905

  • Where: 1919 S. Prairie Avenue (the family's account) or the Everleigh Club, 2131–33 S. Dearborn (the rumour); see R16 for the club
  • What happened: On November 22, 1905, the heir to the Field fortune "suffered a gunshot wound to the abdomen" and "died of complications several days later." The newspapers reported an accident at his home while cleaning a gun; "there is some evidence that he was shot by a prostitute at the Everleigh Club," and a rival madam spread the story that the Everleigh sisters had murdered him.
