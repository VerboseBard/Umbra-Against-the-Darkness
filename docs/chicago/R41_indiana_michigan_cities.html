R41 — Indiana and Michigan beyond Chicago and Detroit, 1920–1940: the cities and their communities

  • Lane: R41 — Indianapolis, South Bend, Fort Wayne, Terre Haute, Muncie, Marion; Grand Rapids, Flint, Lansing, Saginaw/Bay City, Kalamazoo, Benton Harbor, the Upper Peninsula (and Battle Creek, Jackson and a few neighbours). The churches, crimes, disasters, disappearances and folklore of the ordinary cities, as the period press told it. Goes to the neighbourhoods; does not repeat R02 (Dillinger's Indiana raids, Brady Gang, Woolverton, Lamm), R06 (Marion lynching entry, Belle Gunness, Chesterton air crash), R07 (Flint sit-down, Italian Hall, Knute Rockne, Studebaker suicide, House of David, Bath School), R10 (Indiana Klan after Stephenson), R26 (Black Legion, Lansing Kerns fire, Hamtramck), R29 (Notre Dame vs. Klan, Valparaiso, Gary, Crown Point, East Chicago) except to add to them.
  • Date: 2026-09-30
  • Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (research agent)
  • Overview: Forty entries on the ordinary cities of Indiana and Michigan — the places between Chicago and Detroit where the 1930s was lived out at town scale. The Indiana half is dominated by what the Klan left behind (the convicted Klan mayor of Indianapolis who served his thirty days in 1931; Gennett Records pressing red-labelled KKK vanity discs in Richmond; George Dale of Muncie firing the entire police force on his first day as mayor; the Marion photograph and the 1931 anti-lynching law it forced), by labour at its limits (Terre Haute's two-day general strike and six months of martial law in 1935; Lansing's own labour holiday in June 1937 and the Michigan State students who met the flying squadrons with rebar), and by a strikingly Catholic-and-scientific strand (Fr. Nieuwland's thesis paragraph that became Lewisite; the Jesuits' 1934 seminary in a gambler's spa hotel under what had been the world's largest dome; Bishop Noll's national newspaper). The Michigan half adds the Battle Creek Sanitarium and its eugenics foundation, the Copper Country's collapse and the sealed Barnes-Hecker mine, Marquette's castle prison, the Klan's 1925 march through the Catholic side of Grand Rapids, and W. C. Durant running a Flint bowling alley. Divergence notes cluster in three places: the German-American places (Das Deutsche Haus in Indianapolis never renamed; Bay City's Salzburg), the "Red" vocabulary the period press reached for (Terre Haute's "Kerensky," East Lansing's "anti-communist" students) that a no-Soviet timeline cannot use, and the Great War's memorials and weapons (Grand Rapids's WWI tower, Lewisite's 1918 crash programme). Sourcing note: roughly half the entries rest on pages fetched and read in full this session (Indiana Magazine of History on Terre Haute; East Lansing Info and libcom on Lansing; Wikipedia pages fetched by URL; Hoosier State Chronicles' blog on Dale); entries resting only on search-result summaries are marked in Sources and Confidence. Several pages (Kalamazoo Public Library, Atlas Obscura, WMU, History Grand Rapids) returned 403 on fetch. Not covered (no usable source found in time): Saginaw (its Wikipedia history skips the 1930s), Flint's neighbourhoods beyond its two magnates, and South Bend's Hungarian community.

INDIANA

The mayor who served his thirty days late: John Duvall and the Indianapolis machine's fall — 1925–1934

  • Where: Indianapolis (City Hall; the Marion County jail; the Indianapolis Times newsroom)
  • What happened: John L. Duvall won the 1925 mayoral election with Klan backing "at the height of its political power" and took office 4 January 1926. After D. C. Stephenson's conviction (R10 has the Klan collapse), Stephenson, refused a pardon, released his "black boxes" in July 1927 — his former secretary's files, including 30-plus cancelled checks — and grand jury indictments followed on 9 September 1927. Duvall was found guilty on 23 September 1927 under the corrupt practices act (fine $1,000, 30 days in jail, a ban from public office). His gambit, on 26 September, was to appoint his own wife city controller so she would succeed him; on 27 October the council declared both posts vacant and named Claude E. Negley mayor pro tem. Duvall did not actually serve his 30 days until 4 February–5 March 1931. In the 1934 Republican mayoral primary he finished fourth with 170 votes. The case against Gov. Ed Jackson failed on the statute of limitations.
  • The odd detail: One of the cancelled checks — $2,500 to the governor — was explained as payment for a horse named "Senator," supposedly worth $300, which "subsequently choked to death on a corn cob" (Historic Indianapolis's telling). And the Klan's mayor served his sentence in the middle of the Depression, more than three years after leaving office.
  • People: John L. Duvall — Klan-backed mayor 1926–27; D. C. Stephenson — Grand Dragon, the source of the boxes; Ed Jackson — governor, indicted, case failed; George V. Coffin — Marion County Republican chairman, named on Stephenson's payroll list; William Remy — Marion County prosecutor who won the Stephenson conviction; Claude Negley — mayor pro tem, Oct 1927.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Duvall ; https://historicindianapolis.com/indianapolis-collected-d-c-stephensons-revenge/ ; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/murder-wasnt-very-pretty-the-rise-and-fall-of-dc-stephenson-18935042/
  • Confidence: High on dates (three pages agree); the horse story is one local-history writer's telling.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Indianapolis is a city whose ex-mayor is a convicted man serving late — a Golden Circle machine that was caught and is still in the building. The black boxes are a "who else is on the list" MacGuffin; a Secret Service agent in Indianapolis (R33 has the 1931 Hoover visit) can be told the boxes were never all accounted for.

The German House and the name that never changed — Das Deutsche Haus / the Athenaeum, 401 E. Michigan St — 1893–1918, and after

  • Where: Indianapolis, 401 E. Michigan Street (near downtown)
  • What happened: The Socialer Turnverein's stock company built Das Deutsche Haus in two phases, the east wing in 1893–94 and the west wing in 1897–98, to a design by Bernard Vonnegut of Vonnegut & Bohn — both partners American-born of German parents and trained in Germany. It held a gymnasium, locker rooms, a restaurant, an auditorium, a billiard room, bowling alleys, a ball-and-concert hall and a walled beer garden with a concert pavilion. The Normal College of the North American Gymnastic Union was housed in it from 1907 to 1970. On 22 February 1918, under wartime nativism, the club renamed itself "the Athenaeum." Clemens Vonnegut Sr. co-founded the city's first Turnverein in 1851; the family is still commemorated in the Rathskeller's "Vonnegut Room."
  • The odd detail: Legend says the building is haunted — by "Jolly Verner," a regular who "took a lethal fall in the Rathskeller"; ghostly children in the Rathskeller and a "Woman in Blue" on the stairwell looking for them; a Woman in White by the old YMCA rooms; and Dr. Helene Knabe in the Wilkie Room. Knabe was real: a German-born bacteriologist and physician (b. 1875, Medical College of Indiana 1904), found dead with a slashed throat in her bed on 24–25 October 1911 — police called it suicide, the coroner called it murder, women physicians funded a private detective, and over a year later two men were indicted: Dr. William B. Craig, the Indiana Veterinary College's dean of students (whom accusers said killed her to end a relationship), and the undertaker Alonzo Ragsdale, her estate's executor, accused of hiding and laundering her bloodstained kimono. The ghost stories are legend.
  • People: Bernard Vonnegut — architect; Clemens Vonnegut Sr. — Turnverein founder; Dr. Helene Knabe — physician, murdered 1911, ghost legend attached to the building; Dr. William B. Craig, Alonzo Ragsdale — indicted 1912.
  • Sources: https://indyencyclopedia.org/athenaeum/ ; https://hauntedus.com/indiana/the-athenaeum-haunted/ ; search summaries (not fetched): https://historicindianapolis.com/bacteria-blood-bad-dreams-the-unsolved-murder-of-helen-knabe/ , Wikipedia "Athenæum (Das Deutsche Haus)"
  • Confidence: High on the building history; ghosts are legend; Knabe details from a search summary.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in Umbra America never entered the Great War and Germany won it, so there is no 1918 anti-German wave and no renaming: the building is still Das Deutsche Haus in the 1930s, the Turners still drill in it, and the German-American community of Indianapolis is proud, not chastened (R23 has Germany's Midwest footprint).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A German house in a Klan-scarred capital, its Rathskeller a natural meeting place for Germany's "patron of other people's revolutions." The Knabe murder — a woman doctor killed in her bed, an undertaker accused of washing her kimono — is a cold case a 1930s Section 13 researcher could reopen (the 1911 acquittal outcome is not in the summaries I read).

Crispus Attucks High School: the school for Black students only, and the best faculty in the state — Indianapolis, 1927–1934

  • Where: Indianapolis, 1140 Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. St. (northwest of downtown, near Indiana Avenue)
  • What happened: In an era of open segregation, with white residents opposing integrated schools and some Black residents arguing for integration, the Indianapolis school board decided that all the city's Black high-school students would attend one new school. The board first chose the name Thomas Jefferson High School; community petitions forced a change to Crispus Attucks, the Black patriot killed at the Boston Massacre in 1770. The school opened 12 September 1927 with 42 faculty and 1,345 students (more than the 1,000–1,200 projected) and was dedicated 28 October 1927. By 1934, among 62 faculty, 17 held master's degrees and two held doctorates.
  • The odd detail: A school built as an instrument of segregation had by 1934 a faculty of unusually high qualifications — in a segregated city a Black PhD had nowhere else to teach — and its name was chosen by petition over the board's first choice.
  • People: Crispus Attucks — namesake (d. 5 March 1770); the Indianapolis school board — decided the segregated plan.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crispus_Attucks_High_School
  • Confidence: High on dates and numbers (one source); the Klan's role in the 1920s board is context from R10, not confirmed on this page.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A Black school with a doctorate-heavy faculty is a natural home for investigator-scholars no federal agency will recruit openly; its segregated origin is the "period's own categories" the design notes want used, not softened.

Indiana Avenue and the Madam Walker Theatre — Indianapolis, 1927 onward

  • Where: Indianapolis, Indiana Avenue (the Black business district northwest of downtown); the Madam Walker Theatre
  • What happened: Indiana Avenue was the commercial and cultural heart of Black Indianapolis: the Indianapolis Leader (the first Black-owned paper, from the 1870s), the Indianapolis Recorder, and clubs that from the 1920s to the 1960s fed jazz players including Freddie Hubbard, Wes Montgomery and J. J. Johnson. The Madam Walker Theatre, which "Madam Walker's daughter helped build," opened in 1927 and "quickly became known as the 'Crown Jewel of the Avenue.'" Crispus Attucks High School stood a few blocks away. The district declined when desegregation drew the Black middle class out in the late 1950s; the Walker Building faced closure by 1965 and IUPUI's campus later cleared much of the rest.
  • The odd detail: The theatre was named for a woman who made a fortune selling hair care to Black women and opened in the same year the Klan's mayor was on trial for corruption in the city's courts.
  • People: Madam C. J. Walker — hair-care entrepreneur, d. 1919; her daughter — whose company built the theatre (name not on the page I read); Wes Montgomery, Freddie Hubbard, J. J. Johnson — jazz names from the Avenue.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_Avenue
  • Confidence: Medium — one Wikipedia page.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (R14 has Bronzeville; R28 has women of the decade.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A second Black metropolis two hours from Chicago that a Bronzeville investigator can flee to, or a place where a Kabbalah or IHS contract worker can meet someone the Chicago machine cannot reach.

John Dillinger's funeral and the grave buried under iron — Mooresville / Crown Hill Cemetery, Indianapolis, 25 July 1934

  • Where: Mooresville, Morgan County; Crown Hill Cemetery, Indianapolis, Section 44, Lot 94
  • What happened: Dillinger died outside the Biograph in Chicago on 22 July 1934 (R02 has it). His sister Audrey identified the body, and he was buried at Crown Hill on 25 July 1934. The Crown Hill Foundation's page for the grave says the plot has had "no grass growing" because of the constant visitors. A widely repeated account (search summaries only; the Atlas Obscura page returned 403) says about 5,000 attended; souvenir-hunters took flowers and handfuls of dirt from the fresh grave; and Dillinger's father and brother had it opened and re-sealed under a cap of concrete mixed with scrap iron and chicken wire, topped by four stepped reinforced-concrete slabs, to prevent grave robbing. The headstone has since been replaced several times because visitors chip off pieces.
  • The odd detail: At the Chicago alley, spectators dipped handkerchiefs and newspaper scraps in his blood; at Crown Hill they took the dirt. Every crowd wanted a relic — and a man was sealed in iron and concrete in a cemetery that is also the last address of a president.
  • People: John Dillinger Sr. — father (per search summary); Audrey Dillinger — sister, identified body.
  • Sources: https://crownhillhf.org/notable/john-dillinger/ (read: date, Section 44 Lot 94, "no grass growing," Audrey's identification) ; search summaries: The Daily Beast, Atlas Obscura, Visit Indiana (concrete-and-iron details, crowd)
  • Confidence: High on date, section, lot; Medium on the concrete details and crowd size.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A grave sealed in iron against thieves is a hunter's scene to invert — what if a cult wanted the bones and the "souvenir hunters" were never after souvenirs? R02's "Was it Dillinger?" already has the doubt in the record.

Terre Haute stands still: the general strike of 22–23 July 1935 and six months of martial law

  • Where: Terre Haute, Vigo County, Indiana — the Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co. plant; downtown
  • What happened: A strike at Columbian Enameling & Stamping dragged through spring 1935. On 17 July the company brought in about 50 armed guards — most recruited around Chicago with help from the National Metal Trades Association — and the city gave them a police escort plus a carload of guns and ammunition; inside they were issued shotguns and submachine guns. On 19 July delegates of 48 unions voted a "labor holiday" to begin 1 a.m. Monday, 22 July, unless the guards were removed. Over 20,000 workers walked out; by midday the shutdown was called 90 percent effective and buses, streetcars, taxis and filling stations stopped. Sheriff William Baker insisted the crowd was good-humoured and he "had the situation perfectly in hand" until businessmen closeted in the Chamber of Commerce office pressured the mayor, sheriff and police chief into wiring Gov. Paul McNutt that they could not maintain order. McNutt declared martial law for all of Vigo County from 5 p.m. on 22 July; about 1,100 National Guardsmen were in the city by Tuesday, used tear gas and rifle butts three times, and made over 150 arrests (other tallies: 185). Martial law lasted until 10 February 1936 [UNVERIFIED — V_R41: the Bailey study says martial law ran "through at least February 1936"; the exact 10 February date was not confirmed] and became a bigger controversy than the strike.
  • The odd detail: The Chicago Daily Tribune called the strike "a touch of Russia, of Kerensky going down under the Reds." Meanwhile a threatened general strike in South Bend that June had been "narrowly averted," and Sullivan County to the south had been under martial law since 1933.
  • People: Paul V. McNutt — Democratic governor; William Baker — sheriff; Sam Beecher — mayor; Lewis Wheeler — police chief; Thomas Taylor — union speaker.
  • Sources: https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/imh/article/download/10560/14832/27077 (Gary L. Bailey, "The Terre Haute, Indiana, General Strike, 1935," Indiana Magazine of History — read in full) ; search summaries for the martial-law end date: https://theclio.com/entry/122466
  • Confidence: High on the narrative; the 10 Feb 1936 end date and the 185 arrest tally are from search summaries.
  • Divergence check: BENDS (lightly) — the domestic strike holds, but the Tribune's "Russia … Kerensky … Reds" cannot exist where there is no Soviet Union and no Red Scare; a 1935 editorial would reach for "Berlin's agents" or "foreign agitators" instead.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Armed men imported from Chicago, a governor who defines radicalism broadly, a city under the military for six months — a cover for anything the civil courts cannot ask about. "Martial law in Vigo County" is an FBI Section 13 jurisdictional headache.

Sin City on the Wabash: Terre Haute's vote-fraud mayor and red-light district — 1913–1930s (living memory)

  • Where: Terre Haute, Indiana — the red-light district between Second and Mulberry Streets; Fourth Street; the gambling houses on Wabash Avenue
  • What happened: In November 1913 Terre Haute's mayor Donn M. Roberts ran a vote-fraud machine: 132-plus people were indicted and 100-plus convicted; the methods included a $6,000 slush fund from saloons and gambling houses, 2,500 false registration cards, "floaters" paid $5–$8 a vote, and one voter who cast 22 ballots. Roberts was arrested 17 January 1914 (bond $8,000), sentenced to six years at Fort Leavenworth and fined $2,000; Circuit Judge Eli Redman got five years and died in Leavenworth; Sheriff Dennis Shea got five years. A 1916 report counted 900 prostitutes and 400 madams, and four gambling houses on Wabash Avenue open from 9 a.m. to midnight. Roberts lost a comeback mayoral race in 1921 by 552 votes. Prohibition killed the breweries and fed the bootleggers. A city that had been a byword for open vice within living memory was the one that struck in 1935.
  • The odd detail: A convicted mayor nearly returned to office eight years after Leavenworth, and the brothels were counted to the head — 900 women, 400 madams.
  • People: Donn M. Roberts — mayor convicted of election fraud; Judge A. B. Anderson — federal judge who called Roberts "arch conspirator"; Eli H. Redman — circuit judge, five years; Dennis Shea — sheriff, five years.
  • Sources: https://brisray.com/th/sincity1.htm
  • Confidence: Medium — one local-history site; the federal record not checked.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened (the 1913–14 fraud); the 1916 census and Prohibition years — holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The "arch conspirator" city is where a cult, or a Golden Circle cell, could trade in votes and vice with almost no shame; the 1935 strike is then the Chamber of Commerce's fear of losing the last thing it did not own.

The photograph: Lawrence Beitler's postcard and the song it became — Marion, Indiana, 7 August 1930 → 1939

  • Where: Marion, Grant County, Indiana — the courthouse square, the Grant County jail; a photographer's studio
  • What happened: On 7 August 1930 a mob of thousands (5,000 in one source; 10,000–15,000 in others) broke into the jail and lynched Thomas Shipp and Abram Smith, accused of killing Claude Deeter and assaulting Mary Ball; James Cameron, 16, was spared (R06 has the event). The local studio photographer Lawrence Beitler photographed the bodies and the crowd and "printed thousands of copies, which sold for fifty cents apiece" over the next ten days. In 1937 the schoolteacher Abel Meeropol saw the print and, "haunted for days," wrote the poem "Bitter Fruit," which became "Strange Fruit"; Billie Holiday recorded it in 1939 and it reached No. 16 on the charts in July.
  • The odd detail: The picture was a local product sold like a studio portrait; there is no record on the page I read of anyone pressing Beitler to stop selling it. A schoolteacher in New York made a song of it seven years later.
  • People: Lawrence Beitler — Marion photographer; Thomas Shipp, Abram Smith — lynched; James Cameron — survived; Abel Meeropol — poet of "Bitter Fruit"; Billie Holiday — recorded "Strange Fruit," 1939.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Beitler ; https://blog.history.in.gov/from-strange-fruit-to-seeds-of-change-the-aftermath-of-the-marion-lynching/ ; search summary: https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/marion-indiana-lynching-1930/
  • Confidence: High on the photograph and song chain; crowd size varies by source.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The period's categories at their ugliest. If used, the horror should belong to the crowd, not to a supernatural excuse; the unidentified woman's voice (R06) is the one uncanny detail the record itself gives.

After Marion: the 1931 anti-lynching law, the flight to Weaver, and Cameron's twenty-one years — 1930–1935

  • Where: Marion and Anderson, Indiana; Weaver (a historic Black settlement in Grant County); the Indiana Statehouse, Indianapolis
  • What happened: Katherine "Flossie" Bailey, Marion's NAACP leader, organised statewide meetings in 1931 and urged Black Hoosiers to write their legislators. House Democrats introduced an anti-lynching bill in February 1931 and Gov. Harry Leslie signed it in March: sheriffs whose prisoners were lynched could be dismissed, and victims' families could sue for damages. Neither the Marion sheriff nor any mob member was sentenced for the murders of Shipp and Smith. James Cameron was tried at Anderson, sentenced in 1931 to twenty-one years as accessory before the fact of voluntary manslaughter, released in 1935, and pardoned by Gov. Evan Bayh in 1993; he moved to Milwaukee in 1950, founded America's Black Holocaust Museum in 1988 and died in 2006. About 200 Black residents fled Marion for Weaver after the lynching. Cameron's stepfather, Hezekiah Burden, drunk in October 1930, shot at his wife and stepdaughter and then at police and was sentenced to one to ten years.
  • The odd detail: The Indiana Historical Bureau's blog notes that Mary Ball's own relatives later said Shipp and Smith were not the actual perpetrators. The remedy the 1931 law gave was a lawsuit against the county.
  • People: Flossie Bailey — NAACP Marion; Harry G. Leslie — governor, signed the law; James Cameron — survivor; Hezekiah Burden — Cameron's stepfather; Evan Bayh — 1993 pardon.
  • Sources: https://blog.history.in.gov/from-strange-fruit-to-seeds-of-change-the-aftermath-of-the-marion-lynching/
  • Confidence: High on the law and the sentences; "not the actual perpetrators" is the Bureau's paraphrase of relatives' statements, not adjudicated.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Weaver — a small Black settlement that took in 200 refugees — is a real hidden place: a hamlet where a fugitive from the Klan or the Golden Circle could live unrecorded.

"Middletown": the Lynds' Muncie, the X family, and the town that stayed the same — 1924–25, 1929, 1935, 1937

  • Where: Muncie, Delaware County, Indiana (the study's "Middletown")
  • What happened: Robert and Helen Lynd, commissioned by the Institute of Social and Religious Research, lived in Muncie for about fifteen months (sources differ: late 1923 or 1924 start) and published Middletown: A Study in Contemporary American Culture in 1929, comparing 1890 with 1924 under six headings: getting a living, making a home, training the young, using leisure, religious practice, community activities. It treated the middle class as a tribe in the anthropological sense. It also mapped power: the Ball family — "the X family" — directly employed about 10 percent of the town's workers and supported many of its leading institutions, including Ball Memorial Hospital (opened 1929), the teachers' college later renamed Ball State, and the YMCA and YWCA. Robert Lynd returned in 1935 and published Middletown in Transition in 1937, concluding that "basically the texture of Middletown's culture has not changed" despite the Depression; the business class held power and workers "lacked power and seldom openly gave voice to their grievances." The only person named in both books is the anti-Klan editor George Dale (next entry). The Ball brothers — Lucius, William, Edmund, Frank and George — moved from Buffalo to Muncie in 1887–88 for its natural gas; Frank was company president for 55 years.
  • The odd detail: The famous "ordinary American town" was studied under a pseudonym every citizen could see through, and the family running a tenth of its jobs was called "X."
  • People: Robert S. and Helen M. Lynd — sociologists; the Ball brothers — glass manufacturers; George R. Dale — the one real name in both books.
  • Sources: https://www.encyclopedia.com/economics/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/middletown-transition ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_brothers ; search summaries: Britannica, BSU Middletown libguide
  • Confidence: High on the two books' dates and headline findings; fieldwork length varies by source.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. The 1890/1924 comparison includes the Great War; in Umbra Muncie's men never went to France, so there are no 1919 Legion posts or war memorials of that kind [proposal].
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Middletown" is a model for cult versus population: an ordinary population, one family controlling a tenth of its jobs. The Lynds' six headings work as an investigation checklist for any small-city mission.

George Dale's "Koo Koo Side Lights": the editor who beat the Muncie Klan — 1922–1936

  • Where: Muncie, Indiana (the Muncie Post-Democrat; City Hall; the Muncie jail)
  • What happened: George R. Dale (1867–1936) edited the Muncie Post-Democrat from about 1920. His satirical column "Koo Koo Side Lights" mocked the Klan: from 10 November 1922 he lampooned Helen Jackson, a fraudulent "escaped nun" lecturer, and from December 1923 he called Daisy Douglass Barr, the Quaker minister who headed the women's Klan auxiliary, the "Quakeress Fakeress." He printed Klan membership names from stolen lists; hooded men shot at him and attacked his house; he was jailed repeatedly (the story was that inmates applauded when he came back) [UNVERIFIED — V_R41: not in the cited Hoosier State Chronicles article, which mentions only "Klansmen shooting at him and attacking his home"]. He won the 1929 mayoral election and, taking office, immediately fired all 42 police officers. A 1932 Prohibition indictment followed and President Roosevelt pardoned him on Christmas Eve 1933. He died at home on 27 March 1936.
  • The odd detail: An anti-Klan editor became mayor and fired the whole police force on his first day; the Prohibition men indicted him; the President pardoned him at Christmas.
  • People: George R. Dale — editor, mayor of Muncie (1930–35 per a search summary); Daisy Douglass Barr — Klan women's leader; Helen Jackson — "escaped nun" performer.
  • Sources: https://blog.newspapers.library.in.gov/koo-koo-side-lights-george-dale-vs-the-klan/ (Hoosier State Chronicles — the Post-Democrat is digitised there) ; search summary: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_R._Dale
  • Confidence: High on the Hoosier State Chronicles dates; the mayoral term and the jailing story are from search summaries.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar; the pardon by President Roosevelt in December 1933 fits (FDR is president from 1933).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A searchable real newspaper archive full of the Klan's stolen membership rolls — a photocopied 1925 roster is a fine Golden Circle clue. A mayor who fired 42 cops in a day knows whom the cops answered to.

The Jesuits and the once-biggest dome in the world: West Baden Springs — Orange County, 1902 → 1934

  • Where: West Baden Springs, Orange County, southern Indiana (beside French Lick)
  • What happened: Lee Sinclair rebuilt the West Baden hotel after a 14 June 1901 fire as a circular fireproof structure under a 200-foot dome — the world's largest dome 1902–1913 and America's largest free-spanning dome until 1955; 500 men built it in 270 days for $414,000 and it opened 15 September 1902. Edward Ballard, who "operated an illegal gambling business" and owned the Hagenbeck-Wallace Circus (whose train wrecked at Hammond in 1918; R29), bought it from Lillian Rexford for $1 million in 1923 (half of it repaying his earlier debt to him). The Sanitarium: Battle Creek, the Towers, and receivership — 1928–1942

Barnes-Hecker: the mine that drank a lake — Ishpeming, 3 November 1926 (living memory)

  • Where: Barnes-Hecker Mine, about 4 miles west of Ishpeming, Marquette Range, Michigan (Cleveland-Cliffs)
  • What happened: On 3 November 1926, after routine blasting, a stope cave-in broke through to the surface, opening a pit 300 feet long and 200 feet wide that drained North Lake into the mine; the workings, under a 200-foot water table layer of "quicksand" on three levels at 600, 800 and 1,000 feet, filled in about 15 minutes. Fifty-one men died — the worst mining accident in Michigan's history; one man, Rutherford "Wilfred" Wills, led colleagues up an emergency ladder to the surface in 14 minutes, and three other miners had left just before. County mine inspector William E. Hill, recently re-elected, died on his inspection visit. Only ten bodies were recovered; Cleveland-Cliffs abandoned and sealed the mine on 20 November 1926 with 41 bodies entombed and paid double workers' compensation. It left 42 widows and 132 minor children. A memorial stone was set near Negaunee in 1971. Ishpeming's population was 9,238 in 1930 (down 12% from 10,500 in 1920), and the town claims to be the birthplace of organised skiing in America (the National Ski Association was founded there 21 February 1905).
  • The odd detail: The tomb sits under a 300-by-200-foot pit that a lake poured through, and the county mine inspector, just re-elected, was among the dead.
  • People: Rutherford "Wilfred" Wills — the escapee who led two colleagues up the ladder; William E. Hill — county mine inspector, killed.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnes-Hecker_Mine_Disaster ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishpeming,_Michigan ; search summary: https://www.usdeadlyevents.com/1926-nov-3-barnes-hecker-iron-mine-cave-in-and-flooding-ishpeming-michigan-51/
