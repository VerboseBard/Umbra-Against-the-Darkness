Overview

51 entries. Milwaukee in the 1930s was a city of about 580,000 governed for 24 years by a Socialist mayor whose party could fit in one Turner hall, and it was — before April 1917 — the most German city in America: a German opera house, a German press with its own eight-storey building and bronze Germania, German parish schools, eight Turner chapters keeping their minutes in German, a Lutheran seminary and a Catholic archdiocese run by a Swiss. In our history the war dismantled that in eighteen months (the statue taken down at night, the Journal's Pulitzer "for Americanism," German gone from the schools by 1920, Berger sentenced to twenty years, the Leader cut off from the mails). In the setting none of that happens, and the entries below say what survives: a bilingual city whose German institutions are still whole in 1936, whose Socialist congressman sat undisturbed through the 1920s, and whose 1930s "Bund" quarrel becomes a much older fight between the 1848ers' heirs and the Kaiser's men. Around that spine the file collects the decade's own record — the 1917 police-station bomb (living memory, unsolved), the 1934 streetcar strike's man electrocuted in the power house, the 1935 teenage bombers, 54 strikes and 29 sit-downs in 1937, Beer Night, the cathedral fire, the Auditorium's Christmas Eve fire [UNVERIFIED — V_R27], the Kashubian village the city evicted from Jones Island, the Spiritualist college at Whitewater graduating its last class in May 1939, Capone's would-be killer from Milwaukee, and — the find of the run — the Milwaukee Fictioneers, where Robert Bloch corresponded with Lovecraft and Ray Palmer learned his trade. Sources are the Encyclopedia of Milwaukee (emke.uwm.edu) and Wikipedia, every page opened this session; Chronicling America rate-limited every attempt (see Leads).

1. City Hall — the Socialists

Victor Berger — the twenty-year sentence that never happens, and the streetcar that still kills him — 1918–1929

  • Where: Milwaukee; the Leader office; the House of Representatives; the corner of North Third Street and West Clarke Street (the accident); City Hall (the lying-in-state); Forest Home Cemetery.
  • What happened: Berger — born 28 Feb 1860 at Nieder-Rehbach in the Austrian Empire (now Romania), Jewish, in Milwaukee from 1881, first a German teacher in the public schools, then editor of the Wisconsin Vorwärts (1892) and founder of the Milwaukee Leader (7 Dec 1911) — was the first Socialist ever elected to Congress (1910). In our history he was indicted under the Espionage Act in February 1918 for the Leader's anti-war editorials, won his House seat anyway that November, was tried before Judge Kenesaw Mountain Landis from 9 Dec 1918, convicted on 20 Feb 1919 and sentenced to twenty years; the House refused to seat him on 10 Nov 1919, Milwaukee re-elected him in a special election on 19 Dec 1919, and the House refused him again on 10 Jan 1920. The Supreme Court threw the conviction out on 31 Jan 1921 (Berger v. United States — Landis should have stepped aside). He sat again 1923–29, lost to William Stafford in 1928, and chaired the Socialist Party nationally from 20 Oct 1926 (succeeding Debs). On 16 July 1929 a streetcar struck him at Third and Clarke; he died of the skull fracture on 7 Aug 1929. Some 75,000 people filed past his body in City Hall.
  • The odd detail: His widow Meta Schlichting Berger (married 1897) sat on the Milwaukee School Board 1909–39 and the University of Wisconsin Board of Regents 1928–34 — the Socialist widow governing the schools of the German city for the whole decade.
  • People: Victor L. Berger — editor, congressman, party chairman; Meta Berger — widow, school board 1909–39; Kenesaw Mountain Landis — the trial judge (later baseball's commissioner); William H. Stafford — the Republican who traded the seat with him.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_L._Berger ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/victor-l-berger/
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — hard. No American war means no Espionage Act prosecution, no Landis trial, no exclusion from the House: Berger plausibly sits in Congress continuously from 1919 (he won in Nov 1918 and again in Dec 1919 in our history) and the Leader is never strangled. The streetcar on Third Street and the 75,000 mourners hold — domestic calendar. In the setting his 1929 funeral is that of the most powerful Socialist in America, in a country with no Red Scare to make the word dangerous.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A congressman killed by a streetcar on the street named for the brewery hill, three months before the crash. The Kabbalahs would notice that Milwaukee's leading Jewish figure died on a corner; Section 13 would want to know whether the motorman had been in the Bund's predecessor.

The Milwaukee Leader — the socialist daily the Post Office never got to strangle — 1911–1942

  • Where: Milwaukee (office address not given in the pages read).
  • What happened: The Leader was launched on 7 Dec 1911 by the Social Democratic Publishing Company, funded chiefly by the wealthy radical Elizabeth H. Thomas, with Berger as editor-in-chief and John M. Work, Ernest Untermann and James R. "Jim" Howe writing editorials. It printed 31,874 copies a day in its first month and 50,000 by April 1916; Berger claimed three-quarters of its readers were not Socialists. On 22 Sept (EMKE: October) 1917 Postmaster General Albert Burleson revoked its second-class mailing privilege, "eliminating 5 out of 6 mail subscribers in one blow"; in August 1918 even first-class mail to the office was stamped "Mail to This Address Undeliverable Under Espionage Act"; the Justice Department raided it twice and seized the subscriber lists. Privileges came back only in June 1921. Circulation ran 48,200–49,100 through 1922–29, 38,300 in 1930 and 21,200 in 1940; Berger died leaving $130,000 of debt on it. Sold on 20 April 1938 to three ex-Sentinel men, it became the New Milwaukee Leader, then (Jan 1939) the Milwaukee Evening Post, then the Federated Trades Council's Milwaukee Post (Sept 1940); it died on 23 May 1942.
  • The odd detail: One of only three English-language socialist dailies in the United States, and it lived in the most German city in the country.
  • People: Victor Berger — editor; Elizabeth H. Thomas — the money; Albert S. Burleson — the Postmaster General who cut it off; John M. Work, Ernest Untermann — editorial writers.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milwaukee_Leader ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/milwaukee-leader/
  • Confidence: High (the two sources give the revocation month as September and October 1917 respectively).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — no war, no Burleson revocation, no raids, no seized lists; the paper keeps its 50,000 and probably grows through the 1920s. What holds: the Depression's erosion and the 1938 sale, which was financial, not political.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The one paper in the ring that prints what the Journal will not — a Section 13 agent's back channel, and the place a Golden Circle leak would be planted to discredit the Socialists.

Daniel Hoan's Milwaukee — baby bonds, a debt-free city and the Time cover — 1920–1940 (deepens R07)

  • Where: Milwaukee City Hall, 200 E. Wells St.
  • What happened: R07 has the outline (1916–40, Garden Homes, the parks). The texture: Hoan (born 12 March 1881 in Waukesha; a failed Chicago restaurant before law school; city attorney 1910–16, elected with a 7,300-vote plurality of ~59,000) made Milwaukee only the twelfth U.S. city with comprehensive zoning (1920), bought food by the carload and sold it at city markets from 1917, ran a debt-amortization fund that made the city debt-free, and, in danger of missing the city payroll in January 1933, issued "baby bonds" — small-denomination bonds redeemable in four years with interest (EMKE does not say wages were paid in them) ⟨fixed: V_R27⟩. In 1932 he had his only Common Council majority (12 of 27 aldermen Socialist, plus two non-Socialist Polish aldermen who sided with him) ⟨fixed: V_R27⟩ and won with more than 60%; in 1936 he ran as the Farmer-Labor Progressive Federation candidate and got 54% [UNVERIFIED — V_R27]. He was president of the U.S. Conference of Mayors in 1934–35, ran for national party chairman in 1932 (lost 105–86 to Morris Hillquit), and on 6 April 1936 was on the cover of Time, which called Milwaukee "a paragon of cities." His 1936 book City Government argued a civilization "should be judged by the amount of service that the people in a community demand and can afford to pay for, and not by how low a tax rate they may have." The party behind him had about 3,000 dues-payers in a city of nearly 580,000 (1936). He lost in 1940 to the 32-year-old Carl Zeidler — the last sitting Milwaukee mayor ever defeated — and quit the party in 1941.
  • The odd detail: The Socialist mayor's scrip: in the winter of 1933 a Milwaukee policeman's pay was a city bond. [UNVERIFIED — V_R27]
  • People: Daniel W. Hoan — mayor; Morris Hillquit — the New York Socialist who beat him for the chairmanship; Carl Zeidler — the singing young lawyer who beat him in 1940.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Hoan ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/daniel-webster-hoan/ ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/socialists/
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS in one place — his April 1917 break with the party over the war (the Milwaukee County Council of Defense, 30 April 1917) never happens, so he never has to prove his Americanism and the party never forgives him for it either; his 1936 run under a Farmer-Labor-Progressive label still fits. Baby bonds, the Time cover and the 1940 defeat hold — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The cleanest city hall in America, run for 24 years by a man whose whole party fits in a Turner hall — cult versus population inverted: the cult is the honest one. Section 13's Milwaukee file on Hoan is thin, and that worries them more than a thick one would.

"Sewer socialism" — Hillquit's sneer, 1932

  • Where: The Socialist Party's national convention (1932); Milwaukee.
  • What happened: The phrase the world uses for Milwaukee's socialism was an insult. When Hoan challenged Morris Hillquit for the national chairmanship in 1932 (and lost 105–86), Hillquit derided the Milwaukeeans as "practical" Socialists: "I do not belong to the Daniel Hoan group to whom Socialism consists of merely providing clean sewers of Milwaukee." Milwaukee took the name and kept it. Emil Seidel, the first Socialist mayor (1910–12), had raised the municipal minimum wage from $1.75 to $2 a day and given city workers the eight-hour day; the party had won 9 of 46 council seats as early as 1904 and never had more than about 5,000 members before the war.
  • The odd detail: The Milwaukee sewer system Hillquit sneered at is the one that sells its sludge as lawn fertiliser (R20, Milorganite).
  • People: Morris Hillquit — New York Socialist lawyer; Emil Seidel — mayor 1910–12, Debs's running mate 1912.
  • Sources: https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/socialists/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Hoan
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the 1932 convention fight was about reform versus revolution, and in a world with no Soviet Union the revolutionary wing is weaker still, so Hoan may even win).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Clean sewers" as the period's own reading; underneath, a city that knows exactly what goes into its drains and dries it into pellets.

4. Bombs, strikes and the mad bombers

The Central Police Station bomb — 7:33 p.m., Saturday 24 November 1917 (living memory)

  • Where: Central Police Station, Oneida (now Wells) and Broadway, downtown Milwaukee; the Italian Evangelical Church (Rev. Augusto Giuliani), Third Ward.
  • What happened: On 9 Sept 1917 Rev. Giuliani, an Italian Protestant evangelist, held a "loyalty" rally in Bay View; anarchists disrupted it, police fired, two men were killed and eleven Italian anarchists were arrested and charged with conspiracy to murder Giuliani; federal agents raided three IWW offices at the same time. Shortly before their trial, a twenty-pound black-powder bomb with an acid-delay detonator [UNVERIFIED — V_R27] was found beside Giuliani's church by a social worker, Maude L. Richter; the church janitor, Sam Mazzone, carried it to the Central Station, where it went off at 7:33 p.m. as detectives examined it. Nine policemen died — Henry Deckert, Frank Caswin, Frederick Kaiser, David O'Brien, Stephen Stecker, Charles Seehawer, Edward Spindler, Al Templin, Paul Weiler — and one civilian, Catherine Walker, who had come in to report a robbery; six more were hurt. The bomb was never solved (Galleanists were suspected; later interviews pointed to Mario Buda and Carlo Valdinoci, who had gone to Mexico to dodge the draft). In December 1917 the eleven were each given 25 years; Emma Goldman published The Milwaukee Frame-Up; Clarence Darrow won their release on appeal before the Wisconsin Supreme Court in 1918, and they were deported. Two further plots failed, including a bomb left at prosecutor Winfred Zabel's house. It remained the deadliest single day for American police until 11 Sept 2001.
  • The odd detail: The bomb was carried across downtown in a janitor's arms and sat on a station-house desk while nine men leaned over it.
  • People: Rev. Augusto Giuliani — the Italian Protestant the anarchists hated; Maude L. Richter — social worker who found it; Sam Mazzone — janitor who carried it; Lt. Robert Flood — shift commander; Winfred Zabel — district attorney; Clarence Darrow — the appeal; Mario Buda — suspected bombmaker.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milwaukee_Police_Department_bombing ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/anarchism/ ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/milwaukee-police-department/
  • Confidence: High (EMKE's "Wartime Milwaukee" entry dates only the Bay View riot, to September 1917, and gives the bomb no date; EMKE's police-department entry says "a youngster" carried the bomb, against Wikipedia's janitor ⟨fixed: V_R27⟩; the Bay View shooting was 9 Sept, the bomb 24 Nov).
  • Divergence check: BENDS at the edges — the "loyalty" rally and the draft-dodging in Mexico belong to an America at war. In the setting the Italian church-versus-anarchist feud is untouched, but Buda and Valdinoci fleeing "to Mexico" would be fleeing into American-occupied territory (the Southern War), and there is no draft to flee. The bomb, the nine names and the frame-up hold as a plainly domestic horror.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Nine policemen dead in one room in 1917 is the founding wound of the Milwaukee force that polices the 1930s; the widows' pensions, the survivors' promotions (Kluchesky, Laubenheimer) and the unsolved question of who built the acid-delay fuse [UNVERIFIED — V_R27] are a ready-made haunting of the Central Station.

The streetcar strike — the night of 28 June 1934, and the man electrocuted in the power house

  • Where: Milwaukee Electric Railway & Light Company (TMER&L) lines across the county; the Lakeside power plant (St. Francis, on the lake south of the city).
  • What happened: TMER&L had broken a streetcar strike in 1896 with hundreds of strikebreakers and kept the union out for 38 years. In June 1934 the conductors and mechanics struck for recognition; on the night of 28 June [UNVERIFIED — V_R27] the company ran cars with strikebreakers, a crowd of about 12,000 gathered, company men turned fire hoses on the demonstrators, who threw bricks and stones, and the police broke up the crowd with tear gas ⟨fixed: V_R27⟩ — 16 injured, 58 arrested. Over the following nights one man died: a striker electrocuted inside the company's Lakeside power plant. A compromise settlement came on 30 June 1934 and the union was recognised. Union membership in Milwaukee County, 20,000 in 1932 (no more than in 1913), passed 50,000 by the end of 1934.
  • The odd detail: The death was not on the picket line but inside the generating station, in the dark among the switchgear.
  • People: (no names given in the pages read — the electrocuted striker's name is a lead.)
  • Sources: https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/great-depression/ ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/strikes/ ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/workers-movements/
  • Confidence: High on dates and numbers; the dead man's name and what he was doing in the plant are UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the NIRA of 1933 that set off the organising wave is New Deal, which the setting keeps).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A man dies at the heart of the city's electricity on the second night of a strike, and the lights never flicker. What was he sent to do in there — and by whom?

The mad bombers of 1935 — Idzi Rutkowski and "Shrimp" Chovanec — 26 October to 3 November 1935

  • Where: Shorewood Village Hall (north of the city); two First Wisconsin National Bank branches; two police precinct stations; Rutkowski's family garage on the South Side.
  • What happened: In nine autumn days two South Side teenagers terrorised the city. On 26 Oct 1935 Isador "Idzi" Rutkowski, 19, and Paul "Shrimp" Chovanec (EMKE spells it Chovonec), 16, set off a home-made bomb at the Shorewood Village Hall (damage, no dead); on 28 Oct they bombed two branches of the First Wisconsin National Bank; on 31 Oct — Halloween — two police precinct stations. On 3 November 1935 the garage behind Idzi's South Side home blew up while they were building the next, biggest charge, killing both boys and a nine-year-old neighbour, Patricia Malnarek. R06 flagged this as unreachable; the Encyclopedia of Milwaukee's Depression entry confirms the sequence.
  • The odd detail: Halloween night's targets were the police stations — and the boys died three days later building a bomb in the family garage with a child playing next door.
  • People: Isador "Idzi" Rutkowski — 19 (the "Hugh" of some retellings is UNVERIFIED); Paul "Shrimp" Chovanec — 16; Patricia Malnarek — 9, killed.
  • Sources: https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/great-depression/
  • Confidence: Medium-High — one authoritative secondary source; the newspaper record (Chronicling America returned 429 twice this session) and the exact addresses, motive and the size of the final charge are still to be pulled — see Leads.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Two Polish boys with no politics bomb a village hall, two banks and two police stations in the order a ritual would want them, then die with the fifth charge. The period's reading: thrill-seeking. The folklore: the South Side said someone taught them. What is actually there: the question of what they were building the big one for.

The strike year — 54 major strikes, 29 sit-downs, and a pinball ban — 1937

  • Where: Milwaukee's plants; the Common Council chamber.
  • What happened: In 1937 Milwaukee had 54 major strikes involving more than 10,000 workers: 34 by AFL unions (including a 65-day Checker Cab walkout) and 20 by the new CIO (including a 50-day strike at Bucyrus-Erie in South Milwaukee). Twenty-nine of the fifty strikes of 1937–38 were sit-downs, until the Supreme Court declared the tactic illegal. Union membership went from about 20,000 (1930) to more than 100,000 (1939), "with virtually every major plant unionized." The same year juvenile delinquency rose 34% and the Common Council banned pinball machines and barred anyone under 21 from taverns; the WPA had 24,000 county residents on its rolls by 1938; the federal Parklawn project (518 units) opened in 1937 and the greenbelt town of Greendale (572 houses, eight miles south-west) in 1938; 21 "toy libraries" opened from 1937.
  • The odd detail: In the year every factory sat down, the city's answer to its teenagers was to outlaw pinball.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/great-depression/ ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/strikes/
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (What BENDS is the label "Communist" on the CIO organisers — see Allis-Chalmers.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A city where the workers occupy the plants at night: 29 buildings full of men sleeping beside their machines, each a locked room for whatever walks the Menomonee Valley after dark.

Allis-Chalmers, West Allis — Local 248, Harold Christoffel and the strikes of 1939 and 1941

  • Where: The Allis-Chalmers works, West Allis (the industrial suburb the company created in 1902 when it moved out of Walker's Point).
  • What happened: Allis-Chalmers — Edward P. Allis's 1861 sheriff's-sale purchase of the Reliance Works, merged in 1901 with Fraser & Chalmers, Gates Iron Works and Dickson — employed 5,500 at West Allis in 1916, 9,681 in 1937, and up to 20,000 in the war. UAW Local 248 (CIO) was formed in 1937 under Harold Christoffel, whose organisers were accused of Communist Party ties; the union struck unsuccessfully for a closed shop in 1939 and then ran the 76-day "Defense Strike" of 1941. Christoffel was later indicted for perjury before Congress. The company made steam engines, mining hoists, generators, tractors and, in the 1940s, Manhattan Project equipment.
  • The odd detail: The largest machine-builder in the state was run from a town it had built for itself, and by 1937 the men in it answered to a union president in his twenties.
  • People: Harold Christoffel — Local 248 president; Edward P. Allis — founder (d. 1889).
  • Sources: https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/allis-chalmers/ ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/strikes/ ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/workers-movements/
  • Confidence: High on the company; Christoffel's age and biography not in the pages read (UNVERIFIED beyond his role).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the "Communist" charge that defined Local 248 belongs to a world with a Soviet Union; in the setting the militant wing of the Milwaukee CIO is homegrown and German-socialist, and the owner's Germany as the patron of other people's revolutions is the obvious substitute: who funds the closed-shop strike at the plant that builds America's generators?
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The 1941 strike is outside the window, but the 1939 one is not — a Section 13 agent sent to find the foreign money in Local 248 finds instead what the company keeps in the old foundry.

2. German Milwaukee — the institutions that were never renamed

The Turners — citizenship as a wartime condition, minutes in German until the mid-1920s — 1853–1940 (deepens R07)

  • Where: Turner Hall, 1034 N. 4th St. (Henry C. Koch, 1882–83); the South Side Turners; eight chapters in the city and six more in Washington and Ozaukee counties.
  • What happened: August Willich, the exiled 1848 revolutionary, founded the Sociale Turnverein in 1853 (Turnverein Milwaukee from 1854 to 1940). At the peak one Milwaukeean in twelve had family in it; the Milwaukee gymnasts took the most prizes at the 1880 Turnfest at Frankfurt; Debs spoke at the hall in 1898; Berger led the South Side Turners from 1889. The Uihlein brothers, Frederick Pabst and Republican mayor Emil Wallber were members who objected to the socialists. When America entered the war the society made U.S. citizenship a condition of membership; it kept its minutes in German until the mid-1920s; by 1940 German was heard "on rare occasion" and only one of the eight city chapters was left.
  • The odd detail: A gymnastic society whose two factions were the brewers and the socialists, meeting under an owl in the glass.
  • People: August Willich — founder; Emil Wallber — Republican mayor and Turner; the Uihleins and Pabst — members.
  • Sources: https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/milwaukee-turners/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turner_Hall_(Milwaukee)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — no war, no citizenship rule; the minutes stay in German past 1930 and the eight chapters do not shrink to one by 1940 — the Turners of the 1930s are still a mass institution, with the brewers and the socialists still fighting inside it.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Eight halls, each with a gymnasium, a bar and a stage — the ready network for anything that needs to move men and boxes around the county without a policeman asking.

Schlitz and the Uihleins — "Uihlein Hill," Eline chocolate and a shipwreck inheritance — 1856–1933

  • Where: The Schlitz brewery at Third and Walnut (1870); Schlitz Park beer garden, Eighth St. near Walnut (1879); the Schlitz Palm Garden (1895); the Uihlein (later Alhambra) Theater (1896); the Hillside neighbourhood — "Uihlein Hill" — between Walnut and Fond du Lac/McKinley; the Eline chocolate plant on N. Port Washington Rd.
  • What happened: August Krug's 1849 tavern-brewery on Chestnut (Juneau) St. passed to his bookkeeper Joseph Schlitz, who married the widow (1858) — and when Schlitz drowned in a shipwreck in 1875 the brewery went to Krug's nephews, the Uihleins, who have held it ever since. William J. Uihlein brought pure-culture yeast from Copenhagen in 1883; the belted-globe logo dates from 1892, "the beer that made Milwaukee famous" from 1894; the firm sent beer to Dewey's men and to Roosevelt's hunting party in 1898 and passed Pabst as the largest brewer in America by 1902. Through Prohibition it made malt syrup, baked goods, sodas and "Eline" chocolate and candy at a plant on Port Washington Road; it reopened on repeal in 1933. The family's hill above the brewery, first German, was Jewish by 1900 — Golda Meir grew up at Sixth and Walnut and went to the Fourth Street School — and then Milwaukee's first substantially Black neighbourhood, which is the Bronzeville of §5.
  • The odd detail: The brewery that made Milwaukee famous has been owned since 1875 by a family named for nobody on the label, because the man on the label drowned.
  • People: Joseph Schlitz — drowned 1875; the Uihlein brothers (August, Henry, Alfred, Edward; the next generation Joseph Sr., Erwin, Robert — names beyond William J. and Joseph Sr. from general knowledge, UNVERIFIED here); Golda Mabovitch (Meir) — the Fourth Street School's most famous pupil.
  • Sources: https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/schlitz-brewing-company/ ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/hillside/
  • Confidence: High.

The Milwaukee Fictioneers — Lovecraft's correspondent at 620 E. Knapp St., the Martian's author dying on Third Street's side of town [UNVERIFIED — V_R27], and the future editor of Fate — 1932–1938

Lovecraft in 1933 as a Lincoln High schoolboy, was answered, coached and lent manuscripts, sold "The Feast in the Abbey" and "The Secret in the Tomb" to Weird Tales in July 1934 at seventeen (printed Jan and May 1935), joined the Fictioneers in 1935 (where Gus Marx was a fellow member) and later, c. 1942–53, worked as a copywriter at Marx's advertising agency ⟨fixed: V_R27⟩; Lovecraft's death in March 1937 left him feeling "a part of me had died with him." 5 (continued). The wards — Jewish Milwaukee

The Settlement Cook Book — the 50-cent pamphlet of 1901 that built the Jewish Community Center of 1931

  • Where: The Settlement House in the Haymarket district (west of the river; the Jewish quarter until the 1920s move to Sherman Park); the Abraham Lincoln House (1911); the Jewish Community Center (1931).
  • What happened: Lizzie Black Kander's The Way to a Man's Heart — The Settlement Cook Book — 24 cooking lessons and 500 recipes for the Jewish and Italian immigrant girls of the Settlement — was printed on 1 April 1901 by Yewdale and Sons at 50 cents and sold out; the second printing of 1,500 sold out; by 1925 the royalties were $25,000 and had paid for the Abraham Lincoln House (1911); the Jewish Community Center of 1931 was "financed largely by the Settlement Cook Book sales." More than two million copies in 40-plus editions (the 43rd in 1991). The 1915 edition carried a "Liberty supplement" of wartime meatless, wheatless recipes. Milwaukee's 20,000 Jews were the eleventh-largest community in the country; the Federated Jewish Charities dated from the turn of the century; in the 1930s "initially impoverished families" fleeing Nazi Germany arrived on relatives' affidavits.
  • The odd detail: The Jewish institutions of the German city stand on a cookbook.
  • People: Lizzie Black Kander — author, settlement founder (1858–1940); Rabbi Joseph Baron of Emanu-El (name from general knowledge, UNVERIFIED).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Settlement_Cook_Book ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/jews/
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS in two places — no 1915 "Liberty supplement" (no war shortages), and no 1930s refugees from a Nazi Germany that does not exist; German Jews in the setting's 1930s are citizens of the victor, and Milwaukee's German-Jewish "uptown" families are on the winning side of the hyphen. What holds: the cookbook, the Center, the Haymarket-to-Sherman-Park move.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Kabbalahs are "plural small Jewish occult traditions" — a Milwaukee one, funded by a cookbook, working out of a settlement house the Church's contractors know by its nursery school.

3/4 (continued). Relief and the river

The CWA winter — 2,000 men straightening the Milwaukee River, and 5,000 women making dolls — 1933–1940

  • Where: The Milwaukee River's S-curve and Lincoln Park lagoon (north side); Greenfield and McGovern Park pools; the Milwaukee State Teachers College (the Handicraft Project's sponsor); the WPA sewing centre.
  • What happened: The city had 11,000 men on short-shift make-work in 1930–31 and 20,500 in 1932; in the winter of 1933–34 the Civil Works Administration employed 26,000 on 138 city projects — among them "almost 2,000 men straightening an S-curve on the Milwaukee River" and building the lagoon and islands at Lincoln Park (98% men; 2.4% Black). The county's outdoor-relief department paid 50 cents an hour. The Milwaukee Handicraft Project employed more than 5,000, mostly women, making educational toys, dolls, wall hangings, rugs, furniture and textiles for schools, hospitals and orphanages; a sewing centre ran shifts of three to four hundred women and turned out nearly a million garments; in Aug 1935 "several thousand women" were classed as their family's breadwinner. By spring 1940 the WPA had built 84 public buildings, 884 miles of streets, 31 bridges, 206 miles of sidewalk and 215 miles of street lighting in the county and rebuilt 478 buildings; in 1937 there were 44,200 relief cases and 2,035 families on Aid to Dependent Children.
  • The odd detail: The city rerouted its own river with pick and shovel in the coldest winter of the decade, and paid women to make dolls by the thousand.
  • People: Elsa Ulbricht — the art teacher who ran the Handicraft Project (name from general knowledge, UNVERIFIED here).
  • Sources: https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/relief-and-welfare/ ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/great-depression/
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the New Deal alphabet is in the setting).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Two thousand men digging a new channel for the river find what the old channel was built to cover. The Handicraft dolls — 5,000 women's hands, a million garments — are the setting's version of the poppet: what is sewn into them, and by whom, on the shift nobody supervises.

8 (continued). Crime

Prohibition Milwaukee — one federal agent, six-to-one for repeal, Club Madrid, and Capone's would-be killer from Milwaukee — 1919–1933

  • Where: The roadhouses on Blue Mound Road (Club Madrid); the rum-runners' landings at Racine and Kenosha; the Milwaukee Auditorium (the 1922 rally); Frank Aiello Jr.'s house, Milwaukee.
  • What happened: In 1918 the city's nine breweries employed 6,000 and produced $35 million; under Prohibition they turned to near beer, soda, candy, cheese "and even snowplows." At one point in 1921 the whole eastern district of Wisconsin had one federal Prohibition agent; the police "sponge squads" visited speakeasies occasionally and anyone could drink "without much fear of arrest." Italians distilled from apples, potatoes and cherries and used teenagers to carry it; the rich drove out to Club Madrid; doctors wrote prescriptions. The Wisconsin Anti-Prohibition Association (1922) filled the Auditorium with 8,000; in 1929 the state repealed its Severson enforcement act 350,337 to 196,402, Milwaukee voting six to one for repeal. The Chicago war reached the city twice: in Nov 1927 Chicago police, on an anonymous tip, arrested the Milwaukee gunman Angelo La Mantio and four others hired by Joe Aiello to kill Capone and Lombardo — La Mantio confessed when apartment receipts were found in his pockets, and Capone posted two dozen gunmen outside the station to wait for Aiello; and on 23 May 1931 Aiello's nephew Frank Aiello Jr. was shot dead through the window of his Milwaukee home while playing cards, a killing police tied to the Chicago feud although he had no known rackets. Milwaukee bootlegging otherwise "went on without the volume of violence" of other cities.
  • The odd detail: A man shot at his own card table through his own window in Milwaukee for being the nephew of a dead Chicagoan.
  • People: Angelo La Mantio — Milwaukee gunman, 1927; Joe Aiello — Chicago Sicilian boss, killed 23 Oct 1930; Frank Aiello Jr. — killed Milwaukee 23 May 1931.
  • Sources: https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/prohibition/ ; https://emke.uwm.edu/entry/crime/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Aiello
  • Confidence: High (EMKE's "Frank Aiello" bootlegger murder and Wikipedia's Frank Aiello Jr. of 1931 are probably the same case — UNVERIFIED that they are).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Secret Service "knew what Capone was and took him anyway" (the brief); the Milwaukee family answers to Chicago (above). The card-table killing of 1931 is the one Milwaukee murder in the decade with Capone's shadow on it — and the window was shot from outside, at night, by something that did not come in.

10 (continued). Monuments

The Soldiers Home on the west side — Old Main (1869), a theatre with a railway ticket office, and the Veterans Administration takeover of 1930

  • Where: The Northwestern Branch, National Home for Disabled Volunteer Soldiers — 400-odd acres on the west side of the city (now the Zablocki VA Medical Center grounds); Wood National Cemetery (from 1871).
  • What happened: Congress created the National Asylum on 3 March 1865; George H. Walker won the Northwestern Branch for Milwaukee (1866–67) after a month-long Soldiers' Aid Fair on Main Street raised the money. E. Townsend Mix's Victorian Second Empire Old Main (1869), the Governor's Residence (c. 1867), the hospital (1879), Ward Memorial Hall (1882 — a theatre, meeting room, store, restaurant and railroad ticket office under one roof), the chapel (1889), the Wadsworth Library (1892) and the headquarters (1894) made a self-contained veterans' town with its own lake. In 1930 it passed to the new Veterans Administration and became the "Wood, Wisconsin" station; a paint shop went up in 1938. It is one of three surviving soldiers' homes in the country and a National Historic Landmark (2011).
  • The odd detail: A theatre that sold railway tickets, in a walled town of old soldiers on the edge of the German city.
  • People: George H. Walker — pioneer, its champion; E. Townsend Mix — architect.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwestern_Branch,_National_Home_for_Disabled_Volunteer_Soldiers_Historic_District ; https://emke.uwm.edu/?s=Soldiers+Home
