R01 — The Outfit and gangland Chicago, 1929–1939

  • Lane: R01 — The Outfit and gangland Chicago (Capone, Nitti, the Secret Six, the Touhy frame, the post-repeal rackets, gangland geography)
  • Date: 2026-09-26
  • Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent, parallel sweep)
  • Status: Complete — 57 entries in 9 themes (entries reordered by theme after progressive writing).
  • Overlap with the 09-22 archive: C01 (Lingle), C03 (Pineapple Primary), H08 (soup kitchen), R04 (St. Valentine's) are tagged in their titles and rebuilt with more detail; R05 (Cermak memorial) is cross-referenced. C04 (Ranieri kidnapping) was not re-covered.

Overview

The best find of the sweep is a primary source: Treasury agent Frank J. Wilson's 60-page final report on Capone (Dec 21, 1933), released by the IRS under FOIA, which I OCR-read directly. It changes several famous stories: the ledger that convicted Capone lay forgotten for four years in a departed agent's papers; Wilson had arranged to recruit Jake Lingle as an informant eight days before Lingle was shot; Capone later offered to hand over Lingle's killer "dead"; a key witness was hidden in South America on the Chicago Association of Commerce's money; the jury panel was bought, and the judge threw the whole panel away on the first morning; and on the day of the St. Valentine's Day Massacre Capone was in a Miami prosecutor's office with a stenographer taking notes. It also holds scenes a GM can lift whole: a Berwyn minister's 1925 raid on Capone's casino ("Reverend, can't we get together? … I give to churches"), and a Stickney brothel's ledgers that charged each woman for towels and were kept in the same building as the dynamite. Around that core the file covers the whole decade: the Public Enemies list and the Secret Six; Capone's trial, prison and syphilis; the fight for the succession (the police shooting of Nitti, Ted Newberry's corpse in the Dunes with Capone's jewelled buckle left on it, Cermak's death in Miami); a run of gangland killings with strong odd details (59 bullets in Joe Aiello; McGurn's comic valentine; McErlane dying on the anniversary of the day he killed his wife and her dogs; O'Hare shot with a crucifix, a rosary and notes on "religious trends" in his pockets a week before Capone walked free); the Factor "kidnapping" and the Touhy frame; the post-repeal rackets (milk, the racing wire, and the Outfit's hold over every projection booth, stage and janitor's closet in Chicago's theatres); the machine politics and fixers; and the places — the Lexington, the Green Mill, Mount Carmel with its "Italian Bride", the Wisconsin hideaways. Church threads for IHS recur on their own: the Holy Name cornerstone whose Bible verse was shot away, O'Banion the ex-choirboy refused consecrated ground, the minister Capone tried to buy, the incorrupt bride. Section 13 threads recur too: the man who caught Capone (Wilson) became Chief of the Secret Service; Ness went from Capone's breweries to Cleveland in time for the Torso Murders. The main divergence risks are Cermak's shooting (the President-elect was the target), anything tied to J. Edgar Hoover's Bureau (the setting has K. Edgar Hoover's FBI, with Section 13 as its supernatural division, and a separate, rival Secret Service) ⟨fixed: factions — the Secret Service and FBI Section 13 are separate; K. Edgar Hoover runs the FBI⟩, Big Bill Thompson's anti-British politics (Britain lost the war in the setting), and a few people's Great War service.

A. Living memory — the 1920s landmarks every 1930s Chicagoan knew

Terry Druggan and Frankie Lake — the gangsters who went home from jail (1924) and the 1932 tax convictions

  • Where: Cook County Jail; the "Bloody Maxwell" district (Valley Gang territory); Lake Zurich estate
  • What happened: The Valley Gang, an Irish street gang formed in the 1890s around Maxwell Street, became the Druggan-Lake beer organisation, owning several breweries by the early 1920s with partner Joseph Stenson. Sentenced to a year in 1924, Druggan and Lake bribed Sheriff Peter Hoffman and Warden Wesley Westbrook to let them come and go from Cook County Jail; after a Chicago American exposé Hoffman was fined $2,500 and jailed for a month, and Westbrook got four months. Wilson's Treasury unit convicted them of tax evasion in 1932, and the gang was absorbed into the Outfit by the end of Prohibition. Druggan retired rich — estates on Lake Zurich and in Florida, a racing stable — and died March 4, 1954.
  • The odd detail: The sheriff went to jail for letting the prisoners out.
  • People: Terry Druggan and Frankie Lake — Valley Gang bosses · Peter Hoffman — Cook County sheriff · Wesley Westbrook — warden
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terry_Druggan · https://www.irs.gov/pub/foia/file-2-report-dated-12211933-in-re-alphonse-capone-by-sa-frank-wilson.pdf (printed p. 1)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Two prisoners who could leave their cells at night — the GM can decide where they went, and why the guards preferred them out.

Dean O'Banion and Hymie Weiss — the flower shop and the cathedral cornerstone — November 1924 and October 11, 1926

  • Where: Schofield's Flower Shop, 738 N. State Street, and Holy Name Cathedral directly across the street, Near North Side
  • What happened: Dean O'Banion, boss of the North Side gang, was shot dead in Schofield's Flower Shop in November 1924, igniting a five-year war with Capone. His successor Earl "Hymie" Weiss — Polish Roman Catholic despite the name, who carried a rosary along with his gun — was machine-gunned at 4 p.m. on October 11, 1926 by two men firing from a rooming house as he crossed toward his State Street headquarters; he was hit about ten times, and Patrick Murray died with him in front of the cathedral. Stray rounds chipped the cathedral's cornerstone (laid July 19, 1874), shattering its Bible inscription — "At the name of Jesus every knee should bow in heaven and on earth" (Philippians 2:10) — so that it read "Every knee should heaven and on earth." Three weeks earlier, on September 20, 1926, Weiss's convoy of ten cars had fired more than 1,000 rounds into Capone's Hawthorne Hotel on 22nd Street in Cicero. Weiss and O'Banion lie at Mount Carmel, Hillside — the same cemetery as Capone.
  • O'Banion and the Church: O'Banion had sung in the Holy Name Cathedral choir as a boy. He bought into William Schofield's flower shop in 1921 — it became "the florist of choice for mob funerals", with the gang's headquarters upstairs. On November 10, 1924 he was clipping chrysanthemums in the back room when Frankie Yale came in with John Scalise and Albert Anselmi; Yale shook his hand and held it while the others shot him. The Archdiocese of Chicago denied him burial in consecrated ground — a priest said the Lord's Prayer and three Hail Marys — and he lay in unconsecrated ground at Mount Carmel until his family won his reburial in consecrated soil.
  • The odd detail: The bullets erased the words "at the name of Jesus" from a cathedral cornerstone — across the street from the flower shop of a former cathedral choirboy whom the Church had refused to bury. Stairs now cover the damaged part; legend says newlyweds used to put their fingers in the bullet hole for luck [UNVERIFIED — V_R01].
  • People: Dean O'Banion — North Side boss, florist, ex-choirboy · Hymie Weiss — his successor · Patrick Murray — killed with Weiss · Frankie Yale — the handshake
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymie_Weiss · https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/chicago/news/chicago-hauntings-gangland-ghosts-holy-name-cathedral/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_O%27Banion
  • Confidence: High on the killings; Medium on the exact erased wording (a contemporary account quoted by CBS); the ghost stories and newlywed custom are legend.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916? No — 1924/1926, after the break, but domestic calendar holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): IHS hook. A cornerstone inscription is a consecration. When gangland bullets erased "at the name of Jesus", the cathedral's ward over the Near North Side was broken — and IHS has been quietly trying to recut the stone since 1926 without anyone noticing a church repairing a gangster's bullet holes. Legend says Weiss walks State Street.

The Reverend Hoover's raid on the Hawthorne Smoke Shop — May 16, 1925 ("Derby Day")

  • Where: Hawthorne Smoke Shop, second floor, 4818 W. 22nd Street, Cicero (the club also operated at the Western Hotel, 4837 W. 22nd, and at 4838, 4835 and 4738 W. 22nd over five years)
  • What happened: The Rev. Henry C. Hoover of Berwyn, president of the West Suburban Ministers' / Citizens' Association of Cook County, led his members with Cook County deputy sheriffs under a Lt. Davidson in a raid on Capone's Cicero casino on Kentucky Derby Day, May 16, 1925 — roulette wheels, blackjack, race charts, pool tables covered for craps, money on the tables. Capone pushed in ("It ought to be my party. I own the place"), emptied the cash register, gave his name as "Al Brown", and warned "This is the last raid you will ever pull." Twenty minutes later he came back shaved and changed and took the minister aside: "Reverend, can't we get together? … I give to churches, and I give to charity … if you will let up on me in Cicero, I will withdraw from Stickney." Hoover refused. A few nights later association member David H. Morgan, a Western Springs machinist, was jumped in his garage by four men, shot and left for dead; he survived a month in hospital. None of the reformers testified, the association made no more raids, and the casino reopened half an hour after the raid. Hoover gave Frank Wilson a sworn statement on March 10, 1931 that went into the tax case.
  • The odd detail: Capone tried to bargain with a minister — offering to pull his brothels out of one suburb if the church let him keep his casino in the next — and opened with "I give to churches."
  • People: Rev. Henry C. Hoover — Berwyn minister, reform raider · David H. Morgan — machinist, shot in his garage · Mr. Bragg — association officer · Lt. Davidson — deputy sheriff in charge · Al Capone — "Al Brown"
  • Sources: https://www.irs.gov/pub/foia/file-2-report-dated-12211933-in-re-alphonse-capone-by-sa-frank-wilson.pdf (Exhibits 8 and 10, printed pp. 10–17, 27–28)
  • Confidence: High — sworn statements and trial testimony in a federal report (the minister's first name is OCR'd as "H. C."; "Henry" is the usual reading — Medium).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (1925, living memory for the 1930s).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A Protestant minister who stood up to Capone, a machinist shot in the dark, and a bargain Capone offered a man of God. In the setting, Hoover's association had a Catholic twin that never raided anything — IHS watched the same casino for a different reason, and Morgan was shot by men who were never found because they were not men.

The Harlem Inn brothel and its ledgers — bought February 29, 1924; raided April 1926

  • Where: Harlem Inn, 4225 Harlem Avenue, Stickney, Illinois (the western suburb adjoining Cicero and Berwyn)
  • What happened: The Capone syndicate bought the Harlem Inn on February 29, 1924 in the name of Louis Lipschultz (Jack Guzik's brother-in-law), who deeded it on January 7, 1925 to Jeannette Keithly, Guzik's sister-in-law. It was a brothel; the Rev. Hoover's association had earlier forced the sheriff to raid it. When police hunted Capone after the McSwiggin murder in April 1926 they raided the Inn and found firearms, "a large amount of dynamite", whiskey — and the daily books. Eighteen days of records (April 6–25, 1926) showed $6,398 in takings, projected to $129,000 a year ⟨fixed: V_R01⟩. Each sheet listed every woman by name in column one, her night's income, the house's cut, a 10% charge "for towel service", her net, her commission on liquor sold, and her total.
  • The odd detail: The house charged its women for towels — in writing, by name, every night — and kept dynamite in the same building as the ledgers.
  • People: Louis Lipschultz — front owner · Jeannette Keithly — Guzik's sister-in-law, deeded owner · Jack Guzik — the syndicate's money man · Rev. H. C. Hoover — reformer who pressed the sheriff
  • Sources: https://www.irs.gov/pub/foia/file-2-report-dated-12211933-in-re-alphonse-capone-by-sa-frank-wilson.pdf (printed pp. 27–28)
  • Confidence: High on the report's figures; the report (printed p. 27) states the house's cut (column 3) as 50% of each woman's income and the towel charge (column 4) as 10% of column two ⟨fixed: V_R01⟩. The women's names are in the exhibits (not in the report text).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (1924–26, living memory).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A brothel ledger names every woman who worked there each night. A GM can use a column of names that repeats — the same woman, every night, for eighteen nights, never earning anything — as the sign that something lived at the Harlem Inn that the house fed rather than paid.

Assistant State's Attorney McSwiggin killed at the Pony Inn — April 27, 1926 (living memory)

  • Where: Pony Inn saloon, 5613 W. 12th Street (now Roosevelt Road), Cicero
  • What happened: William H. McSwiggin, a Cicero-raised assistant state's attorney known as the "Hanging Prosecutor", was out drinking with boyhood friends — the West Side O'Donnell gang's James J. Doherty and Thomas "Red" Duffy — when a machine gun opened up from a car outside the Pony Inn, killing all three. (Wilson's Treasury report dates it to the night of April 26.) McSwiggin had earlier prosecuted Myles O'Donnell and Doherty in the Tancl-Klimas murder case, in which they were acquitted, amid rumours that he had thrown it for his friends. Capone was accused and vanished for months; per Wilson, Treasury agents eventually found him and handed him to the Chicago police, who announced they had no evidence to hold him. The police raids that night seized the Hawthorne Smoke Shop ledger — which would convict Capone five years later.
  • The odd detail: The prosecutor was drinking with the gangsters he had prosecuted — and the raid after his murder produced the book that finally jailed Capone.
  • People: William H. McSwiggin — prosecutor · James J. Doherty, Thomas "Red" Duffy — killed with him · Myles O'Donnell — West Side gang boss
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_H._McSwiggin (redirects to the Myles O'Donnell article) · https://www.irs.gov/pub/foia/file-2-report-dated-12211933-in-re-alphonse-capone-by-sa-frank-wilson.pdf (printed pp. 4, 10)
  • Confidence: High; date discrepancy (April 26 vs 27) noted.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A prosecutor who threw a murder case for his friends, then died beside them. Three layers: the period saw a gang hit; the rumour saw a crooked prosecutor; underneath, the Tancl-Klimas case he threw had a witness that wasn't human.

The Pineapple Primary — April 10, 1928 (also in 09-22 archive: C03)

  • Where: Chicago and Cook County; Senator Charles Deneen's home, 457 W. 61st Place; Judge John A. Swanson's home, 7217 Crandon Avenue; near 800 S. Oakley Boulevard (Esposito); the 20th Ward (Granady)
  • What happened: In the six months before the Republican primary of April 10, 1928, Chicago saw 62 bombings and at least two politicians killed — the "pineapple" was period slang for a hand grenade. On March 21, 1928 "Diamond Joe" Esposito — Nineteenth Ward Republican committeeman, owner of the Bella Napoli Café where Paul Ricca waited tables — was shot down after a day's campaigning within sight of his home near 800 S. Oakley; his bodyguards were untouched and no one was charged. On March 26 bombs hit the homes of U.S. Senator Charles Deneen (457 W. 61st Place) and his State's Attorney candidate John A. Swanson (7217 Crandon Avenue) at the same time. On election day Octavius C. Granady — a reform candidate for 20th Ward committeeman, a lawyer from the U.S. Virgin Islands — was chased in his car and killed. Voters turned on the violence: Deneen's slate won, Louis Emmerson beat Governor Len Small by 400,000 votes and Swanson beat Robert Crowe by 120,000, crushing Thompson's faction.
  • The odd detail: Two bombs went off at two candidates' houses on opposite sides of the city on the same night — and the bombing backfired so badly the voters threw the bombers' friends out.
  • People: Charles Deneen — U.S. senator, reform faction · John A. Swanson — State's Attorney 1928–32 (his investigator was Pat Roche) · "Diamond Joe" Esposito — ward boss, killed · Octavius C. Granady — Black reform candidate from the Virgin Islands, killed · William Hale Thompson — mayor whose faction lost
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pineapple_Primary · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Ricca
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. Granady's Virgin Islands origin fits the setting (the Danish islands are an American possession there too).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Granady was chased through the streets on election day — a Caribbean-born lawyer in a city where the Golden Circle's map is centred on Havana. His killers were Thompson men in the record; the setting can ask who pointed them at him.

The pawnshop revolvers and the Frankie Yale killing — 1928 (living memory)

  • Where: A Miami pawnshop; Palm Island, Miami Beach; Brooklyn (Yale's murder)
  • What happened: Parker Henderson, who ran the Ponce de Leon Hotel in Miami where Capone stayed (room 804), cashed Capone's Western Union money orders of $1,000 to $5,000, negotiated the purchase of the Palm Island house for Capone for $40,000 (Capone himself said $50,000 cash), and held title before transferring it to Mae Capone. At Capone's request he bought six revolvers at a Miami pawnshop with Capone's money and handed them over. When New York police traced the revolver found near the body of Brooklyn boss Frankie Yale — shot July 1, 1928 (date from general record, not the report) — to that pawnshop purchase, Henderson was arrested for the murder; Capone told him not to worry, and the case was dropped. Capone gave him a diamond belt buckle.
  • The odd detail: The gun that killed Frankie Yale was traced by its serial number to a pawnshop in Miami and to Capone's money — and nothing happened.
  • People: Parker Henderson — Miami hotel man and Capone's front · Frankie Yale — Brooklyn boss, Capone's old employer, killed 1928 · Mae Capone — titled owner of Palm Island
  • Sources: https://www.irs.gov/pub/foia/file-2-report-dated-12211933-in-re-alphonse-capone-by-sa-frank-wilson.pdf (Exhibit 88, printed pp. 36–37)
  • Confidence: High for Henderson's sworn account as recorded; the Yale date is general knowledge, not checked this session (Medium).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Six pawnshop revolvers, one accounted for. The other five are a scavenger hunt: each turns up at a killing in a different Great Lakes city over the decade.

The St. Valentine's Day Massacre — February 14, 1929, about 10:30 a.m. (also in 09-22 archive: R04)

  • Where: SMC Cartage Company garage, 2122 N. Clark Street, Lincoln Park
  • What happened: Four to six men — two dressed as police — arrived in a black Cadillac made up as a police car, lined seven men against the garage wall and fired about 70 rounds from Thompson guns and one shotgun blast. Dead: Albert Kachellek (alias James Clark, Moran's second and brother-in-law), Adam Heyer (bookkeeper), Albert Weinshank (cleaning-and-dyeing), Frank and Peter Gusenberg (enforcers), Reinhardt Schwimmer (optician turned gambler) and John May (mechanic). May's dog Highball's barking brought the neighbours. Frank Gusenberg, still alive when police arrived, told them "No one shot me." Bugs Moran saw the "police car", took it for a raid and went round the corner for coffee. Calvin Goddard's ballistics work cleared the Chicago police's Thompsons. In December 1929 Fred Burke shot Patrolman Charles Skelly in St. Joseph, Michigan; police searching his house found two Thompsons identified as the massacre guns. Byron Bolton's 1935 confession named Fred Goetz, Gus Winkler, Fred Burke, Ray Nugent and Bob Carey. The garage was demolished in 1967; the wall's bricks went to a Canadian businessman and now the Mob Museum in Las Vegas.
  • Capone's alibi, from the Treasury file: the same day, February 14, 1929, a Miami stenographer, Ruth Gaskin, took notes at a hearing in the office of the Dade County Solicitor (Robert Taylor) at which Capone was questioned in the presence of the Brooklyn District Attorney and Sheriff M. P. Lehman — he told them "I am a gambler, play race horses", that people called him "Al Brown" and that he took money wired from Chicago under the name "A. Costa". (Frank Wilson report, Dec 21, 1933, Exhibit 89.)
  • The odd detail: The massacre guns were found in Michigan because of a traffic accident and a dead patrolman, ten months later — and that same day Capone was sitting in a Florida prosecutor's office with a stenographer taking down every word.
  • People: Bugs Moran — the intended target, absent · Frank Gusenberg — "No one shot me" · Calvin Goddard — ballistics pioneer · Fred Burke — suspect, St. Joseph, Michigan · Byron Bolton — 1935 confessor
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Valentine%27s_Day_Massacre · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bugs_Moran · https://www.irs.gov/pub/foia/file-2-report-dated-12211933-in-re-alphonse-capone-by-sa-frank-wilson.pdf (Exhibit 89, printed p. 37)
  • Confidence: High. (The Miami hearing is dated Feb 14, 1929 in the report; the hour of the hearing is not stated, so it is a same-day alibi, not a same-hour one.)
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (after 1916, but the setting keeps the American domestic record).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Already a Capone-era landmark in the setting. Proposal: the garage was chosen because of what the seven men were storing, not who they were — and the bricks were sold to a Canadian, off American soil, because someone knew what a wall that had seen that could hold.

The Atlantic City Conference — May 13–16, 1929

  • Where: Ritz-Carlton and Ambassador hotels, Atlantic City, New Jersey (the Breakers had refused them as non-WASP guests)
  • What happened: Organised by Meyer Lansky — on his honeymoon — with Johnny Torrio, Lucky Luciano and Frank Costello, and hosted by Atlantic City boss Enoch "Nucky" Johnson, the conference brought bosses together to discuss bootleg competition in New York and Chicago, plans for repeal, a shift into bookmaking, racing and casinos, and a national structure. Chicago sent Al Capone, Frank Nitti, Jake Guzik, Frank Rio and Frank McErlane. Afterwards Capone and Rio went to Philadelphia, where "two friendly police officers" arrested them for carrying guns (May 16, 1929) — the year in Eastern State followed.
  • The odd detail: The first American mob summit was held because the Breakers hotel wouldn't take them — and it ended with Capone arranging his own arrest.
  • People: Meyer Lansky — organiser · Enoch "Nucky" Johnson — host · Johnny Torrio — Capone's old mentor · Frank Rio — Capone's bodyguard, arrested with him
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_City_Conference · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Capone
  • Confidence: High on dates, venues and attendance as the article gives them (the "friendly" arrest is a common interpretation).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A national meeting of crime bosses at a seaside resort is a good mirror for a meeting of other powers — the Golden Circle held its own session in a different Atlantic City hotel the same week.

Scalise, Anselmi and Giunta found near Hammond — May 8, 1929

  • Where: A lonely road near Hammond, Lake County, Indiana
  • What happened: In the early hours of May 8, 1929 the bodies of John Scalise, Albert Anselmi and Joseph "Hop Toad" Giunta were found near Hammond. All three had been savagely beaten and shot; one wound had torn off Scalise's left little finger, and the coroner said he had never seen such disfigured bodies. The North Side gang was suspected at first but cleared within days. Years later the "more popular story" emerged: Capone invited them to a banquet and beat them with a baseball bat before gunmen finished them.
  • The odd detail: The baseball-bat banquet — the scene every film uses — is a later story; the record has only the bodies and the coroner's horror.
  • People: John Scalise and Albert Anselmi — Capone gunmen, Massacre suspects · Joseph Giunta — Unione Siciliana head
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Scalise
  • Confidence: High on the discovery; the banquet is legend (labelled as such).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers made to order: the papers said gang war; the legend says a banquet and a bat; what the coroner saw — bodies "disfigured" past anything he had seen — was not done by a bat.

B. The war on Capone — crusaders, lists, ledgers and the tax case (1929–1932)

Capone's year away and the contempt case — February 1929 to March 1931

  • Where: Miami (subpoena, February 17, 1929); Philadelphia (arrest, May 16, 1929); Eastern State Penitentiary, Philadelphia; U.S. District Court, Chicago
  • What happened: On February 17, 1929 — three days after the St. Valentine's Day Massacre — Capone was served in Florida with a subpoena to appear before a federal grand jury in Chicago, and did not come (the usual account is that he pleaded illness in Miami; the FBI's page on this was not reachable this session). On May 16, 1929, leaving a gangland conference in Atlantic City, he was arrested in Philadelphia for carrying a concealed weapon and given a year by Judge John E. Walsh; he was moved to Eastern State Penitentiary on August 8, 1929 and released in March 1930. On May 8, 1930 he was arrested in Miami on the orders of the mayor, city manager and public-safety director. On March 2, 1931 Judge Wilkerson sentenced him to six months in Cook County Jail for contempt of court over the ignored subpoena — the first federal sentence he ever received. At his July 30, 1931 plea hearing Wilkerson refused a proposed deal of two and a half years at Leavenworth.
  • The odd detail: The first federal punishment the most notorious gangster in America ever got was for not answering a subpoena.
  • People: Al Capone · John E. Walsh — Philadelphia judge · James H. Wilkerson — federal judge
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Capone · https://chicagology.com/notorious-chicago/capone3/
  • Confidence: High on dates; the Atlantic City conference as the reason for the Philadelphia trip is common knowledge but not checked this session (Medium). Chicagology's summary also gives the Eastern State term as "May 17, 1928 – March 17, 1930", an evident typo for 1929.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. The subpoena and contempt case were the Justice Department's Bureau of Investigation's work in the record — BENDS lightly (in the setting, Section 13 served it).

The murder of Jake Lingle — June 9, 1930 (also in 09-22 archive: C01)

  • Where: The pedestrian underpass to the Illinois Central Randolph Street station, beneath Michigan Avenue at Randolph Street, the Loop
  • What happened: Alfred "Jake" Lingle, a Chicago Tribune legman, was shot once in the back of the head with a .38 in the crowded underpass while heading for the 1:30 pm train to the races; dozens saw a thin, blond, blue-eyed gunman. The Tribune posted a $25,000 reward and other papers added $30,000 ($55,000 total). Then the story turned: on a $65-a-week salary Lingle had more than $60,000 in the bank, two homes, a suite at the Morrison Hotel and a six-figure brokerage account; he had been fixing beer prices, and was tied into dog racing and gambling at Washington Park. He was a childhood friend of Police Commissioner William F. Russell. Treasury agent Frank J. Wilson's own report (Dec 21, 1933) adds: on June 1, 1930 — eight days before the murder — Wilson had arranged through a Tribune attorney and the paper's editor to interview Lingle at the Tribune office to recruit him as a confidential informant on Capone's finances; the Tribune told Wilson that Lingle knew more about Capone than any reporter in the country, but he was killed before a date was fixed. In October 1930 P. F. Roche, chief investigator for the Cook County State's Attorney, met Capone secretly to ask for help finding the killer; in early November 1930 Capone offered to hand over "the dead body of the murderer", and Roche broke off contact. The Tribune reported that within three days of the murder federal agents had come asking what it knew of Capone's income — "Lingle, dead, could not present evidence of Capone's income tax frauds." Leo Vincent Brothers of St. Louis was arrested in January 1931, convicted, sentenced to a minimum of 14 years, and served 8; Wilson notes "no motive was proven" at the trial.

Capone's soup kitchen — early November 1930 to April 10, 1931 (also in 09-22 archive: H08)

Eliot Ness and the "Untouchables" — March 1931 to May 1932

  • Where: Bureau of Prohibition, Chicago; Capone breweries across the city; Dearborn Station (Polk and Dearborn Streets)

Capone's tax trial — indicted June 5, 1931; convicted October 17, 1931; sentenced October 1931

  • Where: U.S. District Court, Chicago (Federal Building); Cook County Jail
  • What happened: Capone was indicted on June 5, 1931 on 22 counts of evading income tax for 1925–29 (a second Wilson-era source says 23 counts across March and June grand juries) and released on $50,000 bail. He pleaded guilty on June 16 expecting a light plea bargain, but Judge James Herbert Wilkerson refused to be bound by it on July 30, 1931, and Capone went to trial. Wilson's report records the jury fight: the defence bought an advance copy of the special venire of 100 prospective jurors, which was delivered to Capone at the Lexington Hotel and handed out to gang members, union agents and friends to work on; Judge Wilkerson swapped the entire panel for another on the first day of trial, then locked the jury up under deputy marshals when Capone's people began reaching jurors' families. Capone's bodyguard Phil D'Andrea sat behind him glaring at witnesses — and was caught in court with a revolver and ammunition in his pocket; Wilkerson's contempt ruling called the Lexington Hotel the "outlaw camp" of "an organized body of men" whose coercion was "nothing less than insurrection against the laws of the United States." On October 17, 1931 Capone was convicted of tax evasion for 1925–27 and failure to file for 1928–29. On October 24 Wilkerson gave him five years on each of the three felony counts (two concurrent) and a year in Cook County Jail on each misdemeanour — 11 years in all — a $50,000 fine plus $7,692 costs, and liability for $215,000 in back taxes plus interest.
  • The odd detail: Capone tried to plead guilty — the trial only happened because the judge would not honour the deal; and the judge beat the jury-fixers by throwing away the whole panel on the morning of trial.

C. Capone behind bars (1932–1947)

Capone in Atlanta, Alcatraz and Terminal Island — May 1932 to November 16, 1939

  • Where: U.S. Penitentiary, Atlanta; Alcatraz, San Francisco Bay; FCI Terminal Island, California; Union Memorial Hospital, Baltimore; Palm Island, Miami
  • What happened: Capone went to Atlanta in May 1932, aged 33; the intake examination diagnosed syphilis and gonorrhea. He was moved to the new Alcatraz in August 1934. In February 1938 he was formally diagnosed with neurosyphilis, then transferred to Terminal Island, and paroled on November 16, 1939. He was treated at Union Memorial Hospital in Baltimore in 1939–40, took penicillin in 1942, and lived out his life at Palm Island in Florida. He had a stroke on January 21, 1947, cardiac arrest the next day, and died January 25, 1947, aged 48. He was buried at Mount Olivet Cemetery, Chicago, and reburied at Mount Carmel Cemetery, Hillside, in 1950.
  • The odd detail: The disease that ended him was found at the Atlanta intake examination in 1932 — the most powerful gangster in America went into prison already carrying the thing that would take his mind.
  • Alcatraz and Baltimore: on good behaviour he was allowed to play banjo in the Alcatraz prison band, the Rock Islanders, which gave Sunday concerts for inmates; on June 23, 1936 he was stabbed and superficially wounded by inmate James C. Lucas. Grateful for his care at Union Memorial Hospital in Baltimore, he donated two Japanese weeping cherry trees to it in 1939. His 1947 funeral in Chicago was private. Earlier, from August 8, 1923, the family home had been a two-flat at 7244 S. Prairie Avenue (bought for $5,500).

D. The succession — the Outfit's bosses and the mayor who fought them (1931–1943)

Paul "the Waiter" Ricca — the real boss behind Nitti, 1931–1943

Convicted in the Hollywood extortion case on December 30, 1943 and given ten years, he was paroled with his co-defendants on August 13, 1947 — a release widely seen as political.

Frank Nitti shot by police — December 19, 1932

  • Where: Room 554, 221 N. LaSalle Street (the LaSalle-Wacker Building), the Loop
  • What happened: Frank Nitti, released from Leavenworth on March 25, 1932 after an 18-month tax sentence, had become the working boss of the Capone organisation. On December 19, 1932 a police squad led by Detective Sergeants Harry Lang and Harry Miller, with Officer Chris Callahan, raided his office. Lang fired five times and hit Nitti three times — neck, chest and back near the spine — and claimed Nitti had shot him in the hand. Lang was paid $300 for "meritorious service". At the 1933 trial Callahan testified that Nitti had been searched twice and was unarmed: "There was only one gun fired up there. Lang must have shot himself." Nitti said Lang shot him while Callahan held his wrists. The Unione Siciliana's chair — four presidents dead in 26 months, September 1928 to October 1930

Joe Aiello and the 59 bullets — October 23, 1930

Frank McErlane — the woman, the dogs, and the anniversary death — October 8, 1931 and October 8, 1932

On October 8, 1931 he shot Rigus four times and killed her German shepherds; associates then paid for his "retirement" to a houseboat. I. Gangland geography — where they lived, drank, hid, died and were buried

Capone moves into the Lexington — July 30, 1928; the "outlaw camp" to 1932

  • Where: Lexington Hotel, Michigan Avenue at 22nd Street (modern address 2135 S. Michigan; Wilson's 1933 report gives "2300 Michigan Boulevard"); before it, the Metropole Hotel, 23rd and Michigan
  • What happened: The ten-storey Lexington was built in 1891–92 for visitors to the 1893 World's Fair. Room clerk Walter Housen testified that on July 30, 1928 Capone arrived with 15 or 20 men, none of whom would sign the register; Housen signed for them, putting Capone in Room 230 under the name "George Phillips", and every man under an assumed name; a man called "Butsie" paid the bills. At the Metropole, where the organisation had been headquartered before, Capone had lived under the name "Ross" (OCR reading). Judge Wilkerson called the Lexington the gang's "outlaw camp", to which witnesses and even defence counsel were summoned. Later Tribune accounts (1981, via Chicagology) put Capone in suite 530 on the fifth floor at the corner of Michigan and 22nd (renamed Cermak Road in 1933), where he "could sit in a swivel chair in his bay window and watch his beer trucks rumble by", with a lavender bathtub, washbowl and toilet with gold fixtures, a gymnasium on the second floor, and "the wrought-iron latticework elevator cage … lined with bullet-proof sheet metal"; September 1930 reporting had his men holding another floor with guards in the lobby, and the rent was reportedly $1,500 a month. The Lexington remained Capone's residence until his arrest in 1931. Later it became the New Michigan Hotel and a 400-room brothel; it closed in 1980. On April 21, 1986 its sealed vaults were opened on live television, The Mystery of Al Capone's Vaults, before about 30 million viewers. It was demolished in 1995 despite landmark listing; "the Lex" apartments (2012) stand on the site.
  • The odd detail: Capone's room at the Lexington was registered to a man who did not exist, in the hand of the desk clerk, because not one of twenty gangsters would write his own name in the book.
  • People: Walter Housen — room clerk at both the Metropole and the Lexington · "George Phillips" — Capone's register name · "Butsie" — the man who paid
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexington_Hotel_(Chicago) · https://www.irs.gov/pub/foia/file-2-report-dated-12211933-in-re-alphonse-capone-by-sa-frank-wilson.pdf (Exhibit 96, printed pp. 32–33, 40–41) · https://chicagology.com/goldenage/goldenage162/ · https://chicagology.com/goldenage/goldenage163/ (Metropole: 2300 S. Michigan, built 1891 by Clinton J. Warren; Capone there 1925–summer 1928 as "Mr. Ross", guarded doors, five-to-eight rooms)
  • Confidence: High; note the conflict between the 1931 clerk's "Room 230" (registration) and the 1981 Tribune's "suite 530" (living quarters) — both may be true. The Metropole alias "Mr. Ross" is confirmed by Chicagology.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The 1986 vaults held nothing. In the setting that is because they were emptied in 1932 — by Section 13, the week Capone went to Atlanta. The GM knows what was in them.

The Green Mill — McGurn's club, 1926 onward; burned April 26, 1933

  • Where: Green Mill, 4802 N. Broadway at Lawrence, Uptown (the Green Mill Gardens complex, built 1914, had a huge green windmill on the roof, a ballroom and a beer garden)
  • What happened: Jack McGurn became a part-owner around 1926. Singer-comic Joe E. Lewis refused to renew his contract and went to a rival club; McGurn's men slashed his throat (1927) — he survived, and the story became the 1957 film The Joker Is Wild. At about 7 a.m. on April 26, 1933 a fire starting in the basement below a Walgreen drugstore at 4800 N. Broadway did an estimated $100,000 damage and made the Green Mill Ballroom a "total loss" (Tribune and Daily News). The present Green Mill lounge only occupied its site from 1935 — three years after Capone went to prison — though a curved booth facing both doors is still called "Al Capone's booth". A "popular story" says patrons could escape through coal tunnels via a trapdoor behind the bar.
  • The odd detail: "Capone's booth" is in a room Capone could never have sat in.
  • People: Jack McGurn — part-owner · Joe E. Lewis — entertainer, throat cut · Ted Newberry — linked to the Green Mill mob in some accounts
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Mill_Cocktail_Lounge · https://www.robertloerzel.com/2024/09/12/bullets-flames-lawsuits-and-votes-the-end-of-an-era/
  • Confidence: High on ownership, the fire and the 1935 date; the tunnels are legend.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A booth for a man who never sat in it — the regulars swear someone does, most nights, after 2 a.m. Legend says the tunnels run to the lake.

Mount Carmel Cemetery, Hillside — gangland's Catholic burial ground (consecrated 1901)

  • Where: Wolf and Roosevelt Roads, Hillside, Illinois (by today's I-290); Archdiocese of Chicago; 214 acres, 226,000+ graves
  • What happened: Mount Carmel holds Al Capone (reburied here in 1950 from Mount Olivet) with his brothers Frank and Ralph; all six Genna brothers; Jack McGurn; Dean O'Banion; Hymie Weiss; Frank Nitti; Roger Touhy; later Sam Giancana. It also holds the Bishops' Mausoleum (1905–12, designed by William J. Brinkmann, interior by Aristide Leonori), a stepped pyramid crowned by the Archangel Gabriel, where Chicago's archbishops lie. And it holds Julia Buccola Petta, "the Italian Bride": she died on March 17, 1921, aged 29, giving birth to a stillborn son, Filippo, and was buried in her wedding dress. About six years later her mother Filomena won permission to open the grave; Julia's body was found still mostly intact, though her son and the arm holding him had decayed. Her monument carries a photograph of her in her wedding dress, a statue made from it — and a photograph of her corpse after exhumation. Legend says her mother had dreamed she was alive; Wikipedia notes no contemporary source supports the dreams.
  • The odd detail: A photograph of an exhumed body on a public monument, a few hundred yards from Capone and the archbishops.
  • People: Julia Buccola Petta — "the Italian Bride" · Filomena Buccola — her mother · the Gennas, O'Banion, Weiss, McGurn, Nitti, Touhy, Capone — residents
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Carmel_Cemetery_(Hillside,_Illinois) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_Petta · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hymie_Weiss
  • Confidence: High on burials and the exhumation; the dreams are legend (labelled).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): IHS hook. An incorrupt body in consecrated ground next to the bishops' tomb is exactly what the Church investigates quietly. Three layers: the family said miracle; the legend says the mother's dreams; underneath, the Italian Bride is the reason gangland buries its dead at Mount Carmel — something there keeps them down.

The Wisconsin hideaways — Couderay, Mercer and Barker Lake (1920s–1930s)

  • Where: "The Hideout", a private lake five miles north of Couderay, Sawyer County; Mercer, Iron County; Barker Lake, Winter, Sawyer County, Wisconsin
  • What happened: The Chicago gangsters retreated north. Al Capone had a home on a private lake five miles north of Couderay in the 1920s and 1930s — now a tourist site called "The Hideout", bought in spring 2010 by the Lac Courte Oreilles Band of Lake Superior Chippewa after the previous owners' bankruptcy. Ralph "Bottles" Capone — whose nickname came from running the organisation's legitimate soft-drink and bottling business — was convicted of tax evasion (three years, Leavenworth, then McNeil Island per the 1933 Treasury file); in the 1930s he bought a home in Mercer and later ran the Rex Hotel and Billy's Bar there; he died in Hurley, Wisconsin on November 22, 1974. "Polack Joe" Saltis, the Back of the Yards beer boss named on the 1930 Public Enemies list, lost his organisation when his political ally John "Dingbat" Oberta was found shot dead in his car with his chauffeur Sam Malaga on March 5, 1930 (the Saltis article's "March 25" is an error; the McErlane article and the record give March 5) ⟨fixed: V_R01⟩ and "quickly retired to his home on Barker Lake in Winter, Wisconsin". The Jack Zuta killing (Delafield, Aug 1, 1930) shows the lakes were not always safe.
  • The odd detail: Capone's lake retreat now belongs to the Ojibwe nation whose reservation surrounds it.
  • People: Al Capone · Ralph Capone — "Bottles" · Joe Saltis — South Side beer boss · John "Dingbat" Oberta — Saltis's political ally, vanished 1930
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Couderay,_Wisconsin · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Capone · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Saltis · https://www.irs.gov/pub/foia/file-2-report-dated-12211933-in-re-alphonse-capone-by-sa-frank-wilson.pdf (Ralph's sentence, printed p. 27)
  • Confidence: High on ownership and the dates given; the Hideout's popular features (stone lodge, gun tower, floatplanes) are NOT verified this session. Ralph's conviction year (1930 in general accounts, 1932 in Wikipedia) is unsettled.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The northwoods are where city monsters go to rest. A Chicago gangster's lodge on Ojibwe land, reached by one road, is a perfect set-piece — and the Lac Courte Oreilles people had their own name for what came up from Chicago every summer.
