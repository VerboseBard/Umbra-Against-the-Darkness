R38 — The Illinois ring cities, 1920–1940: Rockford, Joliet, Aurora, Elgin, Waukegan, Kenosha/Racine, Peoria, Springfield, Rock Island–Moline, Danville

  • Lane: R38 — the cities around Chicago and their communities: who lived there, the churches and lodges, the crime and the rackets, the strange and the uncanny as the period press told it, notable 1930s events. Goes deeper on the cities than R29 (which covers the hinterland as places); does not repeat R29's Woodstock, Evanston, Elgin-watch, Peoria-whiskey or Springfield-riot entries except to add to them.
  • Date: 2026-09-30
  • Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (research agent)
  • Overview: Forty-two entries on ten cities in Chicago's ring, built mostly from local-history sites (Haunted Rockford, the Northwest Quarterly, SangamonLink, Peoria Historian, Racine's own timeline, the Quad Cities press) and cross-checked against Wikipedia where a page would load. The most useful pattern is that the ring cities are little Chicagos with their own cast: Rockford's Swedes (about 35,000 by 1930) and a Prohibition war between Tony Musso and the Giovingo brothers that killed four men in ten years; Rock Island's John Looney, whose blackmail newspaper and 150 dens ended with his son shot on a scoreboard night in 1922; Peoria's economy of vice (the first-ever gambling closure of 20 October 1930, and a gambler's wife shot in her own garden); Springfield's eighteen unsolved Prohibition murders and the Zito brothers, whose family ran both Springfield and Rockford; Racine's Dillinger robbery, the Case strike and the "Little Moscow" nickname, and Frank Lloyd Wright's Johnson Wax Great Workroom; Kenosha's Nash sit-down; Aurora's boxcar Mexican camp ("El Camp," gone in 1934); Joliet's Slovenian parish, dead steel works and limestone; Waukegan's asbestos plant and a Klan police chief; and Danville's 1903 lynching and old-soldiers' home. Several entries BEND hard: the Palmer Raid in Rockford (a Red Scare that has no Soviet Union to answer to), Camp Grant's 1918 flu camp (a war America never entered), "Little Moscow" and Nazi organizing in Racine, and the Mexican migrants of Aurora (under a US-occupied Mexico). Where a page would not load or a claim rests on a search-result summary, the entry says so; the ghost stories are all labelled legend.

Part 1 — Rockford (Winnebago County, ~90 miles west-northwest of the Loop)

Swede Town: Seventh Street, seventy blocks and the churches of a city-within-a-city — Rockford, 1930

  • Where: Rockford, Illinois — the east side, roughly Kishwaukee Street to 11th Street and State Street to 20th Avenue; the commercial spine is Seventh Street (Midtown District today).
  • What happened: By 1930 Rockford's Swedish population was about 35,000, counted in one account as the densest Swedish settlement in urban America; in 1900 first- and second-generation Swedes had already been some 40 percent of the town. The settlement covered nearly 70 blocks, and along Seventh Street and Kishwaukee Avenue Swedish was as common as English "as late as the 1920s." In 1910–20 that six-block stretch held seven Swedish churches, two Swedish banks and two concert halls. The nine Swedish congregations (organised 1854–1925) included First Lutheran (225 S. 1st St.), Bethany Methodist (8th St. and 3rd Ave.), Emmanuel Lutheran (3rd Ave. and 6th St.), Zion Lutheran, the Mission Covenant and the Evangelical Free church, and Tabor Lutheran, organised in 1925 and built in 1935 — a church dedicated in the middle of the Depression. The lodges: two Good Templar lodges, four Vikings lodges, three Vasa lodges; singing societies (Svea Sonor 1890, Lyran 1893) with halls at 326 Seventh St. (Svea Sonor's opera house; burned 1980) and 1115 Fourth Ave.; picnics at rural camps on the Kishwaukee River. Furniture, knitting mills and machine tools were the money; the Italians (arriving from 1878 and in numbers from the 1880s) lived in the southwest of town, each neighbourhood clustering by home province — Romans, Venetians and Sicilians on different streets — and teenage fist-fights between the two peoples at the downtown bridges over who was dating whom are a local memory (search-summary only; not on a page I read) [UNVERIFIED — V_R38].
  • The odd detail: In 1938 the town threw a New Sweden Tercentenary coffee party serving 10,000 cups, and Prince Bertil of Sweden visited that July and spoke to 6,000 people at the Swedish-American Hospital — a Swedish royal touring a Depression-era Illinois furniture town.
  • People: Per August Peterson — "King of the Swedes," ran Union Furniture and founded or led some 50 enterprises before dying in 1927; John Nelson — invented the automatic knitting machine (1869) behind the "Rockfords" work sock; Prince Bertil — Swedish royal, visited July 1938.
  • Sources: https://oldnorthwestterritory.northwestquarterly.com/2021/08/06/swede-town-usa/ ; https://www.notabletravels.com/rockford-illinois-swede-town/ ; https://www.gorockford.com/plan-your-trip/about/history/
  • Confidence: High for the Swedish figures (Northwest Quarterly; the 35,000 and 70 blocks agree across sources); Medium for the "Italian province" detail and the bridge fights (search summary of an unfetched page).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (The Swedish Socialists' 1917 draft refusal is treated in the next entry and does BEND.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A city where a whole language and its lodges (Vikings, Vasa, Good Templars) sit one street from the Italians and their home-province quarters is a ready-made cult versus population case: a lodge with a rite that is only spoken in Swedish. Rockford's furniture money makes it a plausible seat for a rich family with an inherited "collection."

The Palmer Raid in Rockford — 1 January 1920 (living memory)

  • Where: Rockford, Illinois — Swedish Socialist hall at 405 Seventh St. and the Swedish east side; trial at the federal court.
  • What happened: On New Year's Day 1920 federal agents arrested 186 mostly-Swedish socialists in Rockford on suspicion of subversion — the largest total among the 37 cities hit that night (another local-history page gives 140–180). Eleven were indicted; the case of Arthur Person went to trial with Clarence Darrow defending, and Person was acquitted, after which the other charges were dropped. The roots were in a Swedish socialist society formed around 1906 that ran a Saturday school teaching English, "humanistic values" and fine arts; in 1917 137 members refused to register for the draft and most drew a year in prison. Two of the society's men, Herman Hallstrom and C. Henry Bloom, later served five terms each as Rockford mayor.
  • The odd detail: The men federal agents arrested for radicalism went on to run the city — two of them for five terms apiece.
  • People: Arthur Person — tried, acquitted; Clarence Darrow — defence counsel; Herman Hallstrom and C. Henry Bloom — later five-term mayors.
  • Sources: https://oldnorthwestterritory.northwestquarterly.com/2021/08/06/swede-town-usa/ ; https://www.gorockford.com/plan-your-trip/about/history/
  • Confidence: Medium-High (one detailed page plus a second confirming the raid and a wider arrest range).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the Palmer Raids were a Red Scare response to Bolshevism and the 1917 draft; in the setting America never entered the Great War (no draft to refuse) and there is no Soviet Union or Red Scare. The event needs a substitute cause (a different Justice Department sweep — Golden Circle/Bureau targeting foreign-language lodges).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Keep the arrests and Darrow, change the cause: an FBI Section 13 (or Bureau) sweep of Swedish lodges in Rockford looking for something the lodges were hiding, with the socialists as the cover story. The 186 files still exist in a federal cabinet somewhere.

The Rockford beer-and-alcohol war: Musso, the Giovingos and four killings — 1923–1933

  • Where: Rockford, Illinois — South Main and Morgan Streets (Joe Giovingo, 1930); Romona Avenue (DeMarco, 1932); South Winnebago Street between Loomis and Montague (Paul Giovingo, 1933); the Giovingo house at 1033 Montague Road; Harding Street ("the center of Rockford gangland").
  • What happened: Rockford's early-1920s reputation for strict enforcement made it profitable: bootleggers from across the state came for the higher prices. The first gangland killing was Louis J. Milani, 8 October 1923, shot and dumped in a culvert near Montague Road (unsolved). Tom Perra vanished on 30 January 1928 after offering to inform, and was found shot near New Milford on 6 February. Antonio "Tony" Musso (Capone-connected, married into the Lombardo family) organised the bootlegging gangs c. 1927–28; Capone sent Nicolo Licata to help him. Joe Giovingo was killed on 14 August 1930 at Morgan and South Main by a shotgun from a big Dodge sedan — 17 wounds, killed within minutes; a funeral of over 1,500 with a 150-car procession. The folk story: Capone telephoned Joe's mother to apologise that the target had been Tony Abbott (Abbatini), a Capone associate hiding in Rockford who was in the car. Jack DeMarco came home from Leavenworth and was shot at his own welcome-home party on 20 January 1932 by three well-dressed men posing as police — never solved. Paul Giovingo was shot on 12 February 1933 on Winnebago Street: car forced to the kerb, ignition off, brake set, eight slugs in the left side, four to the head, after a warning letter; police met "a wall of silence." By 1933 Musso held the town's liquor traffic.
  • The odd detail: The Rockford gang's killers arrived dressed as police, and the murdered man's welcome-home party was under way in the next room.
  • People: Antonio "Tony" Musso — boss; Joe and Paul Giovingo — rival brothers, both shot; Jack DeMarco — Leavenworth returnee, shot; Tony Abbott (Abbatini) — reported Capone associate in Rockford; Nicolo Licata — Capone's envoy, later Los Angeles; Detectives Folke Bengston/Bengsten and Roy Johnson — on the scene in 1930.
  • Sources: https://www.hauntedrockford.com/prohibition-death-on-the-streets-1928-1933/ ; https://oldnorthwestterritory.northwestquarterly.com/2019/08/29/prohibition-the-noble-experiment-that-failed/ ; https://chicagoganghistory.com/suburbs/rockford
  • Confidence: High for dates and addresses (Haunted Rockford draws on the papers); Medium for the Capone-phone-call story (folk, one source). A fourth killing shown in one summary as 11 Feb 1933 is dated 12 Feb by the two page-reads; take 12 Feb.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. Capone is canon and already in the setting.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Musso is the man to whom Capone's Chicago Outfit (the Secret Service "knew what Capone was") subcontracts the northwest road. Four unsolved killings in a decade in a town of gospel Swedes: a GM's spine for a cult that pays the Outfit's bootleggers for silence about what is in a still-house in the culvert country.

The Freeport conspiracy trial — Rockford's 80 accused, January–February 1931

  • Where: Freeport, Illinois (Stephenson County, ~25 miles west of Rockford) — federal proceedings; the defendants from Rockford.
  • What happened: From 12 January to 2 February 1931 the federal government tried the Rockford liquor ring for conspiracy to violate the Prohibition Act: 80 men and one woman were accused, 59 went to trial, 36 were found guilty and 28 sentenced. Sentenced names include Tony Musso, Tony and Paul Giovingo, John Alto, Joe Baraconi, Joe Bendetto, Frank Rumore, Louis Verace, Alfred Falzone, Lorenzo Buttice and others. The federal task force was led by C. Edson Smith, deputy prohibition administrator in Chicago, with Secret Service and federal agents working the case in 1930 — including a June 1930 machine-gun volley from a curtained car at agents dismantling a still on Sanford Street (nobody hit; police suspected Dominick Rossi, 710 Sanford). Jack DeMarco, tried there, got 12 months of an 18-month sentence at Leavenworth and was murdered on the day after he came home.
  • The odd detail: Sentences went out to 28 of the men, but the ones who were shot afterwards — DeMarco and Paul Giovingo — were both returning convicts: the town killed its Leavenworth men when they came home.
  • People: C. Edson Smith — deputy prohibition administrator who led the task force; Tony Musso — convicted; Jack DeMarco — convicted, later shot; Dominick Rossi — Sanford Street suspect.
  • Sources: https://www.hauntedrockford.com/prohibition-death-on-the-streets-1928-1933/
  • Confidence: Medium-High (single detailed local page; verify counts in a federal court source).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (The Secret Service's role is in the setting by design.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A Secret Service agent on the Freeport task force who is really watching what the gang is protecting, not the liquor. The trial file has 28 names; two of them are dead within two years.

Camp Grant: the flu camp, the reserve camp and the CCC — Rockford, 1917–1935

  • Where: Camp Grant, south of Rockford, Illinois (Winnebago County).
  • What happened: Built in 1917 as a major infantry training camp (the 86th Division formed there), Camp Grant was hit by the 1918 influenza: by 3 October about 800 cases in a day, over 10,000 within a week, soldiers dying at 75–100 a day, over 1,000 dead between 23 September and 1 October by one count; the flu spread to Rockford, where 2,000 cases were reported in the first week. [UNVERIFIED — V_R38: the 800/10,000/75–100-a-day figures, the Rockford 2,000 cases and Hagadorn are not on Wikipedia, which confirms only "over 4,000" affected and "over 1,000" dead 23 Sept–1 Oct 1918.] A one-page account says the new commander, Col. Charles Hagadorn, moved men from tents into overcrowded barracks against medical advice. The Army closed the camp by December 1923; it passed to the Illinois National Guard from January 1924, served the Civilian Conservation Corps 1933–1935, and was reactivated in 1940–41.
  • The odd detail: The same fields that held a 1918 death-camp housed unemployed CCC boys fifteen years later, and a town-side account has the flu jumping from the camp to Rockford within a week, with 2,000 civilian cases reported.
  • People: Col. Charles Hagadorn — commander who reversed the tent order (per a genealogy-trails/AARC account).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_Grant_(Illinois) ; https://www.gorockford.com/plan-your-trip/about/history/ ; https://www.wikitree.com/wiki/Space:1919_Flu_Pandemic_in_Rockford (search summary only)
  • Confidence: Medium-High for the outline; the exact daily-death figures come from search summaries of the genealogy pages and the Wikipedia count (over 1,000 in nine days, 4,000 infected) is what I read directly.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — America never entered the Great War, so no 1917 conscript camp, no 86th Division and no camp flu; the 1924 Guard and 1933 CCC uses could still occur if the site exists for another reason (e.g. a pre-war cavalry or a Guard training ground; the setting's Southern War could have created it).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): In the setting, Camp Grant is a Southern-War muster ground that the Army closed after a different outbreak — a "flu" that the Secret Service and Section 13 both seal. The CCC boys who cleared the north field in 1934 dug something up.

Rockford's haunted map: Veterans Memorial Hall, Haskell Park and the Tinker house — legends with dates

  • Where: Rockford — Veterans Memorial Hall (built 1903, the first of its kind in Illinois); Haskell Park (the West Side Public Square platted in the 1830s); the Tinker Swiss Cottage on the bluff over Kent Creek (built 1865–70).
  • What happened: The town's ghost stories, as its own local-history sites tell them: Veterans Memorial Hall — legend says a woman in a long gown appears on the stairs, said to be Mrs. Damon (or "Della" [UNVERIFIED — V_R38]), mother of Grant Damon, a soldier who died of mustard-gas injuries in 1918; a guard heard a voice from the basement say "I'm Ella." Haskell Park — a June 1902 report of a shadowy figure rising from the fountain, blamed on James French (who, per the page read, shot his estranged wife near the park in 1896; "hanged" is not on that page) ⟨fixed: V_R38⟩ or on an earlier Native burial ground. Tinker Swiss Cottage — built by Robert Hall Tinker and Mary (widow of John Manny of the Manny Reaper Company); Robert Tinker died in 1926; the couple's niece Jesse (who married and adopted a son, Theodore) died in 1942 and gave the house and its furnishings to the Rockford Park District as a museum; legend says a woman in white, a servant girl in a blue dress on the stairs and a voice saying "Get out."
  • The odd detail: The Rockford Veterans Hall's ghost is a mustard-gas mother — a Great War story in a setting where America sent no doughboys.
  • People: Robert and Mary Tinker — the house's owners (Mary d. 1901, Robert d. 1926); Jesse (their niece) — donor, d. 1942; Grant Damon — soldier said to have died of mustard gas in 1918 (legend only).
  • Sources: https://www.gorockford.com/blog/stories/post/haunted-history-of-rockford/ ; https://www.hauntedrockford.com/the-tinker-family-ghosts/
  • Confidence: High for the buildings and Tinker dates; all ghost material is folklore ("legend says") with no documentary confirmation. I read no source for the Coronado Theatre (built 1927) ghost — see Leads.
  • Divergence check: BENDS for the Damon story (a WWI mustard-gas death cannot occur as told); the rest holds. Tinker house, hall and park are pre-1916 fixtures.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Rockford's Veterans Hall ghost could be a real haunt whose legend has been edited by the 1916 break — the Grant Damon story is a rewritten local myth, and the GM decides who edited it.

Part 2 — Rock Island and Moline (the Quad Cities, ~165 miles west; the Mississippi)

John Looney's Rock Island: the blackmail newspaper and 150 dens — 1905–1922 (living memory)

  • Where: Rock Island, Illinois — the Mirror Lounge (Looney's law office and newspaper plant); Bel Air, Looney's second house, 2824 River Heights Road (built 1905, on the bluffs above the Rock River); the city's vice district.
  • What happened: John Patrick Looney (1865–1942), born in Ottawa, Illinois, admitted to the bar in 1889, lost a legislative race and blamed the Rock Island Argus. He founded the Rock Island News as a blackmail sheet: he bought the Mirror Lounge in 1905, put a law office on the ground floor and the paper upstairs [UNVERIFIED — V_R38: Wikipedia names Drost as running "the Mirror Saloon"; the 1905 purchase and the layout are not on a page read], and prominent citizens paid "kill fees" to keep their secrets out of print. By 1921 he controlled some 150 gambling dens and brothels, and his method included photographing men with his prostitutes. Bel Air had three storeys with three tiers of porches, a third-floor ballroom and an alleged 100-foot tunnel to a stone barn; a 1908 attack on the property, which Looney blamed on rival W. W. Wilmerton, started a private feud in downtown Rock Island. The house fell to its mortgage holder once he went to prison; today it has lost its third floor and porches.
  • The odd detail: The paper's editor, Daniel Drost, said in later life that he could neither read nor write — he edited the blackmail sheet and kept it running while claiming illiteracy.
  • People: John P. Looney — boss, lawyer, publisher; Daniel Drost — editor of the News; W. W. Wilmerton — rival named in the 1908 attack; Connor Looney — his son (next entry).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Patrick_Looney ; https://www.rigov.org/955/Bel-Air-John-P-Looneys-Second-House ; https://www.rcreader.com/art/buried-stories-daniel-drost
  • Confidence: High for the outline (three pages agree); Medium for the tunnel ("alleged" per the city's own page).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (pre-repeal Prohibition-era vice).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A newspaper whose business is knowing what respectable people did, and a bluff house with a tunnel: the natural front for a cult's intelligence arm. Looney's photographs of prominent men would be the kind of "kompromat" the Golden Circle trades in.

Market Square, 6 October 1922: the World Series ambush that killed Connor Looney

  • Where: Downtown Rock Island — the Market Square / Argus newspaper scoreboard block (accounts reconcile: the scoreboard was in the front window of the Argus; Looney's car sat in Market Square and he ran to the nearby Sherman Hotel and returned fire ⟨fixed: V_R38⟩); Chippiannock Cemetery, 12th St. and 31st Ave., for the graves.
  • What happened: On 31 July 1922 William Gabel, who held cancelled cheques endorsed by Looney, was murdered; a gang war followed in which 12 people died. On 6 October 1922, while the crowd watched the World Series report on the scoreboard, Looney's one-time lieutenant Daniel Drost and another ex-lieutenant, Anthony Billburg, with two other men, ambushed Looney, his son and a bodyguard; Connor Looney, 21, drew a gun and turned on the attackers and was shot dead in the car ⟨fixed: V_R38 — "dying the next day" is on no page read; Wikipedia and the River Cities' Reader say he was killed in the ambush⟩. By 26 October every Looney saloon and brothel was shut, six stills smashed, an arms cache seized at one of his homes, and the News ceased [UNVERIFIED — V_R38: the 26 October sweep, the six stills and the arms cache are not on a page read]. Looney fled and was arrested in Belen, New Mexico, on 28 November 1923 (recognised from a wanted poster at the post office; Wikipedia's "November 1924" is wrong) ⟨fixed: V_R38⟩; he was convicted in December 1925 of the Gabel murder (and, per other accounts, of conspiracy), given 14 years and served about 8½, released 7 April 1934 at 68 [UNVERIFIED — V_R38: release date from a Daily Herald search summary only; the Rock Island city page says "nine years"]. He died in 1942 in an El Paso, Texas, tuberculosis sanitarium. Drost served the same 8½ years at Joliet, then lived on a retirement grant doing menial jobs in a downtown Rock Island rooming house; he died in 1939 and is buried in Chippiannock Cemetery (North Public Lot 22, Grave 6) in an unmarked grave near Connor Looney's unmarked one.
  • The odd detail: The betrayer and the man he shot at lie a few paces apart in the same cemetery, both in unmarked graves — and Drost had paid for Looney's daughter Kathleen to enter a St. Louis convent, an act Looney never forgave [UNVERIFIED — V_R38: the convent payment is on the River Cities' Reader page; "never forgave" is not].
  • People: John Looney — boss (convicted 1925); Connor Looney — son, killed at 21; Daniel Drost — editor, ambusher, d. 1939; Anthony Billburg — ex-lieutenant who joined the ambush; William Gabel — murdered 31 Jul 1922; Kathleen Looney — daughter sent to a St. Louis convent.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Patrick_Looney ; https://www.rcreader.com/art/buried-stories-daniel-drost ; https://quadcities.com/articles/entertainment/gangsters-ghosts-come-to-life-in-rock-island (walking-tour page; ghost of the old YMCA building — legend) ; https://news-bulletin.com/features/recognizing-evil-how-a-citizen-of-belen-helped-capture-a-gangster-from-illinois/article_a8149780-13cf-595d-bc5d-7a9e68c35523.html (arrest, added by V_R38)
  • Confidence: High for Gabel, Connor and Drost; Shooting site and arrest date now High (Market Square; scoreboard in the Argus window; Looney ran to the Sherman Hotel; arrested 28 Nov 1923) ⟨fixed: V_R38⟩; release date Medium.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (The Looney story inspired the film Road to Perdition — not relevant in-world.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A vice boss's son dies on the day the whole city stands watching a scoreboard — a ritual-shaped public killing. The old Looney gang's survivors are Joliet ex-convicts in 1930: Drost lived till 1939 and "could not read" — a witness who knew where the blackmail files went.

The Black Angel of Riverside Cemetery — Moline (legend; the statue removed later)

  • Where: Riverside Cemetery, Moline, Illinois — the Deere family tomb, on the bluff's northeast corner.
  • What happened: A stone angel at the Deere tomb became the town's bravery test: teenagers ran through the cemetery at the stroke of midnight and touched it. Legend says it was a white marble statue that turned black after a lightning strike (a second story says a Black child was chained to it by tormentors and lightning struck in judgment); other tales have a young man die of a heart attack on touching it and a young woman found unconscious with her hair gone white after a night in the cemetery. The statue was later removed to stop vandalism; one account says it went to a retreat centre in New Mexico, a reader's comment says the Moline fire department kept it under a cover.
  • The odd detail: The Deere family's own angel is the local ghost — the plough king's monument is Moline's haunt.
  • People: the Deere family — owners of the tomb (Deere & Company, Moline's principal employer).
  • Sources: http://molinememories.blogspot.com/2010/01/black-angel-riverside-cemetary.html ; https://qctimes.com/news/local/ask-the-times/ask-the-times-moline-cemetery-s-black-angel-now-in/article_998ca36d-9991-5d20-b797-df7eddf9228e.html (search summary only)
  • Confidence: High that the statue and legend exist; the legend itself has no documentation; the removal date is not on a page I read (UNVERIFIED).
  • Divergence check: holds — the legend is undated and independent of the war.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A rich family's monument that "turns black" is an ordinary Umbra ghost: the Kabbalahs have a rite for angel images; the IHS treat the "black angel" as a warding statue that did its work. The "test of bravery" ritual is the cult-versus-population lens.

Moline: Belgians, Swedes, Deere and the coining of "the Quad Cities" — 1900–1930s

  • Where: Moline, Illinois — the southwestern bluffs (Belgian settlement), Deere & Company, Velie Motors; the "Quad Cities" (Moline, Rock Island, East Moline and Davenport, Iowa).
  • What happened: Moline's ethnic mix in 1900–1940: Swedes kept coming to work at Deere & Company; Belgians settled the southwest bluffs to work in the young auto trade (Velie Motors, founded by a Deere relative), and for a time Moline had the second-largest Belgian population in the United States after Detroit. Electricity had come in 1881 when Deere lit the streets. In 1929 Moline passed a zoning ordinance that made it the first Illinois city outside the Chicago area to have one. A search summary of the Wikipedia article says the term "Quad Cities" came into vogue in the 1930s when East Moline was given equal status alongside Davenport, Rock Island and Moline ⟨fixed: V_R38 — confirmed on https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quad_Cities: "during the 1930s the term 'Quad Cities' came into vogue, as East Moline was given 'equal status'"⟩.
  • The odd detail: Belgium's second-biggest American colony lived among the plough works on a Mississippi bluff — and in 1914 its homeland was the first thing the German army overran.
