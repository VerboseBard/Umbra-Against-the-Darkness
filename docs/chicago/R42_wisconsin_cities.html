R42 — Wisconsin beyond Milwaukee, 1920–1940: the cities and their communities

  • Lane: R42 — Madison, Green Bay and the Door peninsula, Sheboygan and Kohler, Manitowoc and Two Rivers, Fond du Lac, Oshkosh, Appleton, Wausau, La Crosse; the Ho-Chunk, Menominee and Oneida nations; the German, Norwegian and Polish farm communities; Wisconsin's crimes, disasters, disappearances and period ghost stories (labelled). Does not repeat R27 (Milwaukee), R38 Part 4 (Racine/Kenosha), R39 (Northwoods), R07/R22/R30 — cross-references are named in each entry.
  • Date: 2026-09-30
  • Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (research agent)
  • Overview: Forty-one entries on the ordinary cities and communities of Wisconsin outside Milwaukee, built from Wikipedia, tribal-museum and state-historical-society pages and (for a share of the facts, each flagged) search-result summaries — the web-search budget ran out mid-run, so several entries rest on summaries of pages I could not open and say so. What the state offers Umbra is less a gangster ring than a belt of institutions: the state asylum at Oshkosh whose one 1934 death emptied three institutions' leadership; the asylum at Madison sitting under a 624-foot thunderbird mound; the Klan's own campus fraternity at the university of the Progressives; the state's folklorist (Charles E. Brown) issuing pamphlets titled Ghost Tales (1931) and Sea Serpents (1942) while the WPA gathered 1,000 stories from Wisconsin doors. The best odd finds are a Chicago Cabinet member's "haunted" summer house whose ghost story turns out to have begun in a 1979 book (Summerwind), the would-be assassin of Theodore Roosevelt who said McKinley's ghost sent him and who died on the anniversary of his first dream, a 1926 Ho-Chunk "autobiography" that the anthropologist re-wrote, and an Appleton ordinance that sent Percy Julian to Chicago in 1936. The divergence checks matter more here than anywhere else: German Wisconsin is where the setting's premise bites hardest (no 1917–19 suppression; German-language papers may still be printing), the Belgian shrine at Champion sits on kin under German dominion, La Follette Sr.'s war vote never happens, and Madison's radio station 9XM is never shut down in April 1917. Anything that touches 1929–39 domestic events (Kohler 1934, the Progressive Party, Groves Act, the Packers) simply holds. The Native-nation entries are written from published museum and encyclopedia histories; no Native person was consulted and the hooks say so.

Part A — Politics, labour and the company town

The Kohler strike from inside the village — the model town, the coal car, and two dead on the north side — 22 June – 31 July 1934 (deepens R27 and R36; also see R03 lead)

  • Where: Village of Kohler (incorporated 1912; first called Riverside), just west of Sheboygan, Sheboygan County; the fatal clash was on the village's north side about 9 p.m. on 27 July. (Company address today: 444 Highland Dr, Kohler.)
  • What happened: R27/R36 already give the skeleton (14-point union demand 22 June; indefinite shutdown 26 June; two killed 27 July; Guard arrives 29 July). What this entry adds is the place and the aftermath. The village was a deliberate garden-suburb: Walter J. Kohler Sr. toured European industrial communities in 1913 and consulted Sir Ebenezer Howard; the Olmsted Brothers laid out the plan from 1917; the population went from 403 (1920) to 1,748 (1930); the New York Times praised it in 1931 as a factory "without a visible ash heap." On the evening of 27 July, company special deputies broke through the pickets to escort a coal car the strikers had turned back. Lee Wakefield, 25, took a rifle bullet through the chest (he worked at the Leverenz Shoe Company; his family says he was an innocent spectator), and Henry Engelmann, 27, was shot through the back and abdomen; more than 40 (one count: 47) were hurt by gunfire, gas or blows. Sheriff Ernest Zehms declared it beyond local control and the 105th Cavalry was called in. Engelmann's funeral was 30 July; Wakefield's was held outdoors at Moose Park on 31 July before thousands. The inquests in September — seven doctors testifying — concluded that no one person could be blamed for either death. An NLRB-ordered election in September went to the company union.
  • The odd detail: The killing ground was a planned village whose company kept "design authority" over residents' home improvements, and the official finding was that the deaths had no author. A WebSearch summary of the Sheboygan Press timeline also names Gov. Albert G. Schmedeman as the man who called out the Guard (not opened). Sources disagree on when the strike itself began (Clio says 17 July; the strike is counted as day 11 on 27 July) and on the first dead man's name (Harry vs. Henry Engelmann).
  • People: Walter J. Kohler Sr. — owner and ex-governor; Lee Wakefield (25) and Henry/Harry Engelmann (27) — killed; Ernest Zehms — Sheboygan County sheriff; Federal Labor Union No. 18545 — the AFL local (chartered June 1933).
  • Sources: https://www.yahoo.com/news/kohler-strike-claimed-lives-two-100801664.html (read; the funerals, inquests, Sheriff Zehms, 105th Cavalry) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohler,_Wisconsin (read) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohler_strikes (read) ; https://theclio.com/entry/90588 (read)
  • Confidence: High on the two dead, the date, the Guard and the village's design history; Medium on "47 hurt" vs. "more than 40" and the strike's start date; the end date is disputed (Wikipedia: company union until 1952; the Sheboygan article: strike ran to 1941).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. The New Masses-style charge of "communistic affiliations" BENDS (no Soviet Union; see R27). The 1941 settlement "to pursue WWII government contracts" is post-window and would differ in a world where the US never enters the Great War.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A planned village where the company owns the look of every house and no one is to blame for a killing. What is under the Olmsted plan? An investigator arriving in August 1934 finds a town of 1,700 that will not name a shooter.

The Progressive Party is born in Fond du Lac — 19 May 1934 (Madison and the La Follettes)

  • Where: Fond du Lac (Fond du Lac County, at the south end of Lake Winnebago); the party's home base was Madison. Venue not confirmed — a search result surfaced the Hotel Retlaw (built 1923) as the city's big hotel but no page read ties it to the convention.
  • What happened: On 19 May 1934 a convention at Fond du Lac founded the Wisconsin Progressive Party out of the progressive wing of the state Republican Party, with William T. Evjue (Madison's Capital Times editor) as chairman, led by the brothers Philip La Follette (governor 1931–33, back in office 1935–39) and Sen. Robert M. La Follette Jr. It allied radical farm and labour groups; party colour was green. The 1934 result: Philip elected governor with 39.12%, 11 of 33 state senate seats and 45 of 100 assembly seats; in 1936 he was re-elected with 46.38% and the party became the largest bloc in both houses (16 of 33 senate, 46 of 100 assembly — a plurality, not a majority) ⟨fixed: V_R42⟩. Bob Jr. was re-elected to the U.S. Senate as a Progressive in 1934 with 47.78%. It collapsed in 1938 to a united Democratic-Republican front and Julius P. Heil; R27 has Philip's follow-up, the circled-cross National Progressives of America (Madison, 28 April 1938). Under Philip the state also passed the first unemployment-compensation law in the country (Groves Act, 1932; in force 1934).
  • The odd detail: Wisconsin's third party won the governor's office twice (1934, 1936) and became the largest bloc in the legislature ⟨fixed: V_R42⟩ inside a single decade, in the same years its founder's sons were being called fascists by their enemies.
  • People: Philip La Follette — governor; Robert M. La Follette Jr. — U.S. senator; William T. Evjue — convention chairman, editor of the Madison Capital Times; Julius P. Heil — the 1938 winner.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin_Progressive_Party (read) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_La_Follette (read) ; https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/dictionaries-thesauruses-pictures-and-press-releases/progressive-party-wisconsin (search result only)
  • Confidence: High on date/results (both Wikipedia pages agree); venue UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Robert La Follette Sr.'s defining act was voting against U.S. entry into the Great War in April 1917 and surviving a Senate censure attempt (well-known; not re-verified on a page read this session); with America never entering, that fight does not exist, and the Progressive brand is a peace-and-farm brand without the war martyrdom. His 1924 Progressive run (a search result confirms he ran under the Progressive banner) would face President Leonard Wood in the setting — check the 1924 election. Philip's WWI army service (from memory, unverified here) would also bend.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A third party with a symbol, a convention and a Madison newspaper is a perfect cover organisation. Who funds a party that can carry a legislature — the Golden Circle's "Germany as patron of other people's revolutions" turned on Wisconsin's own?

Percy Julian and the town that would not put him up — Appleton, 1936

  • Where: Appleton (Outagamie County, Fox River); the Institute of Paper Chemistry (founded 1929 per the ACS listing; another Wikipedia page says 1936); the man ended up at the Glidden Company's soya-products laboratory in Chicago.
  • What happened: Percy Lavon Julian, the Black organic chemist (DePauw), was about to be hired by the Institute of Paper Chemistry in Appleton when the Institute discovered Appleton was a sundown town — Wikipedia (citing James Loewen's Sundown Towns) gives the rule as "No Negro should be a bed or boarded overnight in Appleton." The hire fell through. Julian then wrote to Glidden asking for soybean-oil samples; W. J. O'Brien, a Glidden vice-president, offered him the directorship of research at Glidden's Soya Products Division in Chicago. He arrived in 1936, supervised assembly of Glidden's newly bought German extraction plant, and designed the first plant for industrial-grade isolated soy protein. Search-result summaries add that the Glidden VP sat on the Institute's board (not confirmed on a page read). His Oak Park house was firebombed on Thanksgiving 1950 and dynamited on 12 June 1951.
  • The odd detail: A Wisconsin paper town's overnight rule sent one of the century's great chemists to Chicago — and the machine he assembled there was German.
  • People: Percy L. Julian — chemist; W. J. O'Brien — Glidden vice-president.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Lavon_Julian (read) ; https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/julian-the-trailblazer/ and https://chicagoreader.com/columns-opinion/percy-julian-chemist-and-catalyst/ (search-result summaries only; the pages themselves did not load)
  • Confidence: Medium-High (one well-cited Wikipedia line plus consistent search summaries; the "law on the books" wording is Loewen's, and the exact ordinance is UNVERIFIED).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the period sorted people by national origin and religion, and by colour). The German plant in Chicago is a small BENDS: in the setting Germany won and German industrial equipment flows west on better terms.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A Black investigator (or a Kabbalah consultant, or an IHS lay brother) cannot sleep in the town where the case is. The team drives out to Neenah or Green Bay each night — and the thing in Appleton knows the schedule.

Houdini's Appleton — the rabbi's son, 1878–1882, and the Halloween of 1926 (living memory)

  • Where: Appleton, Wisconsin — the family house stood on Appleton Street, the site now called Houdini Plaza; the Outagamie Museum holds a large part of the Houdini collection lent by Sidney Radner (auctioned 2004).
  • What happened: The Weisz family arrived in the United States on 3 July 1878 aboard the SS Frisia and settled in Appleton, where the father, Rabbi Mayer Samuel Weisz, served the Zion Reform Jewish Congregation. In 1882 he lost the post and the family moved to Milwaukee. Houdini died on 31 October 1926 — Halloween — at Grace Hospital, Detroit, of peritonitis, after performing at the Garrick Theatre on 24 October with a 104°F fever, and a week after being punched in the abdomen. (R05, R13 and R32 carry the séance-debunker and Margery material; not repeated.)
  • The odd detail: The man who is often said to have claimed Appleton as his birthplace (a commonly repeated claim; not on a page I read) had in fact arrived there in July 1878 as a small immigrant child (he was born in 1874, from memory) — and the family left because the rabbi lost his job.
  • People: Rabbi Mayer Samuel Weisz — Houdini's father; Harry Houdini (Erik Weisz) — escape artist; Sidney Radner — collector.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Houdini (read) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appleton,_Wisconsin (read)
  • Confidence: High on Appleton dates, the 1882 move and the death details; the birthplace claim is a legend.
  • Divergence check: holds — pre-1916 (the Appleton years) and the death is 1926 in a world where America is at peace and Wood is president; nothing bends.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): For a 1930s Appleton native the town's most famous son died on Halloween ten years ago; a lodge or séance circle still meets in his name. The people who never let him rest are not the spiritualists.

Part B — Farms, land and the driftless country

Coon Valley, Vernon County — the first soil-erosion demonstration project in the United States — 1933–1937

  • Where: Coon Creek watershed, Vernon County, southwest Wisconsin (the "Driftless Area"); the villages of Coon Valley and Westby.
  • What happened: Set up in the winter of 1933–34 by the new federal Soil Erosion Service under Hugh Hammond Bennett, Coon Valley was the first of twenty national watershed demonstration projects [UNVERIFIED — V_R42: the pages read call it only "the first" demonstration project; a search result gives 10 sites by Jan 1934 and ~24 in all] (a search summary gives the basin as 92,589 acres [UNVERIFIED — V_R42; a basin that size would make 43,000 acres less than "more than half"]). Aldo Leopold lobbied the Roosevelt administration to send the CCC and wrote the essay describing the valley as one of a thousand communities that had abused rich soil and helped make the Mississippi flood problem. Farmers had to enrol in five-year cooperative farm plans; most joined, and many who did not adopted the practices anyway. When the CCC left in 1937, over 43,000 acres — described as "more than half" (of the farmland, presumably: the basin figure above is larger) — was under management; farm income of participants rose about 25% (1934–42); by 1974 erosion was down 75% and sedimentation 98%. The CCC built terraces and a 1936 dam.
  • The odd detail: The first national soil-conservation project in America was one Wisconsin valley of steep hillsides, run by federal men who signed the farmers to five-year plans and had the CCC dig the terraces.
  • People: Hugh Hammond Bennett — first Soil Conservation chief; Aldo Leopold — technical adviser and essayist; CCC enrollees.
  • Sources: https://www.aldoleopold.org/blogs/lessons-from-coon-valley-the-importance-of-collaboration-in-watershed-management (read) ; https://www.nacdnet.org/2023/09/11/wisconsins-coon-creek-watershed-project-celebrates-90-year-anniversary/ (read)
  • Confidence: High on the project's existence and results; Medium on the start (the NACD page says 1933, the Leopold Foundation page says the CCC arrived in 1934); the Norwegian character of Westby/Coon Valley is well known but neither page states it (the NACD page names the Norskedalen heritage centre).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (New Deal soil conservation follows the Dust Bowl; the setting's own agricultural collapse is caused differently, so the reason might be tariff-driven farm bankruptcy instead).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Government men arrive in a Norwegian valley telling old farmers what to do with their hillsides; a CCC crew digs terraces into ground no glacier ever touched. What did the terrace-diggers find?

The Fromm brothers of Hamburg — ginseng and 36,000 silver foxes, Marathon County — 1901–1937 (near Wausau)

  • Where: Town of Hamburg, Marathon County (about 15 miles from Wausau); the farm is now a National Register site (2013).
  • What happened: Four brothers — Walter, Edward, John and Henry Fromm — read in 1901 that silver-fox pelts sold for $1,200 in London and formed "the Company." In 1904 they began growing ginseng, learning it from a neighbour, Reinhold Dietsch; in 1913 they raised their first silver-fox pup and carved a marker on Rib Mountain. In 1915 half an acre of ginseng brought $3,500 and they mortgaged the family farm for $6,500 to buy three breeding foxes; by 1919 ginseng sold $40,000 a year [UNVERIFIED — V_R42: the Wikipedia article read gives 3 acres of ginseng by 1915 and $45,000–$115,000 a year through the 1920s, not the $3,500 or $40,000 figures]. By 1929 they were the world's largest producers of both. A 13-row semi-automated ginseng planter was their own design; they funded a University of Minnesota vet (Dr. Robert G. Green) whose work produced fox encephalitis and distemper vaccines. In 1935 they held fur auctions at their own farm instead of sending pelts to New York, London or Paris; at the height of the Depression (1937) the firm did about $10 million of business with 180+ employees, 500 acres of ginseng and 36,000 silver foxes (search-result summary of the Wikipedia/Wisconsin 101 text) [UNVERIFIED — V_R42: not on the Wikipedia page; wi101 page unreachable]. They sat on their ginseng reserve through the Depression.
  • The odd detail: Four farm brothers in the Wisconsin hills ran the world's largest silver fox herd and its largest ginseng garden, and the price of one had been set in London.
  • People: Walter, Edward, John, Henry Fromm — the brothers; Reinhold Dietsch — the neighbour who taught them ginseng; Dr. Robert G. Green — vaccine researcher.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fromm_Brothers_Fur_and_Ginseng_Farm (read) ; https://wi101.wisc.edu/the-fromm-fur-farm/ (search result only)
  • Confidence: High on the 1901–1929 sequence; Medium on the 1937 figures (search summary).
  • Divergence check: holds — pre-1916 (start); fur trade BENDS lightly: in a Germany-won world Leipzig, not London or Paris, would set the price of silver fox.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A farm family with thousands of caged silver animals, a walled ginseng garden under shade lath, and an "outsider" buyer who wants a vaccine. Werewolves do not get distemper — do they?

The Gorman–Horrigan farm killings — Crawford County, Tuesday 11 November 1930

  • Where: The Horrigan farm about 15–17 miles northeast of Boscobel, Crawford County (Kickapoo hills, western Wisconsin), a hollow locals called "Treasure Hollow."
  • What happened: At dinner time three men and a woman posing as hunters asked leave to hunt. One asked for water; when the woman of the house brought it, "the big man" grabbed her and drew a gun and ordered everyone to face the wall. A shoot-out followed: Patrick Gorman and his son Paul, 22 (visiting from Canton, South Dakota) were killed at the table; Stephen Horrigan (died later, per one summary) and Cornelius Horrigan, about 60, were wounded; the household's return fire wounded at least one bandit. The bandits' 1928 Chevrolet sedan turned up abandoned at Mount Sterling; posses formed at Soldiers Grove and sheriffs of four counties (Crawford, Grant, Richland and Vernon) worked the case, with vigilantes guarding roads. Nobody was ever arrested. Locals had long said an Irish immigrant, John Horrigan, had buried gold on the place; people were seen digging afterward and nothing was found.
  • The odd detail: The robbery may have been aimed at buried money that a family legend put under the very farm the family was born on.
  • People: Patrick Gorman and Paul Gorman — killed; Cornelius and Stephen Horrigan — the farm's owners (Stephen reported dying afterwards); the four "hunters," never identified.
  • Sources: https://www.telegraphherald.com/news/tri-state/article_ca3a82ea-8954-11e5-8db9-f798141cc930.html (read twice; the summaries disagree on ages — Patrick Gorman is given as 36, 56 and 79 across readings, and find-a-grave-type pages give 1865–1930 — treat as UNVERIFIED)
  • Confidence: Medium on the event; Low on the ages and on exactly who died beyond the two Gormans.
  • Divergence check: BENDS in a small way — 11 November 1930 is the real Armistice Day; in the setting America never entered the war, so the US date would have no such holiday. Otherwise holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A four-person crew with a woman as the door-opener, a hollow with a treasure name, a two-year silence. What was actually buried on the Horrigan farm — and did the family leave because they found it?

Part C — Madison, the university and the mounds

Madison's mounds — a 624-foot thunderbird on the asylum hill, and the man who fought to keep them — 1908–1944 (Charles E. Brown)

  • Where: Madison, Dane County (Four Lakes region): the Mendota State Hospital Mound Group; also mounds in Forest Hill Cemetery, Vilas Park, Picnic Point and Observatory Hill.
  • What happened: Wisconsin is the country's heartland of Native effigy mounds — earthen birds, bears, panthers and long lines built centuries before the settlers. In Madison, the Mendota hospital grounds hold a bird effigy with a 624-foot wingspan on a steep slope above a wetland. Charles E. Brown, director of the Wisconsin Historical Society museum 1908–1944 and founder-editor of the Wisconsin Archeological Society, led the fight that produced Wisconsin's first historic-preservation law; a state-funded survey then mapped hundreds of mounds within two years. Losses continued: Observatory Hill mounds damaged by sidewalk construction, Picnic Point's forms ploughed out, mounds at Mendota lost to a road and hospital construction; Brown and volunteers excavated a Madison-area mound in 1931 just before development destroyed it [UNVERIFIED — V_R42], and Brown restored the Picnic Point mounds in 1939. He is buried at Forest Hill Cemetery (Section 30), among mounds.
  • The odd detail: The state built its madhouse on top of a giant thunderbird, and the man who saved the ones that survived is buried among them.
  • People: Charles E. Brown — state museum director and archaeologist; the Wisconsin Archeological Society.
  • Sources: http://foresthill.williamcronon.net/effigy-mounds/mounds-in-madison/ (read) ; https://www.wpr.org/news/archaeologists-wisconsin-ancient-effigy-mounds-new-book (search result) ; https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=307448 (search result)
  • Confidence: High on Brown and the Mendota bird; Medium on "first preservation law" (search summary — the date is not stated on a page read).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (nothing here touches 1916).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): An asylum on a thunderbird. The patients say the hill pulls; the doctors chart it as delusion. Who put the mound up first — and does the State Hospital's plan match the bird's?

The ghost tales, the sea serpents and the lost treasure — Charles E. Brown's Wisconsin Folklore pamphlets and the WPA collectors — 1931, 1936–38, 1942, 1945 (FOLKLORE, labelled)

  • Where: All of Wisconsin; the collector sat in the State Historical Society building, Madison. Sightings are from the Four Lakes, Lake Geneva, Elkhart, Red Cedar and Koshkonong lakes.
  • What happened: From 1921 to 1945 Brown issued 47 short pamphlets (444 pages in all) of Wisconsin folklore, most only four to eight pages, mostly without citing sources. Titles include Ghost Tales (1931 — "ghosts, spooks, phantoms, spirits, skulls, skeletons, ghouls, hexes, undertakers and burying grounds; short stories for use at the fireside and camp fire"), The Ridgeway Ghost (1943), Sea Serpents: Wisconsin Occurrences of These Weird Water Monsters (1942), Lost Treasure Tales (1945; "Lake Mendota chests, Wisconsin River coin, jug of gold dust, Potawatomi cache, Clarno gold hoard"), Hermits (1945). Separately, in 1936–38 the WPA Federal Writers' Project (headed in Wisconsin at first by Brown, then run by Dorothy Miller from December 1935) gathered 600+ interviews — about 1,000 stories, 3,000 songs, 9,000 sayings — including Ho-Chunk and Ojibwe accounts. Recorded lake-monster sightings: 12 June 1897 on Lake Monona (Eugene Heath, Mr. Schott and his two sons saw a creature "roughly 20 feet long" shaped like the bottom of a boat; Heath fired two shots and it came toward them — Wisconsin State Journal, "Sea Serpent Appears Early This Year"); 1917 on Lake Mendota (a fish-scale-like object at Picnic Point; a fisherman saw a snake-like head with blazing eyes; a napping girl felt something tickle her foot); and on Geneva Lake the Janesville Gazette of 29 September 1902 reported a snake-like creature 65 feet long and 8–10 inches thick. [UNVERIFIED — V_R42]
  • The odd detail: The state's own museum director spent the Depression issuing four-page booklets on fireside ghosts and lake serpents — and the Federal Writers' Project paid people to knock on doors to ask what kind.
  • People: Charles E. Brown — folklorist and museum director; Dorothy Miller — FWP folklore director; Eugene Heath — Monona sighting, 1897.
  • Sources: https://legacy.wisconsinhistory.org/turningpoints/search.asp?id=1622 (read) ; https://legacy.wisconsinhistory.org/Records/Article/CS324 (read) ; https://edgeeffects.net/lake-monster/ (read) ; https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/book/lookupname?key=Brown%2C+Charles+E.+%28Charles+Edward%29%2C+1872-1946 (read)
  • Confidence: High on the pamphlets, dates and the WPA project; sightings are period press as reported (one is a 2010s article citing the State Journal); the Geneva Lake 1902 item comes from a search-result summary.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. The WPA (a New Deal agency) exists in the setting because Roosevelt burns the surplus and puts the country to work.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): An archive of local ghosts assembled by a museum man and a federal typist in 1936: the "cases" already exist as file cards. An investigator uses Brown's Ghost Tales as a gazetteer — and finds the first card that Brown did not print.

Part D — Oshkosh, Fond du Lac, Appleton, Wausau: the Fox and Wisconsin river cities

Winnebago State Hospital and the death of Oscar Schrader — Oshkosh, January 1934 (inquiry February–July 1934)

  • Where: Winnebago State Hospital (then also called the Northern Hospital for the Insane), just north of Oshkosh, Winnebago County; a Kirkbride-plan state asylum.
  • What happened: The hospital took its first patient in 1873; its main building was finished on 11 November 1875 with 500 beds. By 1932 it held 864 patients and 164 staff on an official capacity of 727, and it had been renamed Winnebago State Hospital sometime in the 1930s. A cemetery on the grounds ran from 1872 to 1973. In January 1934 a patient, Oscar Schrader, died at the hospital; the death set off a legislative inquiry that ran from February to July 1934 and spread from Winnebago to Mendota (Madison) and to the state prison at Waupun, ending in about 30 dismissals of staff and officials. The Ho-Chunk between the wars — eighty-acre homesteads, an "itinerant economy" and the peyote road — Black River Falls, Wittenberg and the Dells, 1874–1930s

  • Where: Jackson County (Black River Falls), Shawano County (Wittenberg), the Wisconsin Dells region, and the government boarding school at Tomah.
  • What happened: After the Homestead Act was extended to Indians in 1874, the Ho-Chunk who had returned to their homeland could claim homesteads of up to 80 acres and were given separate tribal status with their own roll. Missions followed: an Evangelical and Reform Church school and mission about seven miles from Black River Falls (1875), a Lutheran mission and school at Wittenberg (1884); children were sent to the government boarding school at Tomah. The Wisconsin Ho-Chunk "developed an itinerant economy" — harvesting crops for white growers and returning to their homesteads seasonally — sold baskets, and by about 1913 many took summer jobs as dancers at Wisconsin Dells. The peyote religion (Native American Church) reached the Ho-Chunk through John Rave in 1893 and was brought to Wisconsin in 1908, taking hold at Wittenberg, Black River Falls and the Dells region; it kept tobacco offerings, song and vision, and younger members added Christian elements from their own visions. The Indian Reorganization Act "seemed irrelevant" to the self-sufficient Wisconsin Ho-Chunk, and the nation voted a constitution only in 1963. Sam Blowsnake (b. 1875, Black River Falls; called Crashing Thunder) told his life to the anthropologist Paul Radin, who printed it in 1913 and as Crashing Thunder (D. Appleton, 1926) — Radin "silently rephrased, re-arranged, and in some cases added," and scholars still argue how much of the book is Blowsnake's.
  • The odd detail: A Wisconsin Ho-Chunk man's life story reached print in 1926 through a New York publisher, and its editor had rewritten it.
  • People: Sam Blowsnake (Crashing Thunder) — b. 1875, alive in 1958; John Rave — brought peyote to the Ho-Chunk; Paul Radin — anthropologist, student of Franz Boas.

Our Lady of Good Help — the Walloon chapel at Champion that stood through the fire of 1871 — Brown County, 1859–1942

  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A city that lives by insuring other people's disasters. A claims adjuster's file of "acts of God" from the 1930s is a gazetteer of the odd — every fire, every death, every disappeared warehouse.

Part J — Radio, photographs and trivia

The radio that never went dark — Madison's 9XM and WHA, 1915–1934 (BENDS)

  • Where: University of Wisconsin, Madison — Science Hall's basement, then Sterling Hall (studios until 1934).
  • What happened: Physics professor Earle M. Terry and students built radio apparatus in Science Hall from 1915; the Commerce Department licensed the experimental station 9XM in June 1915. On 1 December 1916 it announced daily (except Sunday) weather forecasts in Morse for farmers, and in early 1917 Terry tested voice. With U.S. entry in April 1917 all civilian transmitters were shut down, and civilians could not even possess a working receiver. The station returned to civil licence in 1920 (9XM call revived 11 February 1920; the university's radio work had already resumed in May 1917 under Navy supervision) ⟨fixed: V_R42⟩, sent spoken weather on 3 January 1921, and got the call letters WHA on 13 January 1922; the Wisconsin School of the Air began on 5 October 1931 with ten weekly programmes for grade schools.
  • The odd detail: The station that was sending farm forecasts in Morse in December 1916 was ordered dark by the war four months later — and had to relearn to speak.
  • People: Earle M. Terry — physics professor; Eric R. Miller — Weather Bureau meteorologist and collaborator.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WHA_(AM) (read)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — with no U.S. entry the April 1917 seizure never happens: 9XM keeps building from 1917 rather than restarting in 1920, so Wisconsin's radio is a few years ahead of history, and every civilian in the setting can own a receiver in 1917–19.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A university station on the air from 1917 will hear what the wartime shutdown kept silent elsewhere: early numbers stations, voices that turn out to be from the setting's Germany, and one signal in the Science Hall log that no one has ever traced.

The photographer of Black River Falls — Charles Van Schaick, the 1890s and Wisconsin Death Trip (living memory)

  • Where: Black River Falls, Jackson County; the studio of Charles Van Schaick, active 1885–1940.
  • What happened: Charles Van Schaick, a German-American immigrant who was justice of the peace and town photographer, made about 30,000 glass-plate negatives between 1890 and 1900 [UNVERIFIED — V_R42: a search result says ~30,000 negatives in all, ~8,000 salvaged; 1890–1900 dates the plates Lesy used]. After his death in 1940 they sat in his studio for thirty years; the Wisconsin Historical Society acquired the collection in 1970 and salvaged about 8,000 negatives. Michael Lesy, a UW graduate student who found the plates in the late 1960s, chose about 200 for Wisconsin Death Trip (Pantheon, June 1973), set against the Badger State Banner (edited by Frank Cooper and his son George), Mendota State Hospital records, and passages from Hamlin Garland, Sinclair Lewis and Glenway Wescott. The book documents the 1890s panic in Jackson County (1885–1900): diphtheria among children, economic collapse after mine closures, violent crime, suicide, mental illness. A 1999 film by James Marsh (narrated by Ian Holm; music by DJ Shadow and John Cale) followed.
  • The odd detail: In 1936 the man who photographed the dead children of the 1890s was still alive and working in the same town.
  • People: Charles Van Schaick — photographer and justice of the peace (studio 1885–1940); Michael Lesy — author (1973); Frank and George Cooper — editors of the Badger State Banner.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin_Death_Trip (read) ; https://unmpress.com/books/wisconsin-death-trip/9780826321930 (search result)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — pre-1916 (the 1890s) and unaffected by the 1916 break.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The 1890s "panic" as a case file: an old JP whose negatives show something in the frame of six unrelated town photographs. He is still alive in 1936, and remembers.

The sundae and the wood type — Two Rivers, 1880–1930s (partly LEGEND)

  • Where: Two Rivers, Manitowoc County, on Lake Michigan (the Hamilton Manufacturing Company factory; Washington House ice-cream parlour).
  • What happened: The claim: in 1881 a customer, George Hallauer, asked soda-fountain owner Ed Berners to top his ice cream with the chocolate sauce used for sodas; it was sold only on Sundays until a little girl asked for one to "pretend it was Sunday"; the spelling "sundae" is said to come from a mistranscription on the check — a rival claim exists elsewhere (not verified here). The record: J. Edward Hamilton started cutting wood type for a local editor's posters in 1880; by 1900 the Hamilton Manufacturing Company was the country's largest wood-type maker; it also made type cabinets, press-room and dental and medical furniture, drafting tables and, by the early 1900s, the first gas-powered clothes dryer; about 1917 it turned to steel furniture. The Two Rivers Historical Museum occupies the Washington House. (R22 records the Christmas Tree Ship, the Rouse Simmons, as wrecked off Two Rivers.)
  • The odd detail: The town that says it named the sundae also makes the letters for every carnival poster in Wisconsin.
  • People: Ed Berners — soda-fountain owner; George Hallauer — the customer; J. E. Hamilton — founder of Hamilton Manufacturing.
  • Sources: https://woodtype.org/pages/history (read) ; https://wisconsinlife.org/story/how-two-rivers-invented-the-ice-cream-sundae/ and https://www.roadsideamerica.com/story/11967 (search results) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_Wood_Type_and_Printing_Museum (search result)
  • Confidence: High on Hamilton; the sundae is a claim (labelled).
  • Divergence check: holds — pre-1916.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): An entire county's handbills, wanted posters and circus bills set in the same wooden fonts. A cipher hidden in the typeface — a font that has one letter missing.
