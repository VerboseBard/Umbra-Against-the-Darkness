R22 — Folklore and the uncanny of the Great Lakes region, as it was told before and during the 1930s

  • Lane: R22 — the real-folklore layer ("three layers of truth") and regional roots for the setting's monster types (vampires, werewolves, demon summoners and cults, the fae, ghosts)
  • Date: 2026-09-26
  • Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent)
  • Rule used throughout: every entry is labelled folklore/legend and says when and where the story is first attested. A legend attested in or before the 1930s is marked PERIOD-ATTESTED; one first recorded later is marked LATER and should not be presented as something a 1930s Chicagoan knew.

Overview

52 entries. The strongest finding is that the Great Lakes' French layer is in print and period-attested: Marie Hamlin's Legends of Le Détroit (1883/84) and Charles Skinner's Myths and Legends of Our Own Land (1896) give Detroit a full bestiary a 1930s reader could borrow from a library — the Nain Rouge (with its 1805-fire sighting), two werewolf legends (Jacques Morand turned to stone at Grosse Pointe; the wolf that stole Archange Simonet, shot with a silver bullet and fled "down the throat of a catfish"), Le Lutin that rides horses by night, the feu follet that "would have entered and strangled us," an excommunicate's coffin of snakes, and a chasse-galerie that is a death-omen Wild Hunt rather than Beaugrand's flying canoe. This corrects R06's note that the region has "no recorded werewolf tradition": it has one, French and Catholic, from the 1880s. The Anishinaabe, Menominee and Ho-Chunk traditions (Mishipeshu and its copper, the Thunderbirds, the windigo and Jack Fiddler) are presented as living traditions with their outside attestations; several famous "Indian legends" turn out to be literary (the Piasa legend was invented in 1836; the Sleeping Bear story's first record is unclear). For the period press, Chronicling America was closed, but Hoosier State Chronicles carried the wire: a Chicago man who axed his mother "because she was a werewolf" (1 Sept 1937), a Buffalo witchcraft murder with grave-dirt and a paper-doll effigy (1930), the Sandusky sea serpent (1931–35) and its python "capture," and "Hanover's vampire murderer" Haarmann (1924–25). Jane Addams's own account of Hull-House's haunted attic (1910) is the best period-attested Chicago ghost. Every entry says when its story is first attested; modern inventions (the "Lake Michigan Triangle," 1977; the Michigan Dogman, 1987; the rugaru in print) are flagged LATER.

A. French Detroit — the habitant legends (Hamlin 1883/84, Skinner 1896)

Source note for this whole section. The French legends of Detroit reach print in two books a 1930s reader could pull off a library shelf: Marie Caroline Watson Hamlin, Legends of Le Détroit (Detroit, 1883/1884) — 31 legends dated 1659–1815, which Hamlin says she heard "from loving, though aged lips of ancestors whose memories extend back into the last century" — and Charles M. Skinner, Myths and Legends of Our Own Land, vol. 6 "Central States and Great Lakes" (1896), which retells many of Hamlin's. Both are PERIOD-ATTESTED (in print forty-plus years before the 1930s). Neither is proof the stories were told in the 1700s — Wikipedia notes "no records that indicate the legend of the Nain Rouge existed in the 18th century." Hamlin's full chapter list, for a GM who wants to mine it: The Cross and the Manitou (1659) · The Baptism of Lake Sainte Claire (1679) · The Nun of Ste. Claire (1690) · The "Nain Rouge" (1701) · The May Pole (1704) · The Phantom Priest (1705) · François and Barbe (1710) · The Devil's Grist (1712) · Jean Chiquot (1721) · The Widow's Curse (1735) · Le Lutin (1746) · The Warrior's Love (1747) · The Miami Seer's Prophecy (1758) · The Bones of the Prophet (1761) · The Bloody Run (1763) · Le Loup Garou (1770) · The Old Red Mill (1775) · La Chasse Galerie (1780) · Le Feu Follet (1785) · The Feast of St. Jean (1790) · Hamtramck's Love (1793) · The Haunted Spinning Wheel (1795) · The Cursed Villaire (1800) · San Souci and Okemos (1805) · The Sibyl's Prophecy (1806) · Captain Jean (1807) · Kennette's Vision (1808) · The Fisherman of Grosse Pointe (1810) · The Ghost of Montreaugeon (1812) · The Eve of Epiphany (1813) · Kishkaukon (1815).

The Nain Rouge — Hamlin's full version: Mère Minique's warning, the May Pole, the 1805 fire — legend set 1701–1812; first printed 1883/84 (deepens R07's entry)

  • Where: Detroit — the strait, the beach below Fort Pontchartrain; the town that burned on 11 June 1805.
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED 1883/84): In Hamlin's telling, at a Quebec banquet in 1701 celebrating Cadillac's expedition a fortune-teller, Mère Minique, promises he will found a great city but warns: "Appease the Nain Rouge (Red Dwarf). Beware of offending him." After the May Pole festivities of 1704 Cadillac meets on the beach a dwarf "very red in the face, with a bright, glistening eye" and "a grinning mouth displaying sharp, pointed teeth," strikes at it — "Get out of my way, you red imp!" — and thereafter loses his seigniory, is removed to Louisiana, and his children never inherit. Hamlin lists its later appearances: before Dalzell's sortie at Bloody Run (1763); at the 1805 fire that destroyed the town, when "many an old habitant thought that they caught a glimpse of his malicious face"; and on a foggy morning before Hull's surrender (1812) — after which, she says, it "accomplished his mission" and did not return. Skinner (1896) adds that it fled when happiness prevailed, as when a young lover's horse won a race for his sweetheart's hand, and calls it "the Demon of the Strait."
  • The odd detail: In the written legend the dwarf stops appearing after 1812 — so any 1930s sighting is, in period terms, a return. (The 1967-riot and 1976-ice-storm sightings on Wikipedia are LATER.)
  • People: Mère Minique — the Quebec fortune-teller of the legend; Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac — Detroit's founder; Marie Caroline Watson Hamlin — author (1883/84); Charles M. Skinner — reteller (1896).
  • Sources: https://archive.org/stream/legendsofledtr00haml/legendsofledtr00haml_djvu.txt ; https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6611/pg6611.txt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nain_Rouge
  • Confidence: High that Hamlin and Skinner print this; the legend's pre-1883 existence is unproven.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (folklore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers: the period reads it as a quaint Detroit story for tourists; the folklore says it marks the city before disaster; underneath, a fae creature bound to the strait since the founding. It went quiet in 1812 — its first "return" could be the morning of the 1932 Ford Hunger March or the 1933 bank holiday, seen by one old French-speaking Ste. Anne's parishioner nobody believes.

The werewolf of Grosse Pointe turned to stone — "Voilà le Garou!" — legend set 1690; printed 1883/84

  • Where: Grosse Pointe, on Lake St. Clair (the rock at "Tonnancour"); a wayside shrine to the Virgin on the beach.
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED 1883/84): In Hamlin's "The Nun of Ste. Claire," the coureur de bois Jacques Morand loves the devout Geneviève Parent, who means to enter the Church. Refused, he goes to "an old Indian witch" who gives him power "to change himself into the form of a wolf or Loup Garou (wehr-wolf)." He hunts her down the beach; she throws herself at the feet of the Virgin's statue in a rocky grotto, and as the beast leaps onto the rocks it is turned to stone. Hamlin says the stone remained at Tonnancour and the habitants pointed at it and said reverently, "Voilà le Garou!"
  • The odd detail: A werewolf you can still visit — a petrified wolf-shaped rock on a Grosse Pointe beach, by the 1930s the lakefront of Detroit's richest families (the Fords and Dodges built there).
  • People: Jacques Morand — the werewolf; Geneviève Parent — the girl who would be a nun.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/stream/legendsofledtr00haml/legendsofledtr00haml_djvu.txt ; https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6611/pg6611.txt
  • Confidence: High that the legend is printed; whether any such rock existed is UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (folklore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The shape of the setting's werewolf pact already sits in period print: a man makes a bargain with a witch for the wolf. In 1930s Grosse Pointe the "garou rock" is in someone's landscaped garden — and it has moved.

Archange Simonet's werewolf, the silver bullet, and why the French would not eat catfish — legend set 1770; printed 1883/84, retold 1896

  • Where: Detroit's French côte (the ribbon farms along the river).
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED): Skinner's "Were-Wolves of Detroit" (from Hamlin's "Le Loup Garou," set 1770): a werewolf carried off the bride Archange Simonet from her own wedding. Her bridegroom hunted it relentlessly; a neighbour shot off its tail with a silver bullet; the beast escaped into the river, and the habitants held that it "jumped down the throat of a catfish" — which, Skinner says, explained the French-Canadian prejudice against eating catfish. Hamlin's own chapter names the bridegroom Pierre La Fontaine (Lafontaine), a young farmer near Grosse Pointe, and Archange's father as the trapper Simonet — that much is CONFIRMED ⟨fixed: V_R22⟩ by independent secondary retellings of Hamlin's text. But the "silver bullet … severed his tail … secured, dried and stuffed … wonder of the whole country" resolution is WRONG as an account of Hamlin's chapter ⟨fixed: V_R22⟩ — that stuffed-tail/bullet ending belongs only to Skinner's short paraphrase (rightly credited to Skinner in the sentence above). Hamlin's own version has Pierre melt down his silver wedding gifts into multiple silver axe-heads and fight a running battle with the werewolf on horseback; no tail-severing or taxidermy resolution is reported in the sources read this session. [UNVERIFIED — V_R22: exact Hamlin wording, not independently re-read this session; secondary agreement from two unrelated retellings]
  • The odd detail: A werewolf hunted with silver axe-heads melted from wedding gifts, in a running battle on horseback — not the "stuffed tail" version this entry originally gave ⟨fixed: V_R22⟩ — with a fish-market taboo attached (Skinner's ending only).
  • People: Archange Simonet — the stolen bride; Pierre La Fontaine — the bridegroom; Simonet — her father.
  • Sources: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6611/pg6611.txt ; https://archive.org/download/legendsofledtr00haml/legendsofledtr00haml_djvu.txt (chapter XVI)
  • Confidence: High as printed legend.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (folklore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A werewolf that took water and hid "in a catfish" is a folk memory of one that survived. Investigators hunting a 1930s Detroit River werewolf find a bootlegger's boathouse, a tail-less wolf, and an old woman who still won't cook catfish.

The feu follet of Presque Isle and the Devil's Grist — legend set 1712; printed 1883/84

  • Where: Presque Isle (Windmill Point), where the Detroit River leaves Lake St. Clair; the windmill there.
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED): In Hamlin's "The Devil's Grist," a woman named Josette, in illness, bequeaths her mill property "to the devil." After her death a storm shatters the mill and "a fiendish laugh was heard loud above the raging storm from the shattered ruins"; on stormy nights thereafter "the marshes around Presque Isle all illuminated by flames, called by the habitants feu-follet," which lure travellers to help grind the devil's grain. (The feu follet — will-o'-the-wisp — is a standard French-Canadian marsh spirit; Hamlin also has a separate chapter "Le Feu Follet," set 1785, whose text I did not reach.)
  • The odd detail: The lights are recruiters: they draw you in to work the devil's mill.
  • People: Josette — the woman who willed her mill to the devil.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/stream/legendsofledtr00haml/legendsofledtr00haml_djvu.txt
  • Confidence: High as printed legend; the 1785 chapter's content UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (folklore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Windmill Point in the 1930s is the waterworks end of Detroit's east side and a rum-runners' landing; marsh lights there are either smugglers' lanterns or something older that wants hands for its grinding.

The Coffin of Snakes — the excommunicated man of L'Anse Creuse — legend; printed 1896

  • Where: L'Anse Creuse, on Lake St. Clair north of Detroit (Macomb County shore).
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED): Skinner tells of a disreputable man, Lizon, who seduced the pious Julienne; when she repented and went home on New Year's Eve, he dragged her away in the middle of the masking celebration and she lost her reason. The priest excommunicated him, declaring he would find no rest even in his grave. When Lizon died and was buried, "a swarm of fat and filthy snakes" poured from his coffin; storms then undermined and destroyed the village church, until "the curse had passed, and when a new chapel was built the old evils had deserted L'Anse Creuse."
  • The odd detail: The curse falls on the church as well as the man — the village had to build a new chapel to be rid of it.
  • People: Lizon — the excommunicate; Julienne — the woman driven mad.
  • Sources: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6611/pg6611.txt
  • Confidence: High as printed legend.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (folklore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): An IHS case file: a priest's anathema that "took," and a grave that must never be opened. A 1930s road-widening on the Lake St. Clair shore exhumes the old L'Anse Creuse burial ground.

The Snake God of Belle Isle — legend; printed 1896

  • Where: Belle Isle, Detroit River (by the 1930s Detroit's great park — Scott Fountain 1925, the Kahn conservatory, the aquarium).
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED): Skinner: the demigod Sleeping Bear hid his daughter in a covered boat on the river; the Winds, coveting her, broke its cable; the boat became Belle Isle, and Manitou set "a girdle of vicious snakes around the shore to guard her." Later priests smashed an idol there and its fragments became a serpent that hampered La Salle's Griffon. (Historically the French called the island Île aux Cochons, Hog Island, where they let livestock run free; it was renamed Belle Isle on 4 July 1845 for Isabelle Cass. The common story that the hogs were put there to eat the snakes is UNVERIFIED.)
  • The odd detail: The same legend ties Belle Isle's serpents to the loss of the Griffon, the lakes' first ghost ship (see section D).
  • People: Sleeping Bear — the demigod father (the same name as the Michigan dunes).
  • Sources: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6611/pg6611.txt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belle_Isle_Park_(Michigan)
  • Confidence: High as printed legend (a romantic literary retelling, not an ethnographic record).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (folklore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A society garden party at the Belle Isle casino, and a guest bitten by a snake that should not live that far north.

The Old Lodger — Kennette's forgiving ghost — Detroit, d. 1868; printed 1896 (Hamlin's "Kennette's Vision")

  • Where: Detroit.
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED): Skinner: Marie Louise Thebault, called Kennette, a reclusive, miserly shoemaker who died in Detroit in 1868, rented a room to a pious woman who told ghost stories. They quarrelled; Kennette swore she would never forgive her, but challenged: "If you die first, come back. If I should, and I can, I will come." The lodger died; Kennette began seeing lights, and one night confronted "a white phosphorescent form" taking human shape. She cried, "I know you. I believe you, and I forgive." Afterwards she aged fast, went to church, and her face grew kinder.
  • The odd detail: A ghost-pact made in anger and kept by the dead — a named Detroit woman with a death year.
  • People: Marie Louise Thebault ("Kennette") — shoemaker, d. 1868.
  • Sources: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6611/pg6611.txt
  • Confidence: High as printed legend; Kennette's historical existence UNVERIFIED (Hamlin dates her vision 1808).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (folklore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "If I die first, I will come back" is a usable rule for the setting's ghosts: a promise binds. A 1930s client made that promise to someone who has just died.

The Phantom Priest — a murdered missionary's midnight Mass — legend set 1705; printed 1883/84

  • Where: The forest near early Detroit.
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED): In Hamlin, Father Constantin del Halle is murdered (she names a chief, Churlioa, as his killer), and his spirit haunts the forest: Indigenous witnesses report that "sounds of a bell might be heard, and different parts of the mass distinctly made out," and a voice "as mournfully sweet as if it had its source in unshed tears, would float on the midnight air." (A Récollet, Father Constantin Delhalle, was historically killed at Detroit in 1706 — the historical details are not checked here, UNVERIFIED.)
  • The odd detail: The ghost is not a figure but a liturgy — you hear a Mass in the woods.
  • People: Father Constantin del Halle — the murdered priest.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/stream/legendsofledtr00haml/legendsofledtr00haml_djvu.txt
  • Confidence: High as printed legend; the historical killing UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (folklore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): IHS keeps a list of places where a dead priest still says Mass; a Detroit subdivision built in the 1920s boom sits on one, and the new owners hear Latin at midnight.

La Chasse-galerie — the flying canoe — printed in Detroit 1883/84; Beaugrand's classic 1891/92

  • Where: French-Canadian lumber and voyageur country — the Ottawa/Gatineau camps in Beaugrand; Detroit in Hamlin's chapter "La Chasse Galerie" (set 1780).
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED): Lumbermen, drunk on New Year's Eve and a hundred leagues from their sweethearts, make a bargain with the devil so their canoe will fly; the rules are never to say God's name and never to touch the cross of a church steeple, on pain of their souls. They paddle over the night sky, seeing Montreal and the frozen rivers below, and a drunken steersman blasphemes and nearly wrecks them. Honoré Beaugrand's telling appeared in English in The Century Magazine, August 1892; its root is the French legend of a nobleman called Gallery who hunted instead of going to Sunday Mass and was condemned to fly forever through the night skies chased by horses and wolves — a Wild Hunt. Hamlin's Detroit version (1883/84) is the older Wild Hunt, not the lumberjacks' joyride: "A Legend of the Canadian Shore," which she says she took down from an old habitante of ninety who had never left the lake, calls it "The Spectral Aerial Hunt" and says "many honest, upright people still living will testify to having seen this phenomenon." It takes three forms: a canoe manned by twelve men with a dog barking in its prow, "always to the north flies the phantom boat"; shaggy black dogs with drooping ears running on the water as if on a scent; and "once in seven years a solitary horseman, with gaunt, bronzed face, rifle in hand, followed by his pack of dogs, is seen in the sky after sunset." Whoever sees it "knows that it betokens death either to himself or to others dear to him." Her tale: the hunter Sebastien Lacelle of Askin Pointe, on the Canadian shore, leaves his bride Zoé de Mersac on the eve of their wedding to shoot ducks — "To-morrow at dawn, dead or alive, sure" — and never returns; she sees him in a boat in the clouds with his dog Chasseur, promising to come for her "in a year and a day"; a year and a day later, dressed as a bride on the beach, she dies crying "there is Sebastien in the boat," and the onlookers see a phantom boat and hear a dog barking in the sky.
  • The odd detail: In the Detroit version it is a death omen with a timetable — the horseman comes once in seven years, and the dead lover keeps "a year and a day."
  • People: Honoré Beaugrand — Montreal writer (1848–1906); Sebastien Lacelle and Zoé de Mersac — the legend's lovers (Askin Pointe, Ontario shore).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chasse-galerie ; https://archive.org/download/legendsofledtr00haml/legendsofledtr00haml_djvu.txt (chapter XVIII)
  • Confidence: High (both printed versions read or reported).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (folklore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A New Year's Eve 1930s case: lumberjacks in a Wisconsin camp vanish and are seen next morning in a Chicago tavern 300 miles away — with no train ticket and frost in their beards.

Le Lutin — the horned goblin that rides your horses by night — Grosse Pointe; legend set 1746; printed 1883/84

  • Where: Grosse Pointe, on Lake St. Clair — farm barns of the French habitants.
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED): In Hamlin's "Le Lutin," the farmer Jaco finds his prize mare L'Éclair exhausted every morning, her mane tangled; a crony whispers "C'est Le Lutin qui la soigne" (it is the goblin who takes care of her). Le Lutin — also called la bête à cornes, the horned beast — had haunted the Pointe for years and, when it disliked a habitant, rode his finest horses by night. Jaco waits at his window with his rifle and sees the barn doors open by themselves and the mare fly out with a rider that "resembled a baboon, with a horned head, a skin of bristling black hair, brilliant, restless eyes, and a devilish leer," beating her with a thorn-bush stick, "without saddle or bridle." Knowing the rifle is useless, he throws down the holy-water font that hung at the head of every good habitant's bed; the thing shrieks and the mare plunges into the lake and is gone. Hamlin writes that "to this day the Grosse Pointe habitants" mark their horses with a cross, and when a horse is found at dawn "reeking with sweat and foam, and with mane all tangled as if by the claws of a beast," they say it is Le Lutin come again.
  • The odd detail: The proof left behind — the broken font and the goblin's dropped thorn stick — and the claim that in 1883 Grosse Pointe farmers still branded crosses on their horses.
  • People: Jaco — the farmer; L'Éclair — the mare.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/download/legendsofledtr00haml/legendsofledtr00haml_djvu.txt (chapter XI)
  • Confidence: High as printed legend and as Hamlin's claim of living custom (1883).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (folklore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The fae already rode the horses of Grosse Pointe; by the 1930s the barns are the stables and garages of Detroit's auto aristocracy. A polo pony found foaming at dawn, a racing car found with its engine hot and the tank empty — "Le Lutin come again."

Habitant house-charms against the feu follet and the thunder — Grosse Ile; legend set 1785; printed 1883/84

  • Where: Grosse Ile, in the Detroit River fifteen miles below the city — the Macomb family's island.
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED): Hamlin's "Le Feu Follet" is set at the Macomb house, where Monique Navarre waits out a storm. She has the servant throw splinters of the half-burnt Christmas log, kept from year to year, on the fire "to prevent the thunder from falling", and is calmed to see a branch of white thorn over the door — "a divine lightning rod," because thorns crowned Christ; Hamlin adds the belief that bread blessed at three Christmas Masses would protect a house from harm. At dusk a shrill whistle sounds and Monique slams the blinds: "It is the feu follet dancing over the fields, and if I had not shut it out, it would have entered and strangled us." Her law-student brother dismisses it as marsh gas — and Hamlin remarks that her own contemporaries blame such things on "electricity."
  • The odd detail: The will-o'-the-wisp here does not lure — it comes indoors and strangles, and a shutter is enough to stop it.
  • People: Monique and Robert Navarre — the legend's siblings (the Navarres and Macombs were real Detroit families; the episode is legend).
  • Sources: https://archive.org/download/legendsofledtr00haml/legendsofledtr00haml_djvu.txt (chapter XIX)
  • Confidence: High as printed legend and printed folk-practice list.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (folklore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A ready-made list of period protective charms for players who ask "what would a French Detroit grandmother put over the door?": holy-water font, crossed horses, white thorn, the Christmas log, thrice-blessed bread.

A2. Chicago's own period-attested haunting

The haunted attic of Hull-House and the pitcher of water on the stairs — 1889; printed by Jane Addams 1910

  • Where: Hull-House, 800 S. Halsted St., Near West Side, Chicago (the 1856 Charles J. Hull mansion).
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED): When Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr moved into the old Hull mansion in 1889 it had been a second-hand furniture store, a factory store-room, and a Little Sisters of the Poor home for the aged. In Twenty Years at Hull-House (1910) Addams wrote that the house "had a half-skeptical reputation for a haunted attic, so far respected by the tenants living on the second floor that they always kept a large pitcher full of water on the attic stairs." She guessed it was "a survival of the belief that a ghost could not cross running water" — but added, disarmingly, "perhaps that interpretation was only my eagerness for finding folklore." (R15 has the 1913 Devil Baby at the same house; that is a separate legend.)
  • The odd detail: The tenants' defence is a pitcher of water on the stairs — a barrier between the living floor and the dead one — and the most famous social worker in America recorded it.
  • People: Jane Addams — founder (d. 21 May 1935); Charles J. Hull — builder of the house (1856); Helen Culver — Hull's heir and the owner who gave the house.
  • Sources: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1325/pg1325.txt (ch. V)
  • Confidence: High (Addams's own words). The later "Mrs. Hull's ghost" stories told on modern ghost tours are LATER and were not checked.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (and Addams's 1935 funeral in the courtyard holds — see R08/R03).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The water-on-the-stairs rule is a period-attested mechanic the setting can use for its ghosts: running (or poured) water as a threshold. After Addams dies in May 1935, someone forgets to keep the pitcher filled.

B. Anishinaabe (Ojibwe, Odawa, Potawatomi), Menominee, Ho-Chunk and Illinois traditions

How to use this section. These are living traditions of living nations, not dead "Indian legends." In the 1930s the Potawatomi, Ojibwe, Odawa, Menominee and Ho-Chunk were present across the region (the Menominee reservation in Wisconsin; Ojibwe reserves and reservations around Lake Superior; the Prairie Band Potawatomi in Kansas and the Forest County Potawatomi in Wisconsin; Ho-Chunk families around Black River Falls and the Wisconsin Dells). Some beings below are sacred and some stories belong to particular families or seasons; the period sources are outsiders' records (missionaries, travellers, ethnographers), and the Skinner retellings are romantic literature. A GM using them should not make the nations themselves monsters or cultists — the setting's principle "cult versus population" applies with double force. I give each item's first outside attestation, not the tradition's age.

Mishipeshu, the underwater panther — the guardian of Lake Superior's copper — attested by the Jesuits (1660s) and Kohl (1859)

  • Where: Lake Superior, above all Michipicoten Island (its home in tradition) and the copper shores — Isle Royale and the Keweenaw; painted at Agawa Rock (Lake Superior Provincial Park, Ontario).
  • What happened (TRADITION, PERIOD-ATTESTED): Among the Anishinaabeg (Ojibwe, Odawa, Potawatomi) the underwater panther — Mishipeshu, "the Great Lynx" — is a composite: the body of a wild cat, horns of deer or bison, upright scales along the back, an immensely long tail. It lives in deep water, raises storms, squalls and rapids, drowns people, and is master of water creatures including snakes; it is the counter-power to the Thunderbirds. It guards the copper of Lake Superior: by the 17th century taking copper from its territory was strongly taboo. The Jesuit Claude Dablon recorded a cautionary tale of four Ojibwe who died after stealing copper from its island; J. G. Kohl (Kitchi-Gami, 1859) saw a Fond du Lac chief display a piece of copper said to be "a strand of hair" of the mishibizhiw. Agawa Rock's pictographs show the panther with two giant serpents and a canoe. As late as the 1950s the Prairie Band Potawatomi still performed a ceremony to placate the Underwater Panther and keep balance with the Thunderbird.
  • The odd detail: Copper is its hair. The whole Michigan copper industry — Calumet & Hecla, the Keweenaw — was dug out of a being's pelt, in the tradition's terms.
  • People: Claude Dablon, SJ — 17th-c. recorder; Johann Georg Kohl — German traveller (1855 visit, book 1859/60).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mishipeshu ; https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6611/pg6611.txt (Skinner: the Chippewas worked the Keweenaw copper "timidly, for they believed that a demon would visit with injury or death the rash mortal who should presume to pillage his treasure")
  • Confidence: High (tradition and its outside attestations as reported).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (tradition; living).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers for any Copper Country case: the period reads mine collapses as bad timbering and a dying industry; the tradition says you are robbing something; underneath — the setting's own choice. Keep the Ojibwe characters as the people who know the rules, not as the threat.

The Thunderbirds and the horned serpents — the war in the sky over the lakes — Menominee, Ojibwe, Ho-Chunk

  • Where: The whole region; Menominee country in northeastern Wisconsin; Thunder Bay/Thunder Cape on Lake Superior.
  • What happened (TRADITION): In Ojibwe tradition the Thunderbirds (animikiig, the thunderers) were made by Nanabozho to fight the underwater spirits; they rule the upper world as the underwater panther and the great horned serpent rule the underworld, and thunder and lightning are their weapons in that struggle. They come with the migrating birds and punish those who break moral codes. Menominee tradition places the thunderbirds on a floating mountain in the west; they bring rain and fight the great horned snakes (Misikinubik) "to keep them from overrunning the earth and devouring humankind." Among the Ho-Chunk a man granted a thunderbird vision while fasting would become a war chief. Skinner (1896) puts Manibozho (Nanabozho) at Thunder Cape on Lake Superior.
  • The odd detail: Every summer thunderstorm over a Wisconsin lake is, in this reading, a battle between two powers — and lightning striking water is a hit.
  • People: Nanabozho (Manibozho) — the Anishinaabe culture-hero.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thunderbird_(mythology) ; https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6611/pg6611.txt
  • Confidence: High as reported tradition (the Wikipedia page cites Vecsey 1983, not the period ethnographers; the classic period records are Alanson Skinner's Menominee work, 1913–15, and Paul Radin's The Winnebago Tribe, 1923 — not read this session).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (tradition; living).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A structural gift for a monster setting: the region's oldest cosmology already has two sides, neither of them human. Something the Thunderbirds hunt might be what a Chicago cult is summoning.

The Windigo — the cannibal spirit of winter famine — Jesuit Relations 1661 → Jack Fiddler 1907 → 1930s ethnography

  • Where: Algonquian-speaking north — Ojibwe, Cree, Oji-Cree, Innu; the Lake Superior shore and the boreal north of Ontario, Minnesota and Michigan's Upper Peninsula.
  • What happened (TRADITION + RECORD): The windigo is a malevolent cannibal spirit of winter, cold, famine and starvation, and a person can be possessed by or become one. In 1661 the Jesuit Relations recorded men "seized with an ailment unknown to us," with "more than canine hunger" for human flesh, and that such men were put to death before they killed. In 1878–79 the Plains Cree trapper Swift Runner killed and ate his wife and five children only 25 miles from Hudson's Bay Company food supplies; he was hanged at Fort Saskatchewan. In 1907 the North-West Mounted Police arrested the Oji-Cree healer Jack Fiddler (Zhauwuno-geezhigo-gaubow, "He who stands in the southern sky") of the Sandy Lake–Deer Lake country, who said he had defeated fourteen wendigos — some sent by enemy shamans, some community members who had asked to be put down before they turned; he was charged over the killing of Wahsakapeequay, his brother Joseph's daughter-in-law. Jack slipped away on a walk and hanged himself (30 Sept 1907); Joseph was sentenced to death, and the order releasing him arrived three days after he died in 1909. Algernon Blackwood's "The Wendigo" (1910) brought the word into English horror; the 1930s were the decade of the ethnographers (John M. Cooper's "The Cree Witiko Psychosis," 1933, and Ruth Landes's Ojibwa fieldwork, 1932–36 — see next entry — both not read this session).
  • The odd detail: Jack Fiddler was a windigo-killer — the person his own people sent for — and Canada tried him for murder. The monster-hunter as defendant.
  • People: Jack Fiddler — Oji-Cree healer, d. 1907; Joseph Fiddler — his brother, d. 1909 in custody; Swift Runner — executed 1879 [UNVERIFIED — V_R22: not on the Jack Fiddler page checked this session]; Algernon Blackwood — author (1910).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendigo ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Fiddler
  • Confidence: High for the Fiddler case, CONFIRMED ⟨fixed: V_R22⟩ in detail: 14 windigos claimed, arrested by the NWMP, escaped and hanged himself the same day (30 Sept 1907); Joseph convicted and sentenced to death, an appeal secured his release but the order arrived three days after his own 1909 death in custody. The 1661 quote and the Cooper 1933 citation are from general knowledge (Medium), not re-checked this session.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened (a Canadian case; Canada's post-1916 status BENDS under Germany's peace but the 1907 trial stands).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Jack Fiddler is the Hunter Guild's ideal and its indictment: a man who killed monsters for his community and was hanged by a state that did not believe in them. A 1930s Guild recruiter carries his story — or his grandson.

Ruth Landes and Maggie Wilson — an ethnographer in Ojibwe country, 1932–1936

  • Where: Ojibwe communities of Ontario and Minnesota (Rainy River country); Prairie Potawatomi in Kansas.
  • What happened (RECORD): Columbia-trained anthropologist Ruth Landes did fieldwork with the Ojibwa of Ontario and Minnesota and the Potawatomi of Kansas between 1932 and 1936, publishing Ojibwa Sociology (1937) and The Ojibwa Woman (1938); her chief informant, Maggie Wilson, told the stories through which Landes described how Ojibwe women handled and bent their roles. Her religious material (Ojibwa Religion and the Midewiwin) waited until 1968.
  • The odd detail: In the setting's exact decade, a young woman from New York was sitting in Ojibwe houses writing down exactly the kinds of stories this file lists — a real, datable NPC type.
  • People: Ruth Landes — anthropologist (1908–1991); Maggie Wilson — Ojibwe storyteller and informant.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Landes
  • Confidence: High for the dates and books; the exact communities are not on the page (Manitou Rapids/Emo, Ontario, is from general knowledge — UNVERIFIED).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The investigator's folklorist: a Columbia ethnographer whose field notebooks hold a windigo account she did not publish — and an FBI Section 13 agent who wants them.

The memegwesi (maymaygwashi) — the little people of the rock cliffs

  • Where: Anishinaabe and Cree country — traditionally associated with rock faces and cliffs by water (the Lake Superior and Canadian Shield lakes).

(The widely repeated associations — that they live in cliff crevices at the water's edge, steal fish and paddles, and made some of the rock paintings — are not on the page I read: UNVERIFIED this session.)

The puk-wudjies — Schoolcraft's little people (1839), Longfellow's killers of Kwasind (1855)

  • Where: Ojibwe country of the upper lakes (Schoolcraft's Sault Ste. Marie informants); later transplanted to Massachusetts.
  • What happened (TRADITION as printed, PERIOD-ATTESTED): Henry Rowe Schoolcraft printed "Puck Wudj Ininee" in Algic Researches (1839), from Ojibwe sources; Longfellow's Song of Hiawatha (1855), built on Schoolcraft, has the Puk-Wudjies kill the strong man Kwasind (canto XVIII). In 1906 a Massachusetts town history relocated the story to Assawompset Pond, and a 1934 anthology (Reynard's The Narrow Land) printed Wampanoag retellings; the "pukwudgie" as a malicious New England or Indiana monster is LATER (post-1980s).
  • The odd detail: Every 1930s schoolchild who recited Hiawatha knew that the little people of the Great Lakes could kill a giant.
  • People: Henry Rowe Schoolcraft — Indian agent at Sault Ste. Marie; his wife Jane Johnston Schoolcraft (Ojibwe) was a principal source of his stories (general knowledge); Henry W. Longfellow — poet.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pukwudgie
  • Confidence: High for the printing history.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Hiawatha pageants were a staple of 1930s summer camps and resorts on the lakes — an outsiders' costume version of real beings, performed on the beings' own shore.

The Piasa — Marquette's painted monster on the bluff at Alton — seen 1673; invented legend 1836; repainted 1924, 1935, 1938

  • Where: The Mississippi bluffs at Alton, Illinois (about 250 miles southwest of Chicago, near the Illinois River's mouth).
  • What happened (RECORD + INVENTED LEGEND): In 1673 Father Jacques Marquette saw two painted monsters on the limestone bluff, "as large as a calf," with "horns on their heads like those of a deer, a horrible look, red eyes, a beard like a tiger's, a face somewhat like a man's, a body covered with scales," and a tail winding round the body "ending in a fish's tail," painted in green, red and black. The original was quarried away (c. 1846–47; nothing left by 1867). In August 1836 John Russell published "The Piasa: An Indian Tradition of Illinois," with a man-eating bird slain by chief Ouatoga, who offered himself as bait under an invisible shield while warriors shot poisoned arrows — and later admitted to his son that he invented it; his gloss "the bird that devours men" is disputed. In 1896 Skinner guessed the carving might remember the last pterodactyls. In 1924 the 18-year-old Boy Scout Herbert Forcade painted a new Piasa on the cliff (dedicated 4 Oct 1924); Irvin H. Mawdsley repainted it in 1935 (in yellows) and again in 1938 (in reds and greens).
  • The odd detail: The Piasa was repainted twice in the 1930s — the monster on the bluff was literally being refreshed in the setting's decade, in two different colour schemes.
  • People: Jacques Marquette, SJ — first recorder (1673); John Russell — inventor of the legend (1836); Herbert Forcade — 1924 painter; Irvin H. Mawdsley — 1935/1938 repainter.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piasa ; https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6611/pg6611.txt
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Perfect three layers: the period sees a civic landmark and a Boy Scout project; the "folklore" is an 1836 forgery; underneath, what Marquette actually saw was a warning painted by people who knew what lived in the river. Why did the 1935 painter change the colours in 1938?

Starved Rock — the siege legend of the Illinois — 1769 legend; printed by Schoolcraft 1825; lodge built by the CCC 1933–39

  • Where: Starved Rock, on the Illinois River near Utica/Ottawa, LaSalle County, Illinois — about 90 miles southwest of Chicago.
  • What happened (LEGEND, PERIOD-ATTESTED): After the Odawa leader Pontiac was assassinated (20 April 1769, by a Peoria man, in Cahokia), Odawa and Potawatomi warriors are said to have taken revenge on the Illinois Confederation, whose people fled to the top of the sandstone butte and were besieged there until they starved. Henry Schoolcraft printed an early written version in 1825. Historians note there is no evidence beyond oral history that the siege happened, and the legend's claim that the Illinois were exterminated is false. The Civilian Conservation Corps (Camp 1609) built Starved Rock Lodge and its log cabins in 1933–1939.
  • The odd detail: In the setting's decade a federal work camp was building a resort lodge on top of a legendary site of mass death by starvation — in the middle of the Depression.
  • People: Pontiac — Odawa war leader (d. 1769); Henry R. Schoolcraft — first printer (1825).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starved_Rock_State_Park
  • Confidence: High (legend and its doubts as reported).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the CCC is New Deal and holds under Roosevelt from 1933).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A CCC boy vanishes from Camp 1609; the others say he went up the rock at night because he heard people calling for food. A ghost case on a federal payroll — work for FBI Section 13 (or for the Secret Service, its older rival).

Porte des Morts — "Death's Door" and the drowned war party — tradition told to fishermen in the 1840s; printed 1917

  • Where: The strait between the tip of Wisconsin's Door Peninsula and Washington Island, joining Green Bay and Lake Michigan (the county is named for it).
  • What happened (TRADITION + RECORD): According to traditions told to fishermen in the 1840s, a Potawatomi war party was caught by the Ho-Chunk (Winnebago) at a bluff in the mid-17th century, and a north wind and surf smashed their canoes on the rocks — hence the French Porte des Morts, door of the dead. A government engineer, Captain Brink, recorded the tradition in an 1834 survey; Hjalmar R. Holand printed it in his History of Door County, Wisconsin (1917). Shipwrecks in the strait included the Fleetwing (1888) and, in 1892, the A. P. Nichols, Forest and J. E. Gilmore, though the passage took relatively few lives.
  • The odd detail: A whole Wisconsin county is named after a drowning.
  • People: Hjalmar R. Holand — Door County historian (also the great champion of the Kensington Runestone — see section F).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porte_des_Morts
  • Confidence: High for the printing history; Medium for the tradition's details (the article notes a pro-Potawatomi bias).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Door County in the 1930s was a summer resort for Chicago money. A yacht party goes through the Door at night, and one guest hears drums under the water.

The Sleeping Bear and the Manitou Islands — date of first record UNVERIFIED

  • Where: Sleeping Bear dunes, Leelanau/Benzie counties, on Lake Michigan's northeast shore; North and South Manitou islands offshore.
  • What happened (TRADITION): An Ojibwe legend tells that a great fire on the Wisconsin shore drove a mother bear and two cubs into Lake Michigan; the cubs drowned within sight of the far shore, and the mother lay down on the bluff to wait; the Great Spirit raised the two Manitou islands for the cubs and buried the mother under the dune. The "bear" was a small tree-covered knoll on the bluff top that looked like a sleeping bear from the water, since much reduced by wind and erosion. (Skinner in 1896 uses "Sleeping Bear" as the name of a demigod in his Belle Isle legend — section A — so the name at least is in print by then.)
  • The odd detail: The fire comes from Wisconsin — across the lake — like the fires of October 1871 (Peshtigo burned the same night as Chicago).

Lake Superior's water gods, the echo-imps of the Grand Portal, and the Witch of Pictured Rocks — printed 1896

  • Where: Lake Superior's south shore — Pictured Rocks (Alger County, Michigan), the Grand Portal arch, La Chapelle; Thunder Cape; the mouth of Superior Bay.
  • What happened (LEGEND as printed by an outsider, PERIOD-ATTESTED 1896): Skinner: many spirits of Lake Superior demanded offerings of tobacco and implements from travellers; storm rulers lived at La Chapelle among the Pictured Rocks; at the Grand Portal "mischievous imps" repeated every word spoken beneath the arch; a spirit at the mouth of Superior Bay destroyed any boat crossing without an offering. In "The Witch of Pictured Rocks," an Ojibwe widow, jealous of a hunter's young wife, pushes her on a vine swing over the lake and "cut the stem," then wears the drowned woman's clothes; the children see through her, the hunter prays, lightning strikes the lake, and a gull rises with the dead wife's beaded belt; caught, it becomes the wife again, and the witch, with "a cry of despair," flies off as "a black, ungainly bird."
  • The odd detail: An arch that repeats what you say — the period's own acoustic legend, and a perfect place for an investigator to hear a voice that is not an echo.

  • Confidence: High that Skinner prints it; how far these are genuine Ojibwe narratives rather than Victorian literary work is UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A shapechanger who steals a wife's clothes and place — the fae changeling pattern, told on Lake Superior.

Devil's Lake, Wisconsin, and Mackinac's cavern of spirits — printed 1896

  • Where: Devil's Lake (Sauk County, Wisconsin, near Baraboo — by the 1930s a state park and CCC camp); Mackinac Island, Michigan.
  • What happened (LEGEND as printed, PERIOD-ATTESTED 1896): Skinner: Devil's Lake, in what locals took for an extinct volcanic crater, cold and pure with no visible inlet or outlet, was the home of a manitou who made its rocks — the Devil's Door-Way, the Black Monument, Cleft Rock — and "all game within its precincts was in the manitou's protection"; hunters would not fish or drink there. D. The lakes — ghost ships, vanished crews, serpents

Note on the "Lake Michigan Triangle." The phrase is LATER: it comes from Jay Gourley's 1977 book The Great Lakes Triangle, with later writers narrowing it to Lake Michigan (Ludington–Manitowoc–Benton Harbor). A 1930s Chicagoan would not have used it. The incidents the phrase collects, though, are real and several are in or before the decade — below. (Captain George R. Donner's disappearance from a locked cabin on 28 April 1937 is already in R06 and R07.)

Le Griffon — the lakes' first lost ship — vanished 18 Sept 1679

  • Where: Left Washington Island at the mouth of Green Bay for Niagara; never arrived.
  • What happened (RECORD + LEGEND): La Salle's Griffon, keel laid 26 Jan 1679 above Niagara and the first sailing ship on the upper lakes, sailed from the Green Bay islands on 18 Sept 1679 with some 12,000 pounds of furs worth 50–60,000 francs and vanished with all hands. La Salle suspected the pilot, Luc (the Dane), of scuttling her and deserting with the furs to join Du Lhut, and hinted that rival traders and the Jesuits covered it up. Legend adds an Iroquois curse (no named source or date on the page I read — UNVERIFIED). Candidate wrecks keep being "found": an 1898 wreck at Manitoulin Island with six skeletons aboard, whose remains were documented in the 1930s–40s and washed away in a 1942 storm; a 2014 claim was a tug.
  • The odd detail: A wreck with six skeletons, claimed as the Griffon, was lying on the Manitoulin shore through the 1930s.
  • People: René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle — owner; Luc — the suspected pilot.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Griffon ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Michigan_Triangle
  • Confidence: High for the loss; the curse and ghost-ship sightings UNVERIFIED as to date.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Skinner's 1896 Belle Isle legend (section A) says a serpent made from a smashed idol "hampered" the Griffon — the period's own occult explanation is already in print.

The Bannockburn — "the Flying Dutchman of the Great Lakes" — lost 21 Nov 1902

  • Where: Lake Superior, last seen about 80 miles off Keweenaw Point, bound for Sault Ste. Marie with 85,000 bushels of wheat.
  • What happened (RECORD + LEGEND, PERIOD-ATTESTED): The Canadian steel freighter vanished with twenty crew, most aged 17 to 20; Captain George R. Wood (37) was the oldest. Captain James McMaugh of the Algonquin saw her several times around midday; that night crew of the passenger steamer Huronic saw lights they took for hers in a winter storm. The only wreckage ever found was one cork life preserver washed up at Grand Marais on 12 Dec. Within a year she was being called "the Flying Dutchman of the Great Lakes," and sailors reported seeing her still.
  • The odd detail: A crew of teenagers, one life-ring, and a ghost-ship name that was attached within a year — thirty years of sightings behind it by the 1930s.
  • People: Capt. George R. Wood — master; Capt. James McMaugh — last to see her by daylight.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Bannockburn
  • Confidence: High for the loss and the nickname's early date; individual later sightings not dated on the page.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A November-gale set piece: a laker's wheelsman in 1936 sees a freighter with a Canadian ensign and boys on deck; the name on the bow is a ship lost 34 years before.

The Christmas Tree Ship — the Rouse Simmons and the Schuenemann women — lost 23 Nov 1912; the trees kept coming to Chicago

  • Where: Lake Michigan off Two Rivers, Wisconsin (wreck in 172 ft of water); destination the Clark Street bridge dock, Chicago.
  • What happened (RECORD + LEGEND): Captain Herman Schuenemann and his brother August had been selling Christmas trees off schooners at Chicago since the 1890s; August had already died when the S. Thal sank in a November 1898 storm. On 23 Nov 1912 the three-masted Rouse Simmons foundered in a gale bound for the Clark Street dock with about 5,500 trees; all 17 aboard died. His widow Barbara and their two daughters kept selling Christmas trees in Chicago (later bringing them by train); the lake trade itself died out by about 1920. In 1924 a fishing net brought up the captain's wallet. The wreck was found only in 1971 by diver Gordon Kent Bellrichard, trees still in the hold. The area is said to have frequent ghost sightings (none dated on the page — treat as later).
  • The odd detail: In 1924 the lake gave back the captain's wallet — twelve years down — while his widow was still selling trees in the city.
  • People: Herman Schuenemann — "Captain Santa"; August Schuenemann — brother, d. 1898; Barbara Schuenemann — widow, kept the trade going.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rouse_Simmons ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Michigan_Triangle (the Rouse Simmons also rode out the May 1891 squall that swallowed the Thomas Hume)
  • Confidence: High; ghost sightings Low/undated; Barbara's exact final season UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (and the 1924 wallet holds).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A Chicago Christmas case: every December a German widow sells trees near the Clark Street bridge, and every year a buyer finds lake-water frozen in the branches. The dead crew still deliver.

The Thomas Hume (1891) and the Rosabelle (1921) — crews that vanished off the Michigan shore

  • Where: Lake Michigan between Chicago and the Michigan shore; the Rosabelle served the House of David colony at Benton Harbor.
  • What happened (RECORD): On 21 May 1891 the schooners Thomas Hume and Rouse Simmons left Chicago after unloading lumber; a squall hit; the Rouse Simmons turned back, the Hume "continued on, and disappeared" with six men. (Found intact in 150 ft of water only in 2006; judged a storm loss.)   • What happened (RECORD, via the WPA's 1930s translation): The Saloniki (Greek), 29 Nov 1913, reported Bill Laskaris found shot dead at a Halsted Street hotel and said "more than ten Greeks have been found dead or murdered" there over the years — once six Greeks dead of gas together — and asked whether it was coincidence or the work of an unknown hand, proposing the place be called the "Death Hotel" and closed.
  • The odd detail: The WPA translators filed it under the English word "haunted" — a community's own ledger of a building that kept killing its lodgers.
  • People: Bill Laskaris — found shot, 1913.
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5422062_4_0578
  • Confidence: Medium (one item; the hotel's name/address not given).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 / living memory.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A lodging house near Hull-House with a quiet body-count, still open in the 1930s under a new name — for the setting's ghosts, or for something that feeds on sleeping men.

H. Chicago's and the ring's other legends — fires, graves, lighthouses, poltergeists

Mrs. O'Leary's cow — a reporter's invention (confessed 1893) and a comet (1882) — the fire of 8 Oct 1871 as folklore

  • Where: The O'Leary barn, DeKoven St., Near West Side, Chicago; and the whole lakes ring that night.
  • What happened (LEGEND + RECORD): The story that Mrs. O'Leary's cow kicked over a lantern and started the Great Chicago Fire became the city's founding folk-tale; in 1893 the reporter Michael Ahern retracted it, "admitting it was fabricated" (the City Council formally exonerated the O'Learys in 1997). Other culprits proposed: Daniel "Pegleg" Sullivan (a modern theory). In 1882 Ignatius Donnelly's Ragnarok: The Age of Fire and Gravel first suggested the fire came from the sky; a physicist revived a Biela's-comet version in 2004 (rejected). The reason the sky theory keeps returning: on the same night, 8 Oct 1871, the Peshtigo fire in Wisconsin killed some 1,200–2,500 people — the deadliest fire in American history — and fires burned at Holland and Manistee, Michigan, and at Port Huron.
  • The odd detail: Four towns around Lake Michigan burned on one night, and the city's own explanation was a cow a reporter admitted inventing — in print, in 1893, the World's Fair year.
  • People: Catherine O'Leary — the blamed woman; Michael Ahern — the reporter; Ignatius Donnelly — the comet theorist (also the Atlantis author).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Chicago_Fire
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers on the city's origin: the period's cow, Donnelly's comet, and — for the setting — a rite performed on four shores at once. A 1930s cult that marks 8 October every year with four fires.

The Robinson family burial ground — a Potawatomi-Odawa chief's land inside Cook County — reserve 1829; family there into the 20th century; ghost lore LATER

  • Where: The Robinson Reserve on the Des Plaines River, Leyden Township (today Robinson Woods, Cook County Forest Preserve, near Schiller Park/Norridge, on Chicago's northwest edge).
  • What happened (RECORD + LATER LEGEND): Alexander Robinson (Chee-chee-bing-way), born c. 1789 on Mackinac Island and fluent in Odawa, Potawatomi, Ojibwe, English and French, helped protect the Kinzie family and evacuate the wounded after the Fort Dearborn fight of 1812. At the 1829 Treaty of Prairie du Chien he received a 1,280-acre reserve on the Des Plaines and $200 a year; he died there on 22 April 1872. The reserve held "a burial ground for many Native Americans"; his family went on living on the land into the 20th century (his youngest child was the family matriarch), and the Forest Preserve District acquired it only in 1955. His gravestone was lost for decades and returned to descendants in 2016. The site's ghost legends are LATER (not dated on the page; treat as post-1950s).
  • The odd detail: Throughout the 1930s a Native family still lived on its own treaty land and burial ground inside Cook County, a streetcar ride from the Loop.
  • People: Alexander Robinson — chief and interpreter (c. 1789–1872).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Robinson_(chief)
  • Confidence: High for the history; the ghost lore is flagged later and not used here.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 / holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Respectfully: the Robinsons are the people who know the ground, not its monsters. A Chicago case that reaches the Des Plaines woods is a chance to meet a family that has been guarding a burial ground since before the city had a name for it.

The Baldoon Mystery — Ontario's poltergeist and the silver bullet in the black goose — events of the 1830s; printed at Wallaceburg (4th ed. c. 1910)

  • Where: The Baldoon settlement (Lord Selkirk's 1804 Scottish colony) on the Chenal Écarté ("Snye Carte") near Wallaceburg, Ontario — the St. Clair River/Lake St. Clair country, across from Michigan.
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED): Neil T. McDonald's pamphlet The Baldoon Mystery ("Belledoon Mysteries"), published by W. Colwell of Wallaceburg and in its fourth edition by about 1910 at 25 cents, says "all occurred in the years between 1830 and 1840" and that men still living would swear to it. The John T. McDonald family heard people marching through the kitchen, saw bullets and stones come through the windows, and fires start about the house. A travelling pedlar, Patrick Tobin of Chatham, lost twenty silver half-dollars overnight; at breakfast they came back one by one, ringing on the window pane, nineteen of them. A schoolmaster, Robert Barker, moved in to study it as witchcraft; a Methodist elder rode with McDonald through the Long Woods by moonlight and they heard an invisible multitude tramp towards them and another host meet it "in mortal combat." The resolution in the retellings: McDonald cast a silver bullet and shot a black-headed goose, and found an old woman on her porch with a broken arm — the witch.
  • The odd detail: Money returned through the glass by an invisible hand — nineteen of twenty coins.
  • People: John T. McDonald — the haunted farmer; Patrick Tobin — pedlar; Robert Barker — schoolmaster-investigator; Neil T. McDonald — author; W. Colwell — Wallaceburg publisher.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/download/baldoonmysterywi00mcdo/baldoonmysterywi00mcdo_djvu.txt ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldoon_Mystery
  • Confidence: High that the pamphlet prints this; the first edition's date (often given as 1871) UNVERIFIED this session.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The region's classic witch case — silver, a shapechanger, a wounded old woman — sits a ferry ride from Detroit. A 1930s rum-runner's family on the Snye whose grandmother still has a crooked arm.

Skull Cave, Mackinac Island — Alexander Henry sleeps on a heap of bones — June 1763 (published 1809)

  • Where: Skull Cave, Mackinac Island, 0.4 mi north of Fort Mackinac.
  • What happened (RECORD): During Pontiac's War, after the capture of Fort Michilimackinac, the fur trader Alexander Henry was hidden in a cave; he made a bed of branches, took the bones on the floor for animals', and at daylight "discovered, with some feelings of horror, that I was lying on nothing less than a heap of human bones and skulls, which covered the floor!" (His Travels and Adventures appeared in 1809 — general knowledge, not on the page.) The cave, an 18th-century Native burial site, is inside Mackinac Island State Park.
  • The odd detail: The most-visited summer resort of the Great Lakes rich had, a short walk from the Grand Hotel, a cave where a man slept on skulls.
  • People: Alexander Henry — fur trader (the elder).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skull_Cave_(Mackinac_Island)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Pairs with Skinner's "cavern hall where spirits had their revels" under Mackinac (section B): the ossuary is the antechamber.

The murdered keeper of Gibraltar Point — Toronto's oldest ghost story — killed 2 Jan 1815; bones found 1893

  • Where: Gibraltar Point Lighthouse, Toronto Islands, Lake Ontario.
  • What happened (LEGEND + RECORD): Keeper John Paul Radelmüller died violently on 2 January 1815. Lore says soldiers from Fort York came for bootleg beer, quarrelled, killed him and cut up the body to hide it; in 1893 keeper George Durnan dug near the light and found part of a jawbone and coffin fragments. Recent research (Eamonn O'Keeffe) confirms a murder and names the accused as John Henry and John Blueman of the Glengarry Light Infantry, but doubts the dismemberment. It is called "Toronto's most enduring ghost story" (when the ghost was first reported is not stated on the page).
  • The odd detail: The 1893 jawbone — a keeper digging for his predecessor.
  • People: J. P. Radelmüller — keeper, d. 1815; George Durnan — keeper who dug, 1893.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibraltar_Point_Lighthouse
  • Confidence: High for the death and 1893 find; ghost's first attestation UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Ontario shore is across a border that the 1916 break has redrawn in spirit (Britain bankrupted by Germany); a Toronto case can open at a lighthouse with a keeper who hears digging under the floor.

Fearsome critters — the lumber-camp bestiary — Cox 1910; Charles E. Brown 1935; Tryon 1939

  • Where: Logging camps of the Great Lakes woods (Wisconsin, Michigan, Minnesota).
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, PERIOD-ATTESTED): Turn-of-the-century loggers told tall tales of "fearsome critters" to pass time and scare greenhorns, printed in William T. Cox, Fearsome Creatures of the Lumberwoods (1910), in Charles E. Brown's Paul Bunyan Natural History (1935) — Brown was the Wisconsin state museum's folklore collector in Madison — and in Henry H. Tryon's Fearsome Critters (1939). Among them: the hidebehind, which attacked loggers and was so quick it was always behind a tree when you turned; the hodag (R07); the squonk, so ashamed of its looks it weeps and dissolves in tears; the agropelter, which hurls branches at passers-by; and the snow snake, active only in winter.
  • The odd detail: The hidebehind is a joke that describes a real horror mechanic: a thing that is never seen because it always moves first.
  • People: William T. Cox — author (1910); Charles E. Brown — Wisconsin folklorist (1935); Henry H. Tryon — author (1939).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fearsome_critters
  • Confidence: High for the books and dates.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A CCC or lumber-camp scenario where the old hands' jokes about the hidebehind stop being jokes — the tall tale as camouflage for something real (the owner's "three layers").

Joseph Médard Carrière collects French folktales at Old Mines — a Northwestern University folklorist in the field — 1934–1936 (book 1937)

  • Where: Old Mines, Washington County, Missouri (the last French-speaking enclave of the old Illinois Country); Northwestern University, Evanston, Illinois.
  • What happened (RECORD): Joseph Médard Carrière (1902–1970), a Franco-Ontarian scholar teaching at Northwestern University (naturalised American 1936; moved to Virginia 1942), made field trips to Old Mines from 1934 to 1936, gathering songs and 73 tales from local conteurs among some 600 French-speaking families — much of it from Joseph Ben Coleman, a French-speaking barite miner — and published Tales from the French Folk-Lore of Missouri (Northwestern University Press, Evanston, 1937). (Whether it contains loup-garou or feu-follet tales I could not confirm this session.)
  • The odd detail: The last living French folk tradition of the Mississippi valley was being written down from Evanston in the setting's exact years — by a professor a streetcar ride from Chicago.
  • People: Joseph Médard Carrière — folklorist; Joseph Ben Coleman — barite miner and storyteller.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_M%C3%A9dard_Carri%C3%A8re ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missouri_French
  • Confidence: High for the fieldwork and publication; the tales' supernatural contents UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A ready NPC: a Northwestern French professor who comes back from the lead-and-barite hills with a notebook of loup-garou stories and one story he will not publish. Investigators in Evanston can ask him what the miners were afraid of.

The rugaru — the loup-garou in Métis and Ojibwe speech — attested LATER (1983 in the source read)

  • Where: Métis and Ojibwe communities of the upper Great Lakes and northern Plains (Turtle Mountain, Red River, Sault Ste. Marie Métis).
  • What happened (FOLKLORE, LATER ATTESTATION): The French-Canadian loup-garou — in the Catholic version, a person who fails Easter duty (Lent) seven years running becomes one — passed into Métis speech as rugaru; an Ojibwe usage glosses it as "the hairy man who appears in symptom of danger or psychic disruption in the community." Peter Matthiessen (In the Spirit of Crazy Horse, 1983) suggested the word came into Ojibwe from French through the trappers. The Wikipedia page cites no Great Lakes attestation with a date before the 1980s, so treat Great Lakes rugaru stories as LATER in print (the Detroit loup-garou legends of section A are the region's period-attested werewolves).
  • The odd detail: In this gloss the werewolf is a symptom — it appears when a community is sick.
  • People: Peter Matthiessen — writer (1983).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rougarou
  • Confidence: Low-Medium (thin, late sources).
  • Divergence check: folklore.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A design idea rather than a fact: werewolves as the sign of a community's breakdown — the Depression's shantytowns and evictions would be thick with them.
