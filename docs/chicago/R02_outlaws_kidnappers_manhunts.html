R02 — The outlaw era in the Midwest: bank robbers, kidnappers, jailbreaks and manhunts, 1930–1936

  • Lane: R02 — outlaws, kidnappers, jailbreaks, manhunts (Great Lakes region first, then the wider Midwest)
  • Sweep: 2026-09-26 Chicago & Great Lakes research library
  • Model: Claude Fable 5.1 (research agent, one of 16)
  • Status: complete for this session — 61 entries; leads not chased and Top 10 at the end. Web-search budget ran out mid-session; everything after that was read directly by URL.

Overview

Sixty-one entries. The Dillinger cycle is given in full, one stop at a time (Michigan City, Lima, the police-arsenal raids, the Chicago police's wrong-apartment killings, East Chicago, Tucson, Crown Point, the second gang, St. Paul, Little Bohemia, Hamilton's lye grave at Oswego, the surgery, South Bend, the Biograph, Anna Sage's deportation, the record-versus-theory question, the soap guns in Columbus, Nelson's last day from Lake Como to a Niles Center cemetery gate, Van Meter's alley, Carroll, Frechette, the lawyer and the madam). The Barker–Karpis section carries St. Paul's sold sanctuary from O'Connor to the 1935 wiretaps, the Hogan car bomb, both brewery kidnappings (with the Bensenville house they shared), Goetz's death in Cicero, Dr. Moran's handless body on the Ontario shore of Lake Erie, the Aurora–Yorkville–Oswego triangle, Ocklawaha, Garrettsville and the necktie arrest. The kidnapping section ties the Lindbergh Law to the Secret Six, the Factor hoax, Urschel, Mary McElroy, the Labatt case in Ontario, the South Bend running-board snatch of Howard Woolverton that revived the federal bill, and August Luer's 123 hours in an Alton farm cellar; the "passing through" section has Kansas City, Verne Miller in a Detroit ditch, Floyd at the Conkle farm, Richetti's gas chamber, the Lansing break, Baron Lamm's school and Red Ryan at Sarnia; six BENDS entries cover the Bureau's rise as it would read under K. Edgar Hoover. Every date and name is from a page read this session; where the standard account goes past what was read, it is marked UNVERIFIED. The two things a GM should take first: the number of this decade's dead who have no faces (Hamilton, Goetz, Moran), and how many of the Bureau's founding stories have a witness against them. Where a claim is theory rather than record (the Dillinger substitution, the Hamilton survival, the Chester Smith claim), it is labelled so.

1. The Dillinger cycle

The Michigan City break — 26 September 1933

  • Where: Indiana State Prison, Michigan City, Indiana (Lake Michigan shore, 55 miles from the Loop)
  • What happened: At 1:30 p.m. on Tuesday 26 September 1933 ten convicts walked out of the Indiana State Prison armed with pistols that had been smuggled in for them: Harry Pierpont, Charles Makley, Russell Clark, Edward Shouse, Walter Dietrich, John Burns, James Jenkins, Joseph Fox, John Hamilton and James Clark. Dillinger himself — paroled from this same prison in May 1933 after nine years — had been financing the plan with a summer of bank jobs (Massachusetts Avenue State Bank, Indianapolis, 6 Sept 1933, $24,000) and is credited with arranging the guns; the exact mechanism (the long-told story is a box of thread delivered to the prison shirt factory) is not stated on the pages read this session and is marked UNVERIFIED. The irony every account notes: four days before the break, on 22 Sept, Dillinger had been arrested in Dayton, Ohio, at the boarding house of his girlfriend Mary Longnaker, and on 23 Sept was transferred to the Allen County jail at Lima, Ohio. James Clark was recaptured 29 Sept; James Jenkins was shot dead near Beanblossom, Indiana, 30 Sept.
  • The odd detail: The man who paid for the escape was already in a cell when it happened — and the escapees' first job, the St. Marys, Ohio bank on 3 October, was robbed to pay for coming to get him; the Lima raid followed on the 12th ⟨fixed: V2_R02⟩.
  • People: Harry Pierpont — the gang's real leader in 1933, Dillinger's prison mentor; Charles Makley — St. Marys, Ohio bank robber, forties; Russell Clark — escapee, later got life; John "Red"/"Three-Finger Jack" Hamilton — Ontario-born, two fingers lost sledding; Walter Dietrich — the last of the Lamm gang (see the "Baron" Lamm entry under Living memory); Mary Longnaker — Dayton girlfriend whose landlady tipped police.
  • Sources: https://johndillinger.com/timeline/september-1933 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hamilton_(gangster)
  • Confidence: High on date, names, Dayton arrest; Medium on the smuggling mechanism (UNVERIFIED this session).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Ten men walk out of a lakefront prison on a weekday afternoon, fully armed, and nobody can say how the guns got in. The "box of thread" is the period's own reading; a Section 13 file could hold a different answer — something else was let into the shirt factory that summer.

Dayton arrest and the Lima jail break: Sheriff Sarber killed — 22 September / 12 October 1933

  • Where: Allen County Jail, Lima, Ohio (the sheriff's residence adjoined the jail)
  • What happened: Held at Lima on the Bluffton bank charge, Dillinger waited nineteen days. On the evening of 12 October 1933 Pierpont, Makley and Russell Clark walked into the jail office claiming to be Indiana State Police officers come to extradite him; Harry Copeland, Hamilton and Shouse stood guard outside. When Sheriff Jess Sarber asked for their credentials, Pierpont shot him. Sarber was pistol-whipped as he lay wounded and died; his wife Lucy and deputy Wilbur Sharp were locked in a cell (the wife/deputy detail comes from the general record and is UNVERIFIED on the pages read). Dillinger was released from his cell and the gang drove out of Ohio. This is the killing that eventually put Pierpont and Makley on Ohio's death row.
  • The odd detail: The gang's impersonation of lawmen was a habit — the same trick (badges, credentials, "we've come to move the prisoner") recurs in the outlaw era, and it worked because a small-county sheriff's office in 1933 had no way to check [UNVERIFIED — V2_R02] (at Lima it did not: Sarber asked for credentials and was shot).
  • People: Jess Sarber — Allen County sheriff, killed; Lucy Sarber — his wife, witness; Wilbur Sharp — deputy; Harry Pierpont — shooter, executed Oct 1934; Charles Makley — killed in the Ohio Pen 1934; Russell Clark — life sentence.
  • Sources: https://johndillinger.com/timeline/october-1933 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dillinger
  • Confidence: High on the core; Medium on Lucy Sarber / Sharp specifics.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three men with false credentials walk into a county jail and take a prisoner out. In the setting, a Section 13 team doing the same thing is indistinguishable from the gang — and a county sheriff has to decide in the doorway which he is looking at.

The police-arsenal raids at Auburn and Peru, Indiana — 14 and 21 October 1933

  • Where: Auburn (DeKalb County) and Peru (Miami County), Indiana — the police stations themselves
  • What happened: Two days after Lima, Dillinger, Pierpont and Walter Dietrich walked into the Auburn police station and emptied its arsenal: one Thompson submachine gun, two .38 revolvers, a .30-caliber Springfield rifle, a shotgun and more, with 1,245 rounds of ammunition. A week later (21 Oct) they did the same at Peru: two Thompsons, six bulletproof vests, two sawed-off shotguns, four .38 police specials and two .30-30 Winchesters. Two days after that they took $75,000–$78,000 from the Central National Bank at Greencastle (23 Oct). The gang armed itself out of the police's own lockers before it robbed a single bank that autumn.
  • The odd detail: Six bulletproof vests, government-issue [UNVERIFIED — V_R02], taken from a small-town police station — the same vests the gang wore into banks, and the reason lawmen began shooting for the head and legs [UNVERIFIED — V_R02].
  • People: Walter Dietrich — the arsenal-raid specialist; Harry Pierpont; Dillinger.
  • Sources: https://johndillinger.com/timeline/october-1933
  • Confidence: High (itemised on the page read).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A police arsenal inventory is a plot device: the Bureau counts what was taken and what wasn't, and the one item no one can explain — something the Peru chief had locked away for reasons of his own — is the lead.

The Chicago police "Dillinger Squad", Sgt Shanley, and the Farwell Avenue mistake — December 1933

  • Where: Chicago — a garage on the North Side (Shanley); 1428 W. Farwell Ave, Rogers Park (the raid)
  • What happened: On 14 December 1933 Sgt William Shanley of the Chicago police was killed when he and two officers waited at a garage to question the man who came to collect a car under watch: John Hamilton, who opened fire and escaped. The Chicago Police Department answered by forming a forty-man "Dillinger Squad" under Capt. John Stege. A week later, 21 December, Stege's men, tipped that Dillinger, Hamilton and Pierpont were in an apartment at 1428 W. Farwell Avenue, hit it with sub-machine guns and killed three men. For three hours the police believed they had wiped out the gang. The dead were Louis Katzewitz (a fugitive on a bank-robbery charge), Charles Tattlebaum (a fugitive jailbreaker) and Sam Ginsburg (an ex-convict who owned a cigar store) — criminals, but not Dillinger's.
  • The odd detail: The city police's Dillinger hunt began with the wrong three corpses; the Bureau's, four months later ⟨fixed: V2_R02⟩, with the wrong CCC worker.
  • People: Capt. John Stege — head of the Chicago Dillinger Squad, an old anti-Capone hand; Sgt William Shanley — killed 14 Dec; Louis Katzewitz, Charles Tattlebaum, Sam Ginsburg — the three killed at Farwell Ave.
  • Sources: https://www.flickr.com/photos/chicagocrimescenes/34748341323 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hamilton_(gangster)
  • Confidence: High on the raid address and names; Medium on the Shanley date (from search summaries citing johndillinger.com's "Dillinger Squad" page, not read directly).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three dead men nobody was looking for, killed by mistake in a Rogers Park flat, and a police captain who has to explain them. What if they were somebody's — a Kabbalah's, a Golden Circle cell's — and the mistake was not a mistake?

East Chicago: Officer O'Malley killed — 15 January 1934

  • Where: First National Bank of East Chicago, Indiana (Lake County, on the Chicago city line)
  • What happened: The First National Bank at East Chicago was robbed of $20,000 on 15 January 1934. Patrolman William Patrick O'Malley was shot dead in the street outside; John Hamilton took bullets in the right hand and groin. The Indiana authorities charged Dillinger with O'Malley's murder — the charge that would send him to Crown Point — though the johndillinger.com timeline credits the robbery to "John Hamilton, Homer Van Meter and an unidentified man," and whether Dillinger was present at all has been argued ever since. East Chicago police officers, notably Sgt Martin Zarkovich, would reappear at the Biograph six months later.
  • The odd detail: The one murder Dillinger was formally charged with is the one job his own timeline-keepers say they cannot prove he attended.
  • People: William Patrick O'Malley — East Chicago patrolman, killed; John Hamilton — wounded; Martin Zarkovich — East Chicago police sergeant, later the Biograph go-between.
  • Sources: https://johndillinger.com/timeline/january-1934 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dillinger_Gang
  • Confidence: High on date/amount/O'Malley; Medium on Dillinger's presence (disputed in the record).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): East Chicago's police were later accused of being in the outlaw business themselves. A murder charge built on a robbery he may not have done, in a town whose sergeant would help deliver him dead — the Golden Circle's kind of arrangement.

Tucson: the Hotel Congress fire and the capture — 22–25 January 1934

  • Where: Hotel Congress, Tucson, Arizona; then 927 North Second Avenue and a house on North Avenue (Dillinger)
  • What happened: Wintering in Arizona under false names, Clark and Makley were registered at the Congress Hotel when it caught fire on 22 January 1934. They paid firemen $50 to go back for their luggage (which held the guns and cash), then moved to 927 North Second Avenue. On 23 Jan a fireman recognised Clark's face from a detective magazine and told the Tucson police. On 25 Jan, in a rolling series of arrests without a shot fired, Makley was taken at a radio store, Clark and Opal Long at the Second Avenue house, Pierpont and Mary Kinder on South Sixth Avenue, and Dillinger and Billie Frechette at the North Avenue house hours later. Dillinger had $7,175.44 on him (on the Dillinger Gang page, which this entry cites ⟨fixed: V2_R02⟩). Four states fought over him [UNVERIFIED — V2_R02]; on 29–30 Jan he was flown by charter plane from Tucson to Douglas, Arizona, then by American Airways with several stops and plane changes to Chicago's Midway (landing 6 p.m., 30 Jan) ⟨fixed: V2_R02⟩ and driven in a thirteen-car motorcade to the Lake County Jail at Crown Point, arriving 7:40 p.m.
  • The odd detail: The whole gang was undone by a hotel fire and a fireman, William Benedict, who read True Detective (Dillinger Gang page; the History Reader excerpt agrees ⟨fixed: V2_R02⟩) — and by the tip they left, which was too generous for tourists.
  • People: Russell Clark and Opal Long; Charles Makley; Harry Pierpont and Mary Kinder; Billie Frechette — Menominee-born, Dillinger's companion; William Benedict — the Tucson fireman (named on the Dillinger Gang page ⟨fixed: V2_R02⟩).
  • Image: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hcongressfire.jpg (Commons — a 19 January 2008 photo of the Dillinger Days re-enactment of the fire, not the 1934 fire; CC BY-SA 3.0 / GFDL ⟨fixed: V_R02⟩) ; https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hotel_Congress_Tucson_AZ.jpg (Commons — modern exterior; check licence). The gang's rooms were on the third floor; the hotel runs an annual "Dillinger Days" re-enactment.
  • Sources: https://johndillinger.com/timeline/january-1934 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dillinger_Gang ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotel_Congress
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A fire that opens the luggage. A GM can run the "what was in the bags" question straight: the gang carried something out of the Midwest that the Hunter Guild wanted back.

Crown Point: the "escape-proof" jail and the wooden gun — 30 January to 3 March 1934

  • Where: Lake County Jail, Crown Point, Indiana; the sheriff's car found at 1057 Ardmore, Chicago
  • What happened: The jail's keeper was Sheriff Lillian Holley, sworn in by 24 January 1933 [UNVERIFIED — V_R02] to finish the term of her husband Roy, who had been shot dead near Gary by a farmer who wounded seven officers before killing himself; she was the first woman sheriff of Lake County [UNVERIFIED — V_R02]. Local officials had called the jail "escape-proof," and on the day Dillinger arrived, prosecutor Robert Estill posed with his arm around the prisoner for a photograph that ended his career. On Saturday 3 March 1934, during morning exercise, Dillinger produced a small pistol — the record says whittled from wood and blackened with shoe polish; the guards had mocked him as "John the Whittler" — took trustee Sam Cahoon as a shield, made the guards lock themselves in cells, took an automatic weapon from the warden's gun rack, and walked out with Herbert Youngblood, a Black prisoner awaiting trial for murder. They drove off in Holley's new Ford V-8 [UNVERIFIED — V2_R02] (the pages read say only the sheriff's car). On 5 March the car was found abandoned at 1057 Ardmore Avenue, Chicago; on 7 March the Bureau entered the case, because driving a stolen car across the Indiana–Illinois line violated the federal Dyer Act. Youngblood was shot dead by police in Port Huron, Michigan (14 March per the johndillinger.com timeline; 16 March per Wikipedia). Deputy Ernest Blunk later swore the gun was real; Bureau files say wood. The suspicion that a real pistol was smuggled in by Dillinger's lawyer Louis Piquett or a bribed official has never been settled.
  • The odd detail: Dillinger reportedly laughed that he had done it with a piece of wood [UNVERIFIED — V2_R02] — and the only reason the federal government got its hands on him at all was a car-theft statute and a state line about ten miles away ⟨fixed: V_R02⟩.
  • People: Lillian Holley — widow-sheriff, blamed by the press for her sex; Robert Estill — prosecutor ruined by a photo; Sam Cahoon — trustee/hostage; Ernest Blunk — deputy, said the gun was real; Herbert Youngblood — fellow escapee, killed at Port Huron; Louis Piquett — Dillinger's lawyer, suspected.
  • Image: the jail itself — Lake County Sheriff's House and Jail, 226 S. Main St ⟨fixed: V2_R02⟩, Crown Point (built 1882; NRHP 1989; a June 2012 photo on https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_County_Sheriff%27s_House_and_Jail ; kept open by the Old Sheriff's House Foundation) ; https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dillingerwantedposter.jpg (Commons — the 1934 federal poster, US government work, public domain)
  • Sources: https://johndillinger.com/timeline/march-1934 ; https://www.thehistoryreader.com/us-history/dillingers-daring-escape/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dillinger ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillian_Holley ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_County_Sheriff%27s_House_and_Jail
  • Confidence: High on dates and mechanics; Medium on the real-vs-wooden question (genuinely unresolved in the record) and on the Youngblood date (two sources disagree).
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — the Bureau is K. Edgar Hoover's FBI in the setting ⟨fixed: V2_R02⟩. Note for the Bureau entries: the federal hook here is the Dyer Act, which predates the break (1919).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The three layers of truth in one object: the period says a wooden gun; the folklore says a real one bribed in; the file could say the gun was wood and still worked — on the man who saw it. The Kabbalahs' work on the eye is a thing the Church forbids its own priests.

Sioux Falls and Mason City: the second gang, with Baby Face Nelson — 6 and 13 March 1934

  • Where: Security National Bank, Sioux Falls, South Dakota; First National Bank, Mason City, Iowa
  • What happened: Three days out of Crown Point Dillinger was robbing banks with a new crew: Homer Van Meter, Eddie Green, Tommy Carroll and Lester Gillis — "Baby Face" Nelson. Sioux Falls (6 March) yielded $49,500 and a wounded motorcycle officer, Hale Keith. Mason City (13 March), with John Hamilton added, yielded $52,000 (Carroll's page says $52,344) but both Dillinger and Hamilton were shot. The gang used hostages on the running boards as shields. This is the point at which the Dillinger name attached itself to Nelson's temper: Nelson's earlier work — Grand Haven, Michigan (People's Savings Bank, 18 Aug 1933, where his getaway driver fled and Earl Doyle was captured by townsfolk and got life) and Brainerd, Minnesota (First National, 23 Oct 1933, $32,000, machine-gun fire sprayed at bystanders) — had been his own.
  • The odd detail: The getaway driver at Grand Haven saw a shotgun and drove off without the gang; townspeople wrestled one robber down themselves.
  • People: Lester Gillis / "Baby Face" Nelson — Chicago-born (the Patch, near West Side), five-foot-four; Homer Van Meter — Fort Wayne, Dillinger's Michigan City friend; Eddie Green — St. Paul "jug-marker" (bank spotter); Tommy Carroll — Red Lodge, Montana ex-boxer, wheelman; Earl Doyle — captured at Grand Haven.
  • Sources: https://johndillinger.com/timeline/march-1934 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommy_Carroll_(criminal) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dillinger_Gang
  • Confidence: High on the two March robberies; Medium on the Grand Haven/Brainerd specifics (from search summaries of Legends of America / local-history pages, not read in full).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A "jug-marker" is a scout who cases banks in advance — a role a fae-touched or a cult's seer could fill; Eddie Green was the gang's eyes and died first.

St. Paul: the Lincoln Court Apartments shootout — 31 March 1934

  • Where: Apartment 303, Lincoln Court Apartments, 93 South Lexington Avenue (Parkway), St. Paul, Minnesota
  • What happened: On 20 March 1934 Dillinger and Billie Frechette moved into Apt. 303 as "Mr. and Mrs. Carl T. Hellman." A suspicious caretaker tipped the Bureau on 30 March; the next morning Agents Rusty Nalls and Rufus Coulter with St. Paul detective Henry Cummings knocked. Frechette stalled at the door while Dillinger and Van Meter (who arrived mid-standoff) shot their way out; Dillinger took a bullet in the left calf and the pair escaped by the back stairs. Days later (3 April) Eddie Green was shot by agents in St. Paul and died on the 10th ⟨fixed: V_R02⟩; Frechette was arrested in Chicago on 9 April and was convicted in St. Paul of harboring, serving two years at the federal farm at Milan, Michigan. St. Paul, the gang's chosen sanctuary, was no longer safe — the O'Connor system (see §3) was breaking.
  • The odd detail: The sanctuary city's own caretaker turned them in; the "layover" was dying from the bottom up.
  • People: Billie Frechette — later toured with the Dillinger family in a show called "Crime Doesn't Pay"; Rufus Coulter and R. C. "Rusty" Nalls — Bureau agents (names as given on the timeline page: "Coulter and Nolls"); Henry Cummings — St. Paul detective; Eddie Green — died 10 April ⟨fixed: V_R02⟩.
  • Sources: https://johndillinger.com/timeline/march-1934 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evelyn_Frechette
  • Confidence: High on address/date; Medium on Green's dates (from the general record; his Wikipedia page not yet read this session).
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — the Bureau is K. Edgar Hoover's FBI in the setting ⟨fixed: V2_R02⟩.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A caretaker who notices the wrong things about the couple in 303 — a Section 13 informant, or a man who saw something through the door that had nothing to do with guns.

Little Bohemia: the Bureau shoots the wrong car — 20–22 April 1934

  • Where: Little Bohemia Lodge, Manitowish Waters, Vilas County, Wisconsin (US-51 in the northwoods)
  • What happened: The gang — Dillinger, Van Meter, Hamilton, Carroll, Nelson, and their women — arrived on 20 April at Emil Wanatka's lodge (built 1929); the standard account has them sent there by a Fox River Grove roadhouse-keeper, Louis Cernocky (UNVERIFIED this session — see §7). Wanatka's wife Nan slipped a note inside a cigarette pack to her brother-in-law Henry Voss, who carried it to the U.S. Marshal, who reached the Bureau's Chicago office early Sunday 22 April. Melvin Purvis flew agents to Rhinelander and drove to the lodge in commandeered cars, arriving about 8:00 p.m. As they deployed, three lodge customers — CCC workers Eugene Boisneau and John Morris and a salesman, John Hoffman — got into a 1933 Chevrolet coupé to leave. The agents shouted; the car radio drowned it; they opened fire, killing Boisneau and wounding the other two. The gang went out the back windows and over the lake bank. Nelson, separated, reached the Koerner house down the road and, when Agent W. Carter Baum, Agent Jay Newman and constable Carl Christensen drove up, shot all three — Baum three times in the neck (killed), Newman in the head (survived), Christensen nine times (survived). Three women — Helen Gillis, Jean Delaney, Marie Conforti — were captured in the lodge and convicted of harboring. The lodge still operates and shows the bullet holes and the belongings the gang left behind.
  • The odd detail: The tip was a note in a cigarette packet; the Bureau's first shots of the manhunt killed a Civilian Conservation Corps boy; and the man who fired at the agents was the one gangster nobody had sent them to find.
  • People: Emil and Nan Wanatka — hosts, informants; Henry Voss — the courier; Eugene Boisneau — CCC worker, killed; W. Carter Baum — Bureau agent, killed by Nelson; Jay Newman; Carl Christensen — constable, nine wounds; Melvin Purvis — Chicago SAC, disgraced by the night.
  • Image: the 1934 exterior on the lodge's Wikipedia page (File:Bohemiaentrancefromhwy512.jpg, captioned "Little Bohemia Lodge as it looked in 1934"; check licence on the file page); the lodge stands at 5625 Little Bohemia Lane, off US-51
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Bohemia_Lodge ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Purvis
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — the Bureau is K. Edgar Hoover's FBI in the setting ⟨fixed: V2_R02⟩ (its "worst failure for 59 years" is his); the CCC is a Roosevelt agency that exists on both timelines.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Something in the northwoods sent the gang to a lodge on a lake, and something sent the Bureau there two days later. The fae are in the setting; the Wisconsin northwoods is where the owner's lodges are. The wrong car shot in the dark is the "cult versus population" rule in one image.

Hastings, Aurora, Oswego: John Hamilton's secret grave — 23–26 April 1934 / found 28 August 1935

  • Where: Hastings, Minnesota (the Mississippi bridge); a rented house in Aurora, Illinois; a gravel pit near Oswego, Kendall County, Illinois
  • What happened: The morning after Little Bohemia, Dillinger, Van Meter and Hamilton were fired on by deputies at Hastings, Minnesota; a rifle bullet through the car's rear took Hamilton in the back. They drove him south to a Barker–Karpis safe house in Aurora rented by Volney Davis and his companion Edna "Rabbits" Murray. Dr. Joseph Moran, the gangs' surgeon, refused to treat him. Hamilton died there on Thursday 26 April 1934. Dillinger, Davis and others buried him in a gravel pit near Oswego and poured cans of lye over his face and hands to defeat identification. On 28 August 1935 [UNVERIFIED — V_R02] federal agents, working from Davis's confession, dug up a body there whose dental amalgams matched Hamilton's Indiana prison records; he was reburied in an unmarked grave in Oswego Township Cemetery [UNVERIFIED — V_R02]. Because the confession's times and places did not agree [UNVERIFIED — V_R02], and because tips kept coming that Hamilton was alive, "Red Hamilton survived" became a durable legend.
  • The odd detail: The lye had done its work — no face, no hands — so the only proof of who lay in the Oswego gravel was the fillings in his teeth.
  • People: John "Red" Hamilton — Byng Inlet, Ontario, b. 1898; Volney Davis and Edna Murray — the Aurora hosts (Barker–Karpis gang); Dr. Joseph P. Moran — refused him (see §3 for Moran's own end in Lake Erie).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hamilton_(gangster)
  • Confidence: High on the sequence; Medium on the "10 cans of lye" figure (from a search summary, not the page read).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A body without face or hands in a Kendall County gravel pit, identified by teeth, and a rumour he never died. The owner's "was it really him?" method fits Hamilton as well as Dillinger — and a werewolf or a revenant is a survival story with teeth.

The plastic surgery at Probasco's — 28 May 1934

  • What happened: Through his lawyer Louis Piquett and Piquett's investigator Arthur O'Leary, Dillinger hired Dr. Wilhelm Loeser (a German-born [UNVERIFIED — V2_R02] surgeon then on parole for narcotics) to alter his face and fingerprints; Dr. Harold Cassidy gave the anaesthetic. The price was $5,000 — $4,400 split among Piquett, Loeser and O'Leary, $600 to Cassidy. 3. The Barker–Karpis gang and St. Paul

The O'Connor "layover" system: a city that sold sanctuary — 1900–1935

  • Where: St. Paul, Minnesota — the Hotel Savoy (downtown), the Green Lantern saloon on Wabasha Street, police headquarters
  • What happened: John J. O'Connor became St. Paul's chief of police on 11 June 1900 and ran the city (with one gap) until 29 May 1920 on a standing bargain with the underworld: a criminal could live in St. Paul unmolested if he (1) checked in with the police on arrival, (2) paid, and (3) committed no major crime inside the city limits. The go-between was William "Reddy" Griffin, who took the check-ins at the Hotel Savoy and died of apoplexy in 1913 at 65; then "Dapper Dan" Hogan of the Green Lantern; then, after Hogan's murder in 1928, Harry Sawyer. The bargain outlived O'Connor by fifteen years. Dillinger and Frechette, Ma Barker and her sons, Baby Face Nelson and Alvin Karpis all "laid over" in St. Paul. It died in 1934–35: the federal crime laws of 1934 gave the Bureau jurisdiction, and the St. Paul Daily News (editor Howard Kahn, with commissioner Henry Warren) hired a Chicago investigator, Wallace Jamie, who wiretapped police headquarters for more than a year; the paper published in July 1935 and a wave of convictions and resignations followed.
  • The odd detail: Sanctuary was a service with a front desk: you registered at a hotel, like a guest.
  • People: John J. O'Connor — chief 1900–20; William "Reddy" Griffin — Hotel Savoy; Dan Hogan; Harry Sawyer; Howard Kahn — editor; Wallace Jamie — the wiretapper (brother-in-law of Eliot Ness's family by the Chicago Jamie line — UNVERIFIED); Thomas A.   • What happened: Hogan owned the Green Lantern — saloon, casino, speakeasy — and was the St. Paul fixer so tied to the Democratic machine that police protected his organisation. The Hamm kidnapping: a brewer taken at lunch, and the wrong men tried — 15–19 June 1933 / trial November 1933

  • Where: St. Paul (the Hamm brewery, the walk home); a Wisconsin stop; a house in Bensenville, DuPage County, Illinois; a farmhouse near Wyoming, Minnesota
  • What happened: Shortly after noon on 15 June 1933 William A. Hamm Jr., president of the Theodore Hamm Brewing Company, walking home from the brewery for lunch, was pushed into a car by Doc Barker and Charles "Fitz" Fitzgerald. Driven to Wisconsin, he signed four ransom notes; then he was held in Bensenville, Illinois, outside Chicago, while the family raised $100,000. The drop instructions involved Duluth and a signal of five flashes of a car's rear lights. He was released at 5:30 a.m. on 19 June by a farmhouse near Wyoming, Minnesota, and the gang went on to Chicago. Karpis pled guilty to it in St. Paul in 1936 and got life. Chief Tom Brown, the record says, had been tipping the gang throughout.

The Bremer kidnapping: $200,000 in fives and tens — 17 January to 7 February 1934

Dr. Joseph Moran, the gangs' surgeon: a boast in Toledo, a body at Crystal Beach — July 1934 / 26 September 1935

4. The kidnapping epidemic and the Lindbergh Law

The "snatch racket" and the Lindbergh Law — 1931–22 June 1932 (amended 1934)

  • Where: Washington; Chicago and St. Louis (the citizens' groups that testified); South Bend, Indiana (the Woolverton case)
  • What happened: By 1931 ransom kidnapping — the "snatch racket," worked first on gamblers and bootleggers who could not go to the police, then on the respectable rich — was a Midwestern industry, and citizens' groups from Chicago and St. Louis went before the House Judiciary Committee to ask for a federal law (search-summary detail; Medium). The bill got its push from two cases: the January 1932 kidnapping of Howard Woolverton, a South Bend manufacturer, and, on 1 March 1932, the taking of Charles Lindbergh's infant son. Senator Roscoe C. Patterson of Missouri introduced S. 1525 on 3 June 1932; President Hoover signed the Federal Kidnapping Act on 22 June 1932. It made it a federal crime to carry a kidnapped person across a state line, with a rebuttable presumption of interstate transport once a victim had been held beyond a fixed period (the page read says 24 hours; the standard figure is seven days — Medium). The 1934 amendment added the death penalty where the victim was not released unharmed and exempted parents taking their own children. Chicago had its own long memory of the racket: the 1928 Ranieri case (a ten-year-old held on an outlying farm, 25-year sentences, a witness murdered — see 09-22 archive: C04) was the pattern the Lindbergh Law was written against.
  • The odd detail: The first victims of the snatch racket could not report it, because they were criminals; the law was written only when the racket reached people who could.
  • People: Roscoe C. Patterson — Missouri senator; Howard Woolverton — South Bend, Jan 1932; Herbert Hoover — signed it (a president on both timelines).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Kidnapping_Act
  • Confidence: High on the Act; Medium on the testimony and the presumption period.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Hoover 1929–33 on both timelines).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A federal statute whose trigger is a state line. Section 13 and the Bureau would fight over every kidnapping in the Calumet, where the Illinois–Indiana line runs down a street.

The Secret Six: Chicago's businessmen hire their own detectives — 8 February 1930 to 17 January 1933

The Factor kidnappings: a son in April, a father in June, and a frame — 18 April / 30 June–12 July 1933 (R01 has Touhy; this is the kidnapping)

The Urschel kidnapping and the birth of the "G-Men" — 22 July to 26 September 1933

  • Where: Oklahoma City (the Urschel house); the Shannon farm, Paradise, Texas; Memphis, Tennessee (the arrest)
  • What happened: On 22 July 1933 George "Machine Gun" Kelly (George Kelly Barnes, b. Urschel's house and took Urschel and his guest Walter Jarrett. Urschel was held over a week at the farm of Kathryn's mother and stepfather at Paradise, Texas, and freed on 30 July for $200,000. Blindfolded, he had counted the minutes between aeroplanes overhead and noted the day the rain grounded them, and left fingerprints on everything he could reach; the Bureau matched the airline schedules to the farm. The Oklahoma City trial was filmed; on 12 October 1933 George and Kathryn Kelly got life. He died at Leavenworth on his 59th birthday, 18 July 1954.

Mary McElroy: the kidnapped girl who pleaded for her kidnapper — 27 May 1933 / 1935 / 21 January 1940

  • Where: Kansas City, Missouri (the McElroy house); a farmhouse at Shawnee, Kansas
  • What happened: On 27 May 1933 Mary McElroy, 25, daughter of Kansas City's city manager Henry McElroy (Pendergast's man), was taken from her bath in her father's house. The Labatt kidnapping: Ontario's brewer, a Muskoka cottage, and the wrong man convicted — 14–17 August 1934

  • Where: Egremont Road, Lambton County, Ontario (between his Lake Huron cottage near Sarnia and London); a cottage on Lake Muskoka; the Royal York Hotel, Toronto; St. Clair Avenue, Forest Hill, Toronto (his release)
  • What happened: On 14 August 1934 John Sackville Labatt, president of the London, Ontario brewery, driving from his Lake Huron summer place to a meeting in London and taking a short cut toward Warwick, was stopped on Egremont Road and taken at gunpoint by Michael "Three-Fingered Abe" McCardell, Jack Bannon, Albert Pegram and Russell Knowles — Americans out of the Covington, Kentucky gambling world by the standard account. The Woolverton kidnapping, South Bend: the running-board snatch that revived the Lindbergh bill — 26–27 January 1932

5. Other outlaws passing through the Midwest

The Kansas City Massacre — 17 June 1933

  • Where: Union Station, Kansas City, Missouri (the plaza outside)
  • What happened: Frank "Jelly" Nash, escaped from Leavenworth in 1930, was picked up in a store in Hot Springs, Arkansas on 16 June 1933 and brought by train to Kansas City. As officers walked him to a car outside Union Station on the morning of the 17th, gunmen opened fire: Bureau agent Raymond J. Caffrey, Kansas City detectives W. J. Grooms and Frank Hermanson, McAlester (Oklahoma) police chief Otto Reed, and Nash himself were killed; two others were wounded. Vernon "Verne" Miller led it; the Bureau named Pretty Boy Floyd and Adam Richetti as the other guns, with Kansas City boss John Lazia providing them, and Floyd denied it to his death. Later writers argue much of the killing was friendly fire from a shotgun in an agent's hands. Within a year, in May and June 1934, Congress gave Bureau agents statutory authority to carry guns and make arrests — the massacre is the standard explanation for it.
  • The odd detail: The federal agents who walked Nash out were not, in law, allowed to carry guns. Their dead colleagues became the argument for arming them.
  • People: Frank Nash; Verne Miller; Raymond Caffrey — Bureau agent, killed; Otto Reed; W. J. Grooms and Frank Hermanson; John Lazia; Pretty Boy Floyd and Adam Richetti — accused.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas_City_massacre
  • Confidence: High on the event; the gunmen's identities are disputed in the record.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the armed Bureau of 1934 is K. Edgar Hoover's; in the setting the Secret Service — a separate, rival federal service; Section 13 is the Bureau's own division — has been armed since 1865 ⟨fixed: V2_R02⟩.   • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Not Great Lakes, but the seed of the whole armed-Bureau decade. The "who fired" dispute is a ready-made three-layers case.

Verne Miller: the ex-sheriff found naked in a Detroit ditch — 29 November 1933

  • Where: A roadside ditch outside Detroit, Michigan; a Chicago apartment (raided 1 Nov 1933)
  • What happened: Miller (b. Kimball, South Dakota 1896) had been sheriff of Beadle County, South Dakota, and a decorated Great War infantryman (Croix de Guerre) before he became a killer for hire and the one identified gunman of the Kansas City Massacre. He hid with Abner "Longy" Zwillman in New Jersey, then in Chicago with his girlfriend Vi Mathias, posing as an optical-supply salesman; when agents raided the apartment on 1 November 1933 he shot his way out. On 29 November a motorist found his body in a ditch outside Detroit: naked, partly strangled with a clothesline, and beaten thirteen times with a claw hammer. The record calls it a contract killing by the national syndicate, with three motives on offer — Zwillman's people, punishment for the massacre, or revenge for an earlier Fox Lake (Illinois) killing.
  • The odd detail: A South Dakota sheriff with a French war medal, tortured to death and left on the road into Detroit.

Pretty Boy Floyd: Buffalo, the fog at Wellsville, the Conkle farm at Sprucevale — 18–22 October 1934

  • Where: Buffalo, New York (the hideout, about a year); Wellsville, Columbiana County, Ohio; the farm of the widow Ellen Conkle near Sprucevale, off the road between East Liverpool and Rogers, Ohio
  • What happened: Floyd and Adam Richetti, with two women, had sat out most of 1934 in Buffalo on Lake Erie. They left on 18 October; at 3 a.m. the next morning, in heavy fog, their car slid into a telephone pole near Wellsville, Ohio. The women went for a tow; a passing motorist, Joe Fryman, and his son-in-law David O'Hanlon reported two men waiting in the woods to Wellsville chief John H. Fultz, who went out with a small party — Richetti was taken after a shooting exchange; Floyd ran. Two days later, on Monday 22 October, he walked out of the woods to Ellen Conkle's farmhouse, was fed, and was sitting in the car of her brother Stewart Dyke when the cars of Purvis's agents and East Liverpool police (Chief Hugh McDermott, patrolman Chester Smith and others) came up the lane. He ran for the corn crib and the treeline and was shot down. His reported words: "I'm done for. You've hit me twice," then "Who the hell tipped you?" and a question about "Eddie." In 1979 Chester Smith said that after his own rifle shots had brought Floyd down, Purvis told agent Herman Hollis, "Fire into him," and Hollis did; former agent Winfred Hopton answered that Hollis was not even present. A death mask was cast in East Liverpool (a Commons photograph exists). At Akins, Oklahoma, between 20,000 and 40,000 people came to the funeral, the largest in the state's history.

The Lansing break: Underhill, Bailey and the warden as a shield — 30 May 1933 / 6 January 1934

Underhill was surrounded at Shawnee, Oklahoma on 30 December 1933 by a 24-man force of agents, troopers and police, shot several times, ran sixteen blocks, broke into a furniture store and collapsed; he died 6 January 1934.

"Baron" Lamm's last job at Clinton, Indiana, and the school he left behind — 16 December 1930

  • Where: Citizens State Bank, Clinton, Vermillion County, Indiana; the fields near Sidell, Illinois
  • What happened: Herman "Baron" Lamm, a former Prussian army soldier, invented the modern bank robbery: hours of casing, a floor plan, timed roles, and a getaway chart ("git") of back roads ("cat roads") pasted on the dashboard, measured block by block. Near Sidell, Illinois, about 200 officers closed in; Lamm and G. W. "Dad" Landy, 71, shot themselves rather than surrender. Walter Dietrich survived, went to Michigan City, and taught the Lamm technique to Harry Pierpont's circle — which is how it reached Dillinger and the 1933 raids.

6. The Bureau's rise — BENDS entries (the man in the setting is K. Edgar Hoover)

Frank Loesch's twenty-eight "public enemies" — April 1930

  • Where: Chicago Crime Commission, Chicago
  • What happened: Frank J. Loesch (b. 9 April 1852; one of the Commission's organisers in 1919; its president from 1928 for five terms; the man who in 1928 warned Capone against more "pineapple primary" violence) picked 28 men from a list of about a hundred "outstanding hoodlums," put Al Capone at the head and Ralph Capone second, and sent the names to the chief of police, the sheriff and every law officer in the county, calling them "public enemies." His stated purpose was to keep the "publicity light" on the city's most prominent gangsters so that police and citizens would keep them under constant observation. The phrase was taken up by Hoover's Bureau for fugitives already under federal charge; between June 1934 and May 1936 it named four men in succession "Public Enemy No. 1" — Dillinger, Floyd, Nelson, Karpis — and the practice became the Ten Most Wanted list. Loesch was the one who, when Dillinger walked out of Crown Point, said: "That's what might be expected of having a woman for sheriff." He died in 1944, aged 92.
  • The odd detail: The Bureau's most famous phrase was a Chicago lawyer's press stunt, and the lawyer was seventy-eight when he coined it.
  • People: Frank J. Loesch; Al and Ralph Capone — first and second on the list; Lillian Holley — the target of his jibe.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_J._Loesch ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_enemy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillian_Holley
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the phrase's federal life belongs to K. Edgar Hoover's Bureau; in the setting the Secret Service "knew what Capone was and took him anyway," so the Crime Commission's list would be read in Washington by two rival services.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A civic list of twenty-eight names with a "publicity light" on it is a hunter's list. In the setting the Commission may have been told which names to leave off.

Cummings's "war on crime": the Bureau gets guns, arrest powers and a federal bank-robbery law — May–June 1934

  • Where: Washington, D.C.; Alcatraz (opened as a federal penitentiary that year)
  • What happened: Attorney General Homer S. Cummings pushed twelve crime laws through Congress in the spring of 1934, buttressing the Lindbergh Law (the death penalty for kidnapping where the victim was harmed), making bank robbery a federal crime, tightening interstate transport of stolen property, extending federal control of firearms (the National Firearms Act — already in the setting), and — in May and June 1934, with the Kansas City Massacre as the argument — giving Bureau agents statutory authority to carry guns and make arrests. He backed Alcatraz as a model prison for "hardened offenders," called a national crime conference, named Dillinger "Public Enemy Number One" in a speech on 22 June 1934, and put up the $10,000 reward. John Paul Chase was the first man tried under the new statute making it a federal crime to kill a federal agent (convicted 24 March 1935). Cummings published We Can Prevent Crime in 1937.
  • The odd detail: Until the summer of 1934 the agents chasing Dillinger through three states could not lawfully carry the guns they carried.
  • People: Homer S. Cummings — Attorney General 1933–39; J. Edgar / K. Edgar Hoover; John Paul Chase — the first defendant.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer_Stille_Cummings ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas_City_massacre ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Paul_Chase ; https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/dillinger-john-dillinger-timeline/
  • Confidence: High on the laws and dates given on the pages read; the individual bill dates are not itemised there.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — this is the legislative birth of the armed federal police in the setting, and it happens under Roosevelt (who holds) with K. Edgar Hoover as its beneficiary. The Secret Service, armed since 1865, would read these bills as a rival's charter — and FBI Section 13 as its own. ⟨fixed: factions — the Secret Service and FBI Section 13 are separate; K. Edgar Hoover runs the FBI⟩
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Twelve bills in one spring. A GM can put a thirteenth in the drawer — the one Section 13 wrote and Cummings would not sign.

Melvin Purvis: the cigar at the Biograph, the fall after Floyd, the pistol in 1960 — 1932–1935 / 29 February 1960

  • Where: The Bureau's Chicago office (the Bankers Building, standard record); Florence, South Carolina
  • What happened: Purvis was Special Agent in Charge at Chicago from 1932. Little Bohemia (April 1934) — "the FBI's worst failure for the next 59 years" — cost him command of the hunt: Hoover put Inspector Samuel Cowley over all agents on the case wherever they were. At the Biograph Purvis gave the signal by lighting his cigar and waving his hand; in October he led the agents at the Conkle farm. He became the Bureau's public face, resigned in July [UNVERIFIED — V2_R02] 1935 under Hoover's displeasure (a 2005 study frames Hoover as jealous of the attention Purvis got after Dillinger), and was erased from the Bureau's own tellings. On 29 February 1960 he died at his home in Florence of a gunshot to the head from the pistol fellow agents had given him when he left the Bureau; a later inquiry allowed that it may have been accidental.
  • The odd detail: The man who lit the cigar died by the gun his colleagues gave him as a going-away present.
  • People: Melvin Purvis; Samuel Cowley; J. Edgar / K. Edgar Hoover.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Purvis
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the Purvis–Hoover rivalry is the Bureau's internal politics; in the setting the man who erased Purvis is K. Edgar, and the reason may be what Purvis learned at Little Bohemia or in the Biograph alley rather than vanity.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Director erases his most famous agent from the record. The owner's method says keep the erasure and put the reason underneath.

Samuel P. Cowley: the apostle's son who ran the "Flying Squad" and died at Elgin — 1934

  • Where: Chicago; Barrington; Sherman Hospital, Elgin, Illinois (standard record for the hospital; the page read gives only the time of death)
  • What happened: Cowley, b. Franklin, Idaho, 23 July 1899, was the son of Matthias F. Cowley, an apostle of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints; he studied at Utah State Agricultural College and George Washington law. In early 1934 Hoover sent him to Chicago as head of the "Flying Squad" to run the Dillinger hunt, over Purvis; he negotiated with Anna Sage, ran the Biograph, and on 27 November 1934 went out himself after Nelson. Shot in the stomach at Barrington, he lived long enough to identify Nelson and his companions to Purvis and died at 2:00 a.m. on the 28th, about ten hours after the fight. The Wikipedia page notes the two histories: "The media always played up Purvis … The official FBI accounts play up Cowley," under Hoover's hand.
  • The odd detail: The Bureau's chosen hero of the Dillinger year was a Mormon apostle's son who died in a Fox Valley hospital; the public's hero resigned under the Director's displeasure ⟨fixed: V2_R02⟩.
  • People: Samuel P. Cowley; Matthias F. Cowley — the apostle; Matthew Cowley — his half-brother, also an apostle.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_P._Cowley
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Cowley is K. Edgar Hoover's man in the setting; his religion is a period category the brief asks GMs to keep (the decade sorted people by faith), and a Latter-day Saint inspector running a federal manhunt in Catholic-Irish Chicago is its own tension.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A man raised inside one church's hierarchy sent to hunt in a city where another church (the IHS, in the setting) hunts something else. What did he see at Little Bohemia that made Hoover trust him?

The Bureau's legends, catalogued: "G-Men," the four Public Enemies, the "criminal brain," the necktie — 1933–1936

  • Where: Memphis; Washington; Ocklawaha; New Orleans
  • What happened: The pages read this session record four manufactured stories in three years. (1) "Don't shoot, G-Men!" — attributed to Machine Gun Kelly at Memphis, 26 Sept 1933; the earliest Bureau account has him standing in a corner with his hands up, and the cry is "a media myth created months after the arrest." (2) "Public Enemy No. 1" — Loesch's Chicago phrase, applied by Washington to Dillinger (22 June 1934), then Floyd, Nelson and Karpis in succession to May 1936. (3) Ma Barker as "the most vicious, dangerous, and resourceful criminal brain of the last decade" (Hoover), against Karpis's "old-fashioned homebody … generally law abiding" and historian Maccabee's finding that Fred Goetz directed the kidnappings. (4) The Director's "personal" arrest of Karpis, 1 May 1936, provoked by a Senate committee — no handcuffs, a necktie, and two irreconcilable accounts of where Hoover stood. Add the erasure of Purvis and the 1979 Chester Smith claim about Floyd, and the Bureau's founding decade is as much a press operation as a manhunt.
  • The odd detail: Every one of the Bureau's four signature stories has a documented counter-version from someone who was there.
  • People: J. Edgar / K. Edgar Hoover; George Kelly; Alvin Karpis; Ma Barker; Melvin Purvis; Chester Smith.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_Gun_Kelly_(gangster) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_enemy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma_Barker ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvin_Karpis ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Goetz
  • Confidence: High (each item is labelled as legend vs. record above).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — these are K. Edgar Hoover's legends in the setting, built to win a public that Section 13 could not court because it does not exist in public. The "three layers of truth" rule applies to the Bureau itself.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Bureau's press office as an antagonist: it needs monsters with faces, and it will make them. The real monsters, which have no faces, prefer it that way.

7. Hideouts and safe places

Fox River Grove: Louie Cernocky's Crystal Ballroom, where both gangs drank — 1923–1934

  • Where: "Louie's Place" / the Fox-River Grove Inn and Crystal Ballroom, Fox River Grove, McHenry County, Illinois (on US-14/Northwest Highway, the Barrington road)
  • What happened: Louis Cernocky Sr. — "a local legend to this day," with property across the village and a "double-life stature as both a respected citizen and Capone gang bootlegger" — built an eight-sided dance hall in 1923 that was restaurant, big-band ballroom, speakeasy and gangster hideout. The village history names the regulars: Alvin "Creepy" Karpis, Ma and Fred Barker, Baby Face Nelson, John Dillinger, Homer Van Meter and Tommy Carroll — the Dillinger and Barker–Karpis gangs at the same tables. The standard account has Cernocky sending the Dillinger gang north to Little Bohemia in April 1934. "The Grove was finally released from the gangsters' grasp in 1934": the chase that ended at Barrington began on Route 14 at its edge.
  • The odd detail: An octagonal dance hall in a river village of a few hundred people, where the two most wanted gangs in America were regulars at once.
  • People: Louis Cernocky Sr.; the two gangs' rosters above.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_River_Grove,_Illinois ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Battle_of_Barrington
  • Confidence: High on the hall and the regulars (village history section); Medium on the Little Bohemia referral.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The owner already has the Fox River valley (Woodstock, an hour out). An eight-sided hall where the outlaws of two gangs met under a respected citizen's roof is a lodge in every sense; the Golden Circle or the Guild could hold its own nights there.

Bensenville: the May Street house that held two brewers — 1933–1934

  • Where: A house on May Street, Bensenville, DuPage County, Illinois (northwest of Chicago, by today's O'Hare)
  • What happened: The village's own history records that "the Barker-Karpis Gang used a house on May Street to hide kidnap victims William Hamm Jr. in 1933 and Edward Bremer in 1934, who they had kidnapped from Saint Paul, Minnesota." Two of the decade's largest ransoms — $100,000 and $200,000 — were negotiated while the victims sat blindfolded in a DuPage County suburb. The householder (Harold Alderton, by the standard record — UNVERIFIED this session) was later convicted with the gang.
  • The odd detail: The same house, twice, seven months apart, for two St. Paul brewers.
  • People: William Hamm Jr.; Edward Bremer; the Alderton household.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bensenville,_Illinois ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidnapping_of_Edward_Bremer
  • Confidence: High on the street and the two victims; Medium on the householder.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A house that is used twice for the same purpose is a house with a habit. In the setting a GM can ask what else was kept on May Street between the brewers.

The lake country as gangster resort: Little Bohemia, Lake Como, Mercer, Rhinelander — 1929–1934

  • Where: Little Bohemia Lodge, 5625 Little Bohemia Lane, off US-51, Manitowish Waters (Vilas County), Wisconsin; Hobart Hermanson's house and Lake Como Inn, Lake Como / Lake Geneva (Walworth County), Wisconsin; Mercer (Iron County), Wisconsin; the Rhinelander airfield
  • What happened: The northwoods and the southern Wisconsin lakes were where Chicago's outlaws went to be tourists. Emil Wanatka built Little Bohemia in 1929; five years later it held the Dillinger gang for two nights and became a museum of the fight, still serving dinner. Agents reached it by flying to Rhinelander and driving through the night in whatever cars they could take, some standing on the running boards. Hobart Hermanson, ex-bootlegger [UNVERIFIED — V2_R02], kept an inn at Lake Como near Lake Geneva where Nelson had wintered before; in November 1934 Hermanson himself told the Bureau Nelson was coming back, and three agents lived in his house for nearly three weeks until the afternoon the Ford V-8 drove up ⟨fixed: V2_R02⟩. Mercer, farther north, was Ralph Capone's town — Al's brother is listed among its notable residents — and the whole Iron–Vilas county belt of resorts was the Outfit's summer country. The lodge has its ghost stories (a Northwoods News feature asks whether the CCC worker haunts it — folklore, labelled).
  • The odd detail: The Bureau's stakeout at Lake Como lost Nelson because its only car had gone for groceries; Little Bohemia's owner had to be tipped into talking by his wife with a note in a cigarette pack.
  • People: Emil and Nan Wanatka; Hobart Hermanson; Ralph Capone; agents Metcalfe, Winstead, McRae.
  • Image: the 1934 exterior on the lodge's Wikipedia page (File:Bohemiaentrancefromhwy512.jpg — captioned "Little Bohemia Lodge as it looked in 1934"; check licence on the file page)
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Bohemia_Lodge ; http://historicalgmen.squarespace.com/near-death-with-nelson-sas-met ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercer,_Wisconsin
  • Confidence: High on Little Bohemia and Lake Como; Medium on Mercer (only the "notable people" line was read).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The fae are in the setting and the northwoods lodges are where the owner's own seed list points. A lake resort where gangsters are guests and the hosts inform on them is a court of a kind; the Bureau flying in at night is the intrusion.

8. Living memory (pre-1930)

Leopold and Loeb: the ransom note that was never meant to be paid — 21 May 1924 (and Loeb's death, 28 January 1936)

  • Where: Kenwood, South Side Chicago (the Harvard School; the Franks house); a culvert by the Pennsylvania Railroad tracks near Wolf Lake, at the Indiana line; Stateville Penitentiary (1936)
  • What happened: On 21 May 1924 Nathan Leopold and Richard Loeb, rich University of Chicago students, offered fourteen-year-old Bobby Franks a ride home from school, killed him with a chisel, and hid the body in a culvert by the railway north of Wolf Lake; they had already typed a ransom note with an "intricate plan" of sequential instructions, for the pleasure of the crime rather than the money. A pair of eyeglasses at the culvert, with a hinge sold to only three customers in Chicago, one of them Leopold, broke the case. Clarence Darrow's eight-hour summation against the death penalty got them life for the murder and 99 years for the kidnapping (10 Sept 1924). Loeb was killed at Stateville on 28 January 1936 by inmate James Day with a straight razor; Leopold was paroled 13 March 1958 after 33 years.
  • The odd detail: The first great Chicago "kidnapping" of the century was a murder with a ransom note as decoration — and one of the two killers was himself cut down in prison inside the game's decade.
  • People: Nathan Leopold; Richard Loeb; Bobby Franks; Clarence Darrow; James Day — Loeb's killer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_and_Loeb
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: post-1916, holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Every 1930s Chicagoan hearing "ransom note" thought of this one first. Wolf Lake sits on the Illinois–Indiana line by the Calumet marshes — the same ground as East Chicago and Gary in this file. And Loeb's razor death in 1936 is a period event a module can use directly.
