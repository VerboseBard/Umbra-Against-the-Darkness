R31 — Places of confinement in Chicago and Illinois, 1925–1940

Lane: asylums, state hospitals, orphanages, poorhouses, homes for "fallen women", reform schools, sanitariums, jails and prisons' hidden wards — and the machinery (courts, laws, treatments) that put people inside them. Date: 2026-09-26 · Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent, 2026-09-26 sweep)

Overview (27 entries): 1930s Illinois ran its confinement through commitment, contract and neglect. Sanity was decided by a jury hearing held at the Cook County Psychopathic Hospital (Keogh, Clarke, Varecha): one jury could find a killer sane and the next insane. And a well-connected gunman could be committed to an unlocked state hospital instead of Chester and simply walk out (the "insane cop", 1930/1940). Illinois never passed a sterilisation law (bills failed in 1925, 1929 and 1933). Instead it locked the "feeble-minded" away for life under its 1915 commitment acts, at Lincoln and Dixon. Dixon's "defective delinquents" escaped 108 times in one year and had no bars until 1937. The Juvenile Court rented beds by the head ($10 a month a boy, $15 a girl) in a patchwork of ethnic and sectarian "industrial schools". Black children had a handful of places, and the Good Shepherd took "white girls" only. Girls and women were held in convent reformatories on House of Correction warrants. The primary sources are the richest part of this file: the state's own 1945 report on Manteno's Well No. 4, and the Tribune pages on Manteno patients working as village servants. For the owner's method the best finds are the Klan's Edward Young Clarke committed in 1932, Henry Darger writing in his rooms, the Bridewell's holy-well name, and Zeller's Old Book legend (folklore, labelled). Search note for later agents: archive.org's full-text search indexes the 1930s Chicago Daily Tribune page by page (see Leads), and that vein is largely untapped. Faction note: hooks name the Secret Service and FBI Section 13 as separate, rival services (K. Edgar Hoover runs the FBI).

Already covered elsewhere in this sweep (not repeated here, only deepened): Dunning's potter's field and the 1923 fire (R06, R08, R09, R13); Manteno's 1939 typhoid and the Bowen indictments (R06, R08, R13); Elgin and Kankakee as places (R09); Oak Forest as a place (R09); the Municipal TB Sanitarium (R13); lobotomy's arrival (R13); Mooseheart (R10); Rube Foster's death at Kankakee (R14); Bellevue Place, Batavia (R29); the Dwight women's reformatory (R29); Mary Bartelme and Edith Sampson of the Juvenile Court (R28); the Municipal Court's Psychopathic Laboratory (R18); Louise deKoven Bowen (R19).

Part 1 — The state hospitals outside Chicago

Manteno's typhoid, from the state's own report: Well No. 4 — 8 July to October 1939 (deepens R06/R08/R13)

  • Where: Manteno State Hospital, Kankakee County, about 40 miles south of Chicago — "100 buildings virtually comprise a small municipality."
  • What happened: The first case fell ill on 8 July 1939; cases peaked on 15 August among employees and 23 August among inmates, and the outbreak ran into October: 453 cases and 60 deaths in a hospital of about 6,200 people (the state's 1945 report; R13 has the 37 and 52 figures from other sources — this is the fullest count). The cause was Well No. 4, one of four drilled wells a few hundred feet apart, drawing from creviced limestone and cased with 14-inch pipe to a depth of only 19 feet, barely a foot into the rock; a sanitary sewer ran about 35 feet away, and sewage had backed up 30 inches deep in a manhole 80 feet from the well. Typhoid organisms were cultured from the well's water on 22–31 August. The activated-sludge sewage plant, built in 1930, was designed for 3,000 people and was taking the waste of 6,200. The Department of Public Health had warned in written water-supply reports in 1931, 1935 and 1937 that the limestone was dangerous and that officials were not exercising "reasonable care". By 5 September practically everyone in the hospital had had three doses of vaccine; chlorinators went in on 19 and 26 August; films, dances, church services and all gatherings of patients were stopped, visitors banned, admissions halted. Two cottages, Clouston and McDowell, became typhoid wards; nine state nurses were sent in on 20 September. An amoebic dysentery case followed on 13 October. The state put the cost at $120,000, and the case went up to the Supreme Court of Illinois (the report sniffs that the court gave the health findings "little probative value").
  • The odd detail: The typhoid wards' excreta were boiled down in 90-gallon cauldrons until only a paste remained, and the paste was thrown on the fire and burned.
  • People: Dwight H. Green — governor under whose authority the report was printed (1945); the report's compilers' names are OCR-garbled in the scan ("Carl E. Scowor", "C. Milton Esswright") — UNVERIFIED spellings.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/stream/34730270R.nlm.nih.gov/34730270R_djvu.txt (Illinois Department of Public Health, A report on a typhoid fever epidemic at Manteno State Hospital in 1939, 1945, NLM copy)
  • Confidence: High — the state's own epidemiological report (numbers are its own; they differ from press figures).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The state knew for eight years that the well drank from the sewer. The occult layer: in the 1939 records the first case is on a ward where a patient had been drawing the same well, in chalk, for weeks. The cauldrons in the yard look like witchcraft to the patients watching from the windows — and to one of them, it is.

Peoria State Hospital, Bartonville — "the Illinois Asylum for the Incurable Insane" — opened 10 Feb 1902; Zeller superintendent 1902–13 and 1921–35

  • Where: Bartonville, on the bluffs just south of Peoria (about 160 miles southwest of Chicago).
  • What happened: The legislature authorised it in 1895 as the Illinois Asylum for the Incurable Insane. The first building, finished in 1897 and described as a facsimile of a feudal castle, was never used: its structure was compromised by abandoned coal-mine shafts under the site (another account says it was simply out of step with modern ideas), and it was razed and replaced with a cottage plan — 33 buildings when it opened on 10 February 1902, eventually 63. By its 25th anniversary in 1927 it held 2,650 patients and had admitted 13,510 in all; it peaked around 2,800 in the 1950s and closed in 1973. Its superintendent Dr. George A. Zeller ran it twice, 1902–1913 and 1921–1935 — so through the whole early Depression — and in the 1920s wrote a memoir, Befriending the Bereft, drawn from "mysterious experiences" at the hospital.
  • The odd detail: The asylum for the incurable was first built as a castle that the ground under it, hollowed by mines, refused to hold.
  • People: George A. Zeller — superintendent, memoirist of the hospital's strange experiences.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peoria_State_Hospital
  • Confidence: High on dates and figures (Wikipedia, citing Bradley University/Peoria Historical Society); Zeller's memoir's exact year UNVERIFIED ("the 1920s").
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The castle stood empty on the mine shafts for years before it came down. Zeller's second tenure ends in 1935; a successor opens the superintendent's files and finds the unpublished chapters of Befriending the Bereft.

"Old Book," the gravedigger who wept at his own funeral — FOLKLORE (patient d. 1910; first told by Zeller, 1920s)

  • Where: The potter's field at Peoria State Hospital, Bartonville, by the "Graveyard Elm".
  • What happened (as legend tells it): Legend says a mute patient recorded as "Manual Bookbinder" alias "A. Bookbinder" (1878–1910), grave marker 713, worked on the hospital's burial crew — the name came from the printing house where he had worked before commitment. At every burial he leaned on the Graveyard Elm and wept. When he died, hundreds of patients and staff attended; the pallbearers found the coffin strangely light; a wailing came from the elm; everyone, Dr. Zeller included, turned to see Old Book weeping by the tree — and when the lid was opened, his body lay inside and the crying stopped. The elm then began to die; workmen who tried to cut it heard it wail, and it was only removed after lightning struck it.
  • The odd detail: A numbered grave marker — 713 — for a man the story says attended his own burial.
  • People: "Old Book" (Manual/Manuel A. Bookbinder) — patient gravedigger; George A. Zeller — the superintendent who tells the story.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Book_(ghost) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peoria_State_Hospital (cites Marilyn Leyland, Peoria Historical Society, "Peoria State Hospital: Bewitched, Bereft, Beloved," arts alive!, Nov 2004)

Chester State Hospital and the courtroom killer — John W. Keogh, 13 Jan 1936 (committed 14 Mar 1936, died there 1947)

  • Where: The shooting: a Cook County Circuit Court courtroom, Chicago. The asylum: Chester State Hospital, on the Mississippi bluffs by Menard penitentiary, Randolph County, far southern Illinois.
  • What happened: Chester opened as the Asylum for Insane Criminals, on the grounds of the Southern Penitentiary at Menard, taking its first patients on 2 November 1891; the original building was badly built and demolished, the institution moved to its own campus near Chester, and in 1910 it became Chester State Hospital — Illinois's asylum for the criminally insane. On 13 January 1936, John W. Keogh, a 73-year-old realty owner obsessed with Illinois's failure to redistrict since 1901 (which, he argued, made the courts illegitimate), was being removed from Judge John Prystalski's courtroom over a mortgage dispute when he drew a pistol: Prystalski ducked; the opposing attorney Christopher Kinney was shot through the heart. Dr. Francis J. Gerty, director of the Cook County Psychopathic Hospital, pronounced him insane ("paranoia and senility") — but a jury declared him sane on 17 January. Judge Denis J. Normoyle ordered a second hearing, which found him insane on 5 February; he was sent to Chester on 14 March and died there in 1947.
  • The odd detail: In 1936 Illinois, sanity was decided by a jury — and one jury could find a man sane four days after he shot a lawyer in open court, until a second jury was convened to find otherwise.
  • People: John W. Keogh — the shooter; Christopher Kinney — the lawyer killed; John Prystalski — the judge he missed; Francis J. Gerty — Cook County's chief psychiatrist; Denis J. Normoyle — the judge who ordered the rehearing; John W. Ryan — the deputy who disarmed him.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_W._Keogh ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester_Mental_Health_Center
  • Confidence: High on the shooting and hearings (Wikipedia, sourced to period press); Chester's 1930s population UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A man convinced the courts are illegitimate because the map was never redrawn since 1901 — in a setting where the map did change. He may be the only sane man in the room, and Chester is where the state puts people who remember the other history.

Jacksonville State Hospital and Elizabeth Packard's jury law — 1860–63 / 1867 (living memory)

  • Where: Jacksonville, Morgan County, west-central Illinois — the state's first asylum (Kirkbride plan, five and a half storeys, opened 1851; first patient Sophronia McElhiney of McLean County, 3 Nov 1851); renamed Jacksonville State Hospital in 1910.
  • What happened: On 18 June 1860 Elizabeth Packard was committed to Jacksonville at her husband's request over a disagreement about her religious beliefs — under an Illinois law that let a husband commit a wife without the proof required for others. Doctors questioned her for three years; in June 1863 they declared her "incurable" and discharged her. She founded the Anti-Insane Asylum Society and in 1867 Illinois passed a "Bill for the Protection of Personal Liberty" guaranteeing anyone accused of insanity a public hearing. The jury hearing was still how Cook County decided sanity in the 1930s (see the Keogh entry). Her books have noir titles: The Mystic Key or the Asylum Secret Unlocked (1866) and The Prisoners' Hidden Life, Or Insane Asylums Unveiled (1868).
  • The odd detail: Her superintendent, Dr. Andrew McFarland, privately called her "a sort of Jeanne D'Arc" — the asylum's own doctor casting his patient as a saint who heard voices.
  • People: Elizabeth Packard — committed wife turned reformer; Andrew McFarland — Jacksonville's superintendent; Sophronia McElhiney — first patient.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Packard ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacksonville_Developmental_Center
  • Confidence: High on Packard; the precise 1930s form of the Illinois commitment statute (whether a jury was mandatory in every case, or only on demand) UNVERIFIED — see Leads.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Great Disclosure of Spiritual Wickedness in High Places (1865) is another real Packard title. A 1930s investigator who reads it in a used bookshop finds the marginal notes of someone who believed it literally.

Part 2 — The "feeble-minded," eugenics and the children's asylum

Henry Darger — from the Lincoln asylum to the rooming house where he wrote the Realms — committed Nov 1904, escaped 1910; writing through the 1930s

  • Where: The Illinois Asylum for Feeble-Minded Children, Lincoln, Logan County (renamed Lincoln State School and Colony); later a rooming house in Lincoln Park, Chicago, and the Catholic hospitals where he worked.
  • What happened: Born in Chicago on 12 April 1892 to a German immigrant tailor, Darger lost his mother in 1895; around 1900 he was placed in the Mission of Our Lady of Mercy (the newsboys' home), and after a clinical examination in November 1904 he was sent to the Lincoln asylum — the intake form gave the reason as "self-abuse". He worked summers on the state farm; his father died on 1 March 1908; after several attempts he escaped in 1910 and walked about 150 miles back to Chicago, where his godmother found him work as a janitor at St. Joseph's Hospital. Through the 1920s and 1930s he worked in Chicago's Catholic hospitals (St. Joseph's, Grant Hospital); in 1932 he took two rooms on the third floor of a rooming house, where over some thirty years he wrote the 15,145-page In the Realms of the Unreal (completed about 1939) — a war of child slaves, led by seven princesses called the Vivian Girls, against the child-enslaving Glandelinians. In 1929–30 he made anonymous inquiries about adopting a child, saying he had "constantly prayed" for it since 1917; he was refused. He went to Mass several times a day. Nobody saw the work until 1972.
  • The odd detail: The spark of the war in the Realms is a lost newspaper photograph: in 1911 Darger clipped the picture of Elsie Paroubek, a five-year-old Chicago murder victim, from the Daily News, lost it, and turned the loss into the book's great grievance — the stolen picture of the child rebel Annie Aronburg.
  • People: Henry Darger — asylum escapee, hospital janitor, visionary; Elsie Paroubek — murdered child, 1911; William Schloeder — his only friend (from c. 1911).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Darger ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Developmental_Center
  • Confidence: High (Wikipedia, drawing on Jim Elledge's and John MacGregor's biographies). The rooming-house address (851 W. Webster Ave is commonly given) UNVERIFIED here.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (his 1917 draft service survives the break only if America drafted without entering the war — BENDS mildly: the owner may prefer a draft for the Southern War).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A janitor in a Catholic hospital who prays for children and draws a war against child-stealers in a locked room. The IHS finds him in 1936 because the Glandelinian uniforms match something real, down to the buttons.

The Lincoln State School and Colony — the state's warehouse of the "feeble-minded" — 1865/1878 to 2002

  • Where: Lincoln, Logan County, central Illinois (on the rail line between Chicago and St. Louis).
  • What happened: Founded in 1865 as an experimental school at Jacksonville, incorporated in 1871 as the Illinois Institution for the Education of Feeble-Minded Children, it settled permanently at Lincoln in 1878 as the Illinois Asylum for Feeble-Minded Children, later the Lincoln State School and Colony. It was "understaffed, unsanitary, and overcrowded"; residents who died "were often buried on the property and their deaths frequently went uninvestigated." In 1907 a boy named Frank Giroux had an epileptic seizure, fell onto a hot radiator and was badly burned — the public scandal forced a legislative investigation. It is where Henry Darger was sent in 1904. The institution survived into living memory of Pulitzer-winning exposés (Jack Dykinga's 1971 Sun-Times photographs) and closed in 2002 after reports of abuse, neglect and preventable deaths.
  • The odd detail: The dead were buried on the grounds and nobody asked why they died — for most of a century.
  • People: Frank Giroux — the burned boy of the 1907 scandal.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Developmental_Center ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Darger
  • Confidence: High on the character of the place; 1930s population and cemetery count UNVERIFIED (see Leads).
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The feeble-minded colony is where the state sends children the Juvenile Court cannot place and nobody will claim. Some of them are changelings — the fae leave theirs where no one will look closely — and the colony's burial ground has too many small graves for its death register.

Illinois never sterilised — it segregated: the 1915 feeble-minded commitment law and three failed bills (1925, 1929, 1933)

  • Where: Springfield (the General Assembly); Chicago's courts; the state schools at Lincoln and Dixon.
  • What happened: While Indiana (1907), Wisconsin, Michigan and dozens of other states passed compulsory sterilisation laws, Illinois never did. Sterilisation bills were introduced in 1925, 1929 and 1933 and failed each time. The only reported sterilisation in Illinois was in 1916, when a 65-year-old prisoner was offered the choice between prison and the operation. Instead, in May 1915 Illinois passed House Bills 654 and 655: HB 655 let a court permanently institutionalise anyone "a respectable expert deemed feebleminded" (on petition, with a court hearing and a physician's examination), and HB 654 empowered the state to build institutions to hold them. Illinois eugenicists argued that sexual segregation — locking the "unfit" away for life — was more effective and more humane than the knife. Chicago was nonetheless a eugenics centre: the Municipal Court's Psychopathic Laboratory printed Laughlin's model sterilisation law in 1922 (see R18).
  • The odd detail: The state that refused to sterilise instead made the sentence indefinite: a 1915 hearing could put a woman away for the whole of her fertile life.

Dr. Harry Haiselden and The Black Stork — the Bollinger baby, 12–17 Nov 1915; the film shown until as late as 1942 (living memory)

  • Where: The German-American Hospital, Chicago; cinemas nationwide.
  • What happened: On 12 November 1915 Dr. Harry J. Haiselden, chief surgeon of Chicago's German-American Hospital, examined a newborn with severe disabilities, Allan Bollinger, decided that surgery could save his life — and advised the parents to do nothing. The baby died five days later. Haiselden did the same with Grace Werder (2 Dec 1915), the daughter of William and Eva Meter (24 July 1917) and Paul Hodzima (Nov 1917, possibly after narcotics he prescribed). A coroner's jury cleared him. He then co-wrote and starred in a silent film dramatising the case, The Black Stork (1917); the National Board of Review nearly banned it and demanded 18 changes; re-released in 1918 as Are You Fit to Marry?, it "continued to appear in theaters until as late as 1942." The Chicago Medical Society moved against him and he was eventually expelled over his eugenics lectures. He died in Havana on 18 June 1919 of a cerebral haemorrhage.
  • The odd detail: A Chicago surgeon who let babies die played himself in a film about it — and the film was still in cinemas all through the 1930s.
  • People: Harry J. Haiselden — surgeon and eugenics propagandist; Allan Bollinger — the infant; Anna Bollinger — the mother (name UNVERIFIED here).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_J._Haiselden

Part 3 — Children: the Juvenile Court's pipeline

The Jack-Roller: one Chicago boy's route through every institution — Clifford Shaw's The Jack-Roller (published 1930)

  • Where: West Side, Chicago (the boy's haunts: around Halsted and Madison, Desplaines St, the Loop); then the institutions below.
  • What happened: In 1930 the University of Chicago sociologist Clifford R. Shaw published The Jack-Roller: A Delinquent Boy's Own Story, a life history written by a Polish-American boy called "Stanley" — some 250 typewritten pages — alongside his official record. It maps exactly how Chicago confined a child in the 1920s: picked up as a runaway at 6½ and put in the Juvenile Detention Home (repeatedly, as a holding pen before court); committed at 9 to the Chicago Parental School for truancy; at 9¾ to the St. Charles School for Boys for truancy and larceny, paroled at 11, recommitted at 12½ after running away from a farm placement ("This boy is an habitual runaway"); at 15 to the Illinois State Reformatory at Pontiac for burglary and "jack-rolling" (robbing drunks), one year; and at 16¾ to the Chicago House of Correction (the Bridewell), another year, released at 17⅔. At home, his stepmother beat him and asked the court to commit him. A follow-up, The Jack-Roller at Seventy (Jon Snodgrass, 1982), found the real man still alive.
  • The odd detail: The court record and the boy's own words sit side by side in the book — two versions of one life, the institution's and his.
  • People: Clifford R. Shaw — Institute for Juvenile Research sociologist; "Stanley" — the jack-roller; Jon Snodgrass — the 1982 follow-up.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/stream/jackrollerdelinq0000clif/jackrollerdelinq0000clif_djvu.txt (the 1930 text, record chapter) ; https://archive.org/advancedsearch.php?q=%22jack-roller%22&fl[]=identifier,title,date,creator&rows=20&output=json (edition list incl. the 1982 follow-up)

The Forty-Two Gang threatens to storm the St. Charles reformatory — 1928

  • Where: Illinois State School for Boys, St. Charles, Kane County (about 40 miles west of the Loop, on the Fox River).
  • What happened: In 1928, with members of the Near West Side's Forty-Two Gang held in the state boys' reformatory at St. Charles, the gang sent word: "Unless you let our pals go, we'll come down there and kill everybody we see." The school's commander, Major William J. Butler, laughed until Chicago police told him to take it seriously; he armed himself and called out the Illinois National Guard. Days later three juvenile gang members were caught outside the walls, led by "Crazy" Patsy Steffanelli; they said they were a scouting party sent to get machine gunners into the school. The Chicago Tribune asked whether such boys should go to Joliet or "the electric chair". The Forty-Two Gang (active c. 1925–34) was the Outfit's farm team — its most famous graduate was Sam Giancana.
  • The odd detail: A boys' reformatory run by a Major, defended by the National Guard against a raid by other boys.
  • People: William J. Butler — St. Charles's commander; "Crazy" Patsy Steffanelli — leader of the scouting party; Sam Giancana — the gang's most famous alumnus.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forty-Two_Gang

The county paid by the head: how Chicago's Juvenile Court filled the private "industrial schools" (system in place by 1918, running through the 1930s)

  • Where: The Juvenile Court and its Child Placing and Delinquent Boys' Departments (9th floor, County Building, 1918); the private institutions in the city and the suburbs.
  • What happened: Chicago did not run most of its children's institutions: it rented beds. The 1918 Chicago Social Service Directory shows the machinery plainly. Part 4 — The Bridewell, the convent reformatory and the maternity homes

The Bridewell: the House of Correction at 26th and California — opened 10 Aug 1871; 1930s city workhouse

  • Where: California Ave and 26th St (the boulevards Marshall and California run past its front), South Lawndale — the same ground where the new Cook County Jail and Criminal Courts rose in 1929; its Farm Colony was at Willow Springs (371.8 acres).

What the wards did to patients in the 1930s: fever, insulin, Metrazol, the continuous tub — Illinois practice, 1917–1941

  • Where: Chicago State Hospital (Dunning), Elgin, Kankakee, Jacksonville, Peoria; Cook County Hospital.
  • What happened: The decade's treatments arrived in order. Malaria fever therapy for general paresis (Wagner-Jauregg, 1917; Nobel Prize 1927) meant injecting a patient with malaria-infected blood to burn out neurosyphilis, then curing the malaria with quinine; an Illinois Medical Journal paper of Feb 1941 describes a series of paretics of whom 65 were treated with malaria, 50 with typhoid fever, 39 with both, warning that malaria is "drastic" and dangerous in deteriorated cases. Cook County Hospital used artificial fever machines for other diseases. Insulin coma (Sakel, published 1933; brought to the US in 1936) meant a course of 50–60 comas, six days a week for about two months, at a death rate of roughly 1–5%. Metrazol convulsions (Meduna, 1934) followed; by 1940 the American Journal of Psychiatry carried "Metrazol Shock Therapy, Elgin State Hospital" by Charles F. Read and the Elgin staff, and a paper noting that at the Chicago State Hospital they had begun to pre-medicate patients because Metrazol's worst effects were "fear and fractures"; a Jacksonville paper of 1941 studied "the element of fear in Metrazol therapy". Hydrotherapy — wet packs and the "continuous tub" in which an agitated patient was held in warm water for hours — was standard: Kankakee's 1910 hospital building was built for it and a "safe continuous tub" was designed in Illinois in the 1920s. Lobotomy reached America only in 1936 (see R13).
  • The odd detail: In an Illinois state hospital of the 1930s a doctor could give a man malaria on purpose, and another could give him typhoid on purpose, as treatment.
  • People: Charles F. Read — Elgin's managing officer (earlier Dunning's superintendent; later the name of Chicago-Read); Julius Wagner-Jauregg; Manfred Sakel; Ladislas Meduna.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malariotherapy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insulin_shock_therapy ; archive.org full-text snippets of American Journal of Psychiatry vol. 96–97 indexes (items sim_american-journal-of-psychiatry_1940-1941_97_index, sim_american-journal-of-psychiatry_1940-09_97_2), Illinois Medical Journal Feb 1941 (sim_illinois-medical-journal_1941-02_79_2), Diseases of the Nervous System June 1941 (sim_journal-of-clinical-psychiatry_1941-06_2_6), Institution Quarterly 1925 (institutionquart101919illi)
  • Confidence: High on the treatments themselves; Medium on the Illinois specifics (read as search snippets, not full articles) — which hospital gave which treatment in which year is UNVERIFIED in detail.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — mildly: the treatments came from Vienna (Wagner-Jauregg, Sakel) and Budapest (Meduna); in a world where Germany and Austria-Hungary won, Vienna's psychiatry would be even more prestigious and its doctors would not have fled after 1933–38 — Sakel's 1936 move to New York may need another cause.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Fever therapy as an exorcism no one names: a Kankakee doctor notices that malaria-burned patients stop "hearing the other voices" — and begins inducing fever in patients who are not paretic at all.

The state reform schools: St. Charles for boys, Geneva for girls — 1904/1882 onward; "unwalled" escapes of 1939

  • Where: St. Charles School for Boys, St. Charles, Kane County; State Training School for Girls, Geneva, Kane County — two towns a few miles apart on the Fox River, ~40 miles west of the Loop.
  • What happened: In 1918 St. Charles (a State institution, "for custody and training of delinquent boys between ages of 10 and 17 years committed by Courts") had a capacity of 800; Geneva (established 1882; managing officer Dr. Clara Hayes) held delinquent girls 10 to 18, who "may be kept in school until they are 21", capacity 500. Both were cottage campuses, not walled prisons — which is why the Forty-Two Gang could threaten to storm St. Charles in 1928 (see Part 3) and why escapes were chronic: the Tribune of 2 Nov 1939 headlined boys who "ATTACK GUARD, FLEE UNWALLED" school — "the most dangerous of the young criminals in the institution" overpowered "a husky guard" — noting how many had escaped that year under superintendent William T. Harmon. Harmon later ran the school for years; St. Charles went on to hold the founders of several Chicago street gangs.
  • The odd detail: "Unwalled" was the reformers' point of pride — and the newspapers' headline word every time a boy walked away.
  • People: Clara Hayes — Geneva's managing officer (1918); G. Charles Griffiths — St. Charles's managing officer (1918); William T. Harmon — St. Charles superintendent (1939).
  • Sources: https://archive.org/stream/chicagosocialser00chic/chicagosocialser00chic_djvu.txt (pp. 71–72) ; Chicago Daily Tribune 2 Nov 1939 (item per_chicago-daily-tribune_1939-11-02_98_262, snippet only) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forty-Two_Gang ; https://en.wikipedia.org/w/api.php?action=query&list=search&srsearch=%22St.+Charles%22+Illinois+%22school+for+boys%22&format=json&srlimit=10 (William T. Harmon; later gang founders)
  • Confidence: High for 1918 data; Medium on the 1939 escape (snippet); St. Charles's founding year printed "1804" in the scan is an OCR error for 1904 (UNVERIFIED exact year).
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Two reform schools four miles apart, boys and girls, and the Fox River between them. Colonel Fabyan's Riverbank estate (R29) is on the same river at Geneva; a Geneva girl who ran away in 1936 was found on the Riverbank grounds and would not say what she had heard there.
