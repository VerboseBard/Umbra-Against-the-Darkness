R13 — Science, forensics, medicine, radio & media, weather & disasters: Chicago and the Great Lakes, 1929–1939

  • Lane: R13 — the real period tools of investigation (forensics, police science, the coroner), the science establishment (physics, astronomy, psychical research), medicine (hospitals, epidemics, radium, quacks), radio and print media (horror radio born in Chicago, the crime press, early TV), and weather/disasters (heat, dust, cold, storms, crashes).

  • Model: Claude Fable 5.1 (research agent, one of 16 in the sweep)

Overview

Sixty-five entries. The investigators' toolkit comes out unusually concrete: from 1929 to 1938 the best crime laboratory in America was a private one on Superior Street, paid for by a soap fortune and open to any sheriff who shipped it a bullet, and its lie-detector business outgrew its ballistics by 1932; the county coroner was a machine politician with a six-man jury picked that morning [UNVERIFIED — V_R13]; wiretap evidence was freely usable in federal court from Olmstead (1928) until the Communications Act's §605 (1934) forbade intercepting and divulging calls, and Nardone (20 Dec 1937) barred federal agents' taps as evidence ⟨fixed: V_R13⟩; police radio was one-way (the car could be sent but could not answer) until 1939 [UNVERIFIED — V_R13]; Chicago drank unfiltered lake water [UNVERIFIED — V_R13] drawn through cribs with keepers living on them; and the whole city's drinking, listening and schooling ran through a handful of choke points — one Merchandise Mart floor for network radio, one hotel plumbing plug for a 400-city epidemic, six radio stations for every schoolchild in September 1937. The science establishment is thick with usable strangeness (Compton's cosmic-ray chambers going up on the fair's balloon and coming down in a rail yard; Zenith's president running a national telepathy experiment over the air in 1937; Rhine himself a Chicago PhD; a spiritualist camp in Indiana raided in 1925 and open every summer after). Medicine gives the two best addresses in the file — the first blood bank (Harrison Street, 15 Mar 1937) and the AMA's nostrum files on Dearborn — and the Radium Girls of Ottawa, the "society of the living dead." Media gives horror radio born at midnight on WENR in January 1934, the Detroit masked men, and a newspaper tower studded with stones from the world's sacred buildings. Disasters cluster in 1929 (a car ferry with no radio, a clinic's X-ray film turned to phosgene), 1933 (the fair's dysentery, the first airliner bombing, over Indiana), 1936 (the killing cold, then the killing heat, then the owner's eclipse), and 1938 (the Niagara bridge, the radium hearings). The strongest BENDS are a single thread: German imports — the Zeiss sky at the Adler, the Deutsches Museum model for the Museum of Science and Industry, the Zeppelin engineers at Akron, the Berlin analysts on Michigan Avenue, the Graf Zeppelin over the fair — all of which, in a Germany-won world, arrive as the victor's patronage rather than as refugees or curiosities; and Otto Struve at Yerkes, who in a no-Soviet world may never have left Russia.

1. Forensics and police science

The Scientific Crime Detection Laboratory, Northwestern University — Fall 1929 to 1938

  • Where: first at 469 Ohio Street (later renumbered; the Northwestern downtown campus), then 222 East Superior Street, Chicago; bought by the city in 1938 but moved from Superior Street to police headquarters, 1121 South State Street, only in 1941 ⟨fixed: V_R13⟩.
  • What happened: After the St. Valentine's Day Massacre (14 Feb 1929) the coroner's jury foreman, Burt A. Massee (Palmolive money), paid for a private crime laboratory attached to Northwestern's law school — "the first institution of its kind in the United States." Calvin Goddard, the Army-officer ballistics expert who had proved the massacre guns were not police Thompsons, ran it from 1929 (director as of 1932; he left c. 1933–34). Departments: firearms, chemistry/toxicology, document examination, photography, and a "psychology" department under Leonarde Keeler (the polygraph). It examined blood, bullets, fingerprints, handwriting, poisons, textiles and "60+ other" evidence types for police forces across the Midwest, and published The American Journal of Police Science from 1930 (merged into the Journal of Criminal Law and Criminology in 1932). In 1938 the City of Chicago bought the whole outfit for $25,000; it moved from 222 E. Superior to police headquarters, 1121 S. State, in 1941 ⟨fixed: V_R13⟩; Fred E. Inbau stayed on to train police. Facilities at the transfer: two chemical laboratories, a photographic darkroom, a polygraph chamber, a document examiner's room, a library of 1,000+ volumes, and an exhibits room with hundreds of weapons.
  • The odd detail: the lab was private for nine years — paid for by a soap fortune, housed in a law school, and any sheriff in the Midwest could ship it a bullet. A player investigator in 1934 would go to Superior Street, not to a police station.
  • People: Calvin Goddard — Army colonel, comparison-microscope ballistics; Burt A. Massee — Palmolive executive, coroner's-jury foreman, funder; Leonarde Keeler — polygraph; Fred E. Inbau — b. 1909 New Orleans, Tulane law, then Northwestern; on the lab staff from the early 1930s and its director after the 1938 police purchase (later co-author of Criminal Interrogations and Confessions and of the Reid technique; taught at Northwestern 32 years; d. 1998) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_E._Inbau ; Clarence Muehlberger — toxicologist (UNVERIFIED this session).
  • Sources: https://chicagology.com/chicagopolice/crimelab/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin_Hooker_Goddard (search result, not fetched)
  • Confidence: High on founding, addresses, 1938 sale and price; Medium on Goddard's exact departure year (sources give 1933 or 1934).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (Goddard's Army career touched the Great War — he served as an Ordnance officer; in a no-US-in-WWI world his résumé shifts but the lab need not.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The private lab is the perfect civilian door for Section 13 — an FBI field office can "consult" it without a police record. Put a locked drawer in the exhibits room: bullets that came back from the comparison microscope with marks no gun makes. ⟨fixed: factions⟩

Goddard, Fred Burke's Thompsons and the birth of ballistics — Dec 1929 to Apr 1931

  • Where: St. Joseph, Michigan (Broad and State Streets) and Burke's house on Lake Shore Drive just south of Glenlord Road, Stevensville, Berrien County, Michigan; the guns went to Goddard in Chicago.
  • What happened: On 14 Dec 1929 patrolman Charles Skelly of St. Joseph stopped a man after a minor traffic collision; the driver shot him three times and Skelly died at 11:10 p.m. The driver was Fred "Killer" Burke, living as "Fred Dane." The raid on his lakeside house turned up ammunition, three bulletproof vests, revolvers, sawed-off shotguns, hand grenades, tear-gas bombs, two Thompson submachine guns and about $390,000 in bonds stolen from a Jefferson, Wisconsin bank. The Thompsons were driven to Calvin Goddard in Chicago, who matched them by comparison microscope to the St. Valentine's Day Massacre casings — and to the July 1928 killing of Frankie Yale in Brooklyn. Burke was caught on 26 Mar 1931 in Green City, Missouri, pleaded guilty to second-degree murder on 27 Apr 1931 (Judge Charles E. White, life), and died at Marquette State Penitentiary on 10 Jul 1940 of a heart attack. He was never tried for the massacre.
  • The odd detail: the guns that ended the "police did it" rumour turned up by accident, in a fender-bender in a Michigan resort town, in a house with trap doors and a shelf of detective novels.
  • People: Fred Burke — St. Louis "Egan's Rats" gunman, Capone contract killer; Charles Skelly — St. Joseph patrolman, 25, killed; Calvin Goddard — ballistics; Frankie Yale — Brooklyn boss killed 1928 with the same guns.
  • Sources: https://www.berriencounty.org/419/Fred-Burke---Charles-Skelly ; https://stvalentinemassacre.org/ballistics/
  • Confidence: High. (The Berrien County page says Yale was killed "July 1929"; the standard date is 1 July 1928 — flagged.)
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Two guns that killed in Brooklyn and Chicago and hid in a Michigan lake house — a Golden Circle courier route in miniature. The occult layer: what else was in the bulletproof vests' lining.

Leonarde Keeler's polygraph and its first courtroom — 1930 to 1938 (Portage, Wisconsin, 2 Feb 1935)

  • Where: Northwestern's crime lab (Chicago); Columbia County courthouse, Portage, Wisconsin; later the Keeler Institute, Chicago.
  • What happened: Keeler (b. 30 Oct 1903, Berkeley; trained under Chief August Vollmer) came to Chicago in 1930 with his instrument — his first handmade device, the 1924 "Emotograph," had burned in a house fire. He ran the lab's "psychology" work and became laboratory head in 1936. On 2 Feb 1935 in Portage, Wisconsin, Judge Clayton F. Van Pelt let Keeler test two men, Cecil Loniello and Tony Grignano, accused of shooting at a sheriff; by stipulation of both sides the charts went before the jury — the first time polygraph results were admitted in a U.S. court. Both were convicted of assault. Keeler tested Grace Yvonne Loomis in 1937 over the death of her five-year-old son in Lombard, Illinois, and in 1938 tested Dr. Francis Sweeney, the Cleveland Torso suspect, who failed. He left Northwestern in 1938 for private business and the Keeler Institute, the first polygraph school. He played himself in Call Northside 777 (1948) and died in Door County, Wisconsin on 20 Sep 1949, aged 45.
  • The odd detail: his wife Katherine Applegate Keeler (m. 1930) became the country's first female handwriting examiner — the lab's document work and its lie-detection were, for a few years, a married couple.
  • People: Leonarde Keeler — polygraph; Katherine Applegate Keeler — questioned-documents examiner, later a WASP pilot, died 1944; Clayton F. Van Pelt — Wisconsin circuit judge; Cecil Loniello and Tony Grignano — defendants; August Vollmer — Berkeley chief, Keeler's mentor.
  • The instrument and the volume: Keeler's patent was filed 30 Jul 1925 and issued 13 Jan 1931 (No. 1,788,434); the machine — blood pressure, pulse and breathing traces on a moving chart, with a galvanic skin-response channel added in the early-to-mid 1930s — packed into a case about 16 × 8 × 9 inches and cost about $1,500, built first by Western Electro Mechanical and from 1936 by Associated Research, Inc. of Chicago. By 1932 lie-detection had overtaken ballistics as the lab's main business; Keeler ran 2,171+ examinations in 1935–38 alone. (The trade-history page that gives these figures dates the Keeler Polygraph Institute to 1948, against Wikipedia's 1938 — the 1938 date is his departure from Northwestern; the school is later.) Ness brought him to Cleveland for the Sweeney test.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonarde_Keeler ; https://liedetectortest.com/polygraph-history/leonarde-keeler-northwest-crime-lab-chicago-years (a polygraph-trade history page — figures plausible, marketing tone; treat as Medium) ; https://www.edn.com/polygraph-first-used-to-get-a-conviction-february-2-1935/ (search result)
  • Confidence: High on dates and the Portage case; Medium on "laboratory head in 1936," the exam counts and the instrument prices.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (Cleveland Torso Murders are already in the setting — the 1938 Sweeney test is a direct bridge.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A polygraph chart that goes flat when the subject is asked about the full moon. Keeler's chamber on Superior Street is where Section 13 learns that some things do not sweat.

Chicago's police radio: WGN experiments to WPDB — Mar 1929 to 1939

  • Where: Police Headquarters (1121 S. State St.); the Tribune's WGN; three police transmitters by end of 1930.
  • What happened: On 17 Mar 1929 the Chief of Detectives' office broadcast a test call over WGN to three radio-equipped squads ("To squads 1, 37A and 38A"): one answered from Wentworth and 22nd in two minutes, one parked at Wieboldt's (School St. and N. Ashland) in thirty seconds, and the third had to walk five blocks to a phone. Col. McCormick's Tribune put up $10,000; Commissioner William F. Russell fitted the cars. The bulletins interrupted WGN's programmes "several times each night," crowds beat the police to crime scenes, and criminals listened for their getaways. The City Council authorised a police-owned system on 10 Jul 1929; on 16 Jun 1930 the first police station opened, with Zenith's E. F. McDonald Jr. heading the technical committee. By the end of 1930 the department ran three stations (WPDB, WPDC, WPDD on 1714 kHz) with 96 radio cars reaching scenes in three to five minutes, and a centralised Complaint Room at headquarters. Two-way radio came only in 1939 [UNVERIFIED — V_R13].
  • The odd detail: for a year every Chicagoan with a radio could hear the police dispatch between dance numbers — and did.
  • People: Robert R. McCormick — Tribune publisher; William F. Russell — police commissioner; E. F. (Eugene) McDonald Jr. — Zenith president, later the telepathy-broadcast sponsor (see §3).
  • Sources: https://chicagology.com/chicagopolice/policecarradio/ ; https://chicagology.com/chicagopolice/history/ (search result)
  • Confidence: High on dates; Medium on the call signs/frequency (from a hobbyist radio-history page in search results, not fetched).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): One-way radio only, until 1939 [UNVERIFIED — V_R13]: a squad car can be sent but cannot answer. A dispatcher who keeps sending cars to an address that does not exist.

2. The coroner, the morgue, the autopsy

The Cook County Coroner's office — Wolff, Bundesen, Walsh, 1922–1940

  • Where: Cook County; the morgue and inquest rooms at the county hospital complex on the West Side (Harrison/Polk St. area — exact 1930s address UNVERIFIED this session).
  • What happened: The Cook County Coroner was an elected politician, not a doctor: Oscar Wolff (Republican, 4 Dec 1922–Dec 1928), then Dr. Herman N. Bundesen (Democrat, Dec 1928–18 Nov 1931, who ran the St. Valentine's Day inquest and resigned to become city Health Commissioner), then Frank J. Walsh (Democrat, appointed 18 Nov 1931, elected 1932 and 1938, died in office 12 Feb 1940). The office was "riddled with patronage and corruption" and coroner's juries "often acted as a rubber-stamp" for police and prosecutors. Deaths were ruled by a six-man coroner's jury at an inquest [UNVERIFIED — V_R13]; the coroner's physician (a pathologist) did the cutting. The coroner system survived until 6 Dec 1976, when an appointed medical examiner replaced it.
  • The odd detail: the man who ruled on every violent death in Chicago from 1931 to 1940 was Frank J. Walsh, a machine Democrat with no medical training, sitting between a Republican and a publicity-hungry doctor who once drew a pistol on a dumper with a pickaxe.
  • People: Oscar Wolff — coroner 1922–28; Herman N. Bundesen — physician, coroner 1928–31, health commissioner 1922–28 and 1931–60; Frank J. Walsh — coroner 1931–40.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cook_County_Medical_Examiner ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Bundesen
  • Confidence: High on names and dates; Low on morgue address and inquest mechanics (Encyclopedia of Chicago's "Cook County Morgue" page would not resolve this session — see Leads).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A coroner's jury is six citizens picked that morning [UNVERIFIED — V_R13]. The Golden Circle does not need to own the coroner; it needs to own the bailiff who picks the six.

Dr. Herman Bundesen, the doctor who ran for governor in a monocle — 1922–1960 (peak 1928–1936)

  • Where: Chicago Board of Health; Cook County Coroner's office.
  • What happened: Bundesen was Chicago's Health Commissioner 1 Feb 1922–Jan 1928 and again 1931–1960, Cook County Coroner Dec 1928–Nov 1931, and President of the Board of Health from 1932. He drove dairy inspections from about 4,300 a year (1920) to 56,127 (1928), cut diphtheria and infant mortality, pushed venereal-disease clinics and prophylactics (denounced as immoral), and opposed the city's first birth-control clinic in 1923. As coroner he chased the cameras after every big murder, the massacre above all. In 1936 he challenged Governor Henry Horner for the Democratic nomination, won Cook County and lost the state — campaigning in monocle and spats and demonstrating his abdominal muscles. He died of pancreatic cancer on 25 Aug 1960.
  • The odd detail: the coroner of the St. Valentine's Day Massacre wrote best-selling baby-care books and posed for muscle photographs.
  • People: Herman N. Bundesen — as above; Henry Horner — Governor of Illinois 1933–40.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Bundesen
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A health commissioner who inspects 56,000 dairies a year sees every farm in the collar counties. His inspectors are the best-placed witnesses in the region for anything that happens in a barn at night.

4. Medicine: hospitals, epidemics, radium, quacks

The first blood bank — Cook County Hospital, 15 Mar 1937

  • Where: Cook County Hospital, 1835 W. Harrison Street, Chicago.
  • What happened: Dr. Bernard Fantus (b. Budapest 1 Sep 1874, MD Chicago 1899), director of the hospital's Department of Pharmacology and Therapeutics, opened the first hospital blood bank in the United States on 15 Mar 1937. It cost $1,500 to set up [UNVERIFIED — V_R13] and handled 1,354 transfusions in its first year; the hospital had been doing about 40 transfusions a month, and within three months was doing 400 [UNVERIFIED — V_R13]. Fantus published the working plan in the Journal of the American Medical Association on 10 Jul 1937 so any hospital could copy it. He had read Russian and Spanish reports on storing blood. Earlier (1910–15) he had invented "candy medication" — sweet tablets for children — and his 1915 book of formulas included aspirin, heroin and cocaine. He died 14 Apr 1940.
  • The odd detail: the phrase "blood bank" was his daughter Ruth's — "everyone in society knows how a bank works." Blood was literally deposited and withdrawn: a donor's family earned credit against a later need.
  • People: Bernard Fantus — Hungarian-Jewish physician; Ruth Fantus — adopted daughter, coined the term; Emily Senn Fantus — nurse, his wife.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Fantus ; https://cookcountyhealth.org/press-release/cchhs-celebrates-75th-anniversary-worlds-blood-bank/ (search result)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (Fantus cited Spanish Civil War blood-storage work; in a no-Soviet world the "Russian publications" he read may not exist — minor BEND.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A refrigerator on Harrison Street holding ten days of stored blood, credited by name, opened in March 1937 — the single most vampire-relevant address in the city. Who made the first large "deposit," and why did the ledger not balance?

The Radium Girls of Ottawa, Illinois — 1922 to 27 Jul 1938

  • Where: Radium Dial Company, 1022 Columbus Street, Ottawa, Illinois (a former high-school building), about 80 miles southwest of Chicago; hearings before the Illinois Industrial Commission, some at Catherine Donohue's bedside.
  • What happened: Radium Dial (founded 1917, in Ottawa by 1922; ~1,000 workers and 4,300 dials a day by 1925) had girls "lip-point" their brushes and paint their teeth and nails with the paint for fun — told it was harmless. Catherine Wolfe Donohue fell ill from 1925 and was fired in 1931 because "my limping was causing much talk." She and four other women sued under the state occupational-disease law; the company appealed at every step. By 1938 Donohue was carried to the hearings on a stretcher and testified lying down, barely above a whisper; when a doctor told her in the hearing room that she was dying she collapsed, and the hearing continued at her home. On 6 Jul 1938 the Industrial Commission threw out the company's appeal and awarded her an additional $730 [UNVERIFIED — V_R13]. She died 27 Jul 1938. The company's head, Joseph A. Kelly Sr., simply reopened "a few blocks away" as Luminous Processes Inc., which painted radium dials until 1978.
  • The odd detail: the Illinois State Journal of 6 Apr 1938 called the plaintiffs "the society of the living dead." Margaret "Peg" Looney, who started at 17 and died at 24, was told by the company she had diphtheria and was quarantined in the company hospital. Donohue weighed under 60 pounds when she died; it was Peg Looney's body, not Donohue's, that was exhumed in 1978 (the year Luminous Processes closed) for study at Argonne National Laboratory ⟨fixed: V_R13⟩. The successor firm, a few blocks away, painted dials for forty more years and was fined in 1976 for radiation 1,666 times the allowable level.
  • The case, more exactly: Chicago attorney Leonard Grossman took the women pro bono before the Illinois Industrial Commission (created 1917); the hearing was continued at Donohue's home in February 1938; the women won and the company appealed all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court, which refused it — but Radium Dial had already fled to New York and paid only about $10,000 collectively. Joseph Kelly was ousted from Radium Dial in 1934 and founded Luminous Processes. The case produced the Illinois Occupational Disease Act.
  • People: Catherine Wolfe Donohue — lead plaintiff, d. 27 Jul 1938; Margaret "Peg" Looney — d. at 24; Beatrice Workman — Park Ridge, d. 1959; Leonard Grossman — Chicago attorney, pro bono; Joseph A. Kelly Sr. — Radium Dial, then Luminous Processes.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium_Dial_Company ; https://www.nprillinois.org/equity-justice/2018-01-25/the-radium-girls-an-illinois-tragedy
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (Radium Dial's Chicago origin was the Marshall Field Annex — a Loop address for a GM.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Women whose bones shine in the dark, dying in a river town, in a building that was a school. The "three layers": the period reads it as industrial poisoning; folklore says the girls glow in their graves; underneath, something in the paint was never radium.

5. Radio and media

"Lights Out" — horror radio is born at midnight on WENR — 3 Jan 1934 onward

  • Where: NBC's Chicago studios (Merchandise Mart — UNVERIFIED that early episodes originated there), broadcast on WENR, Chicago, at midnight Wednesdays.
  • What happened: NBC staff writer Wyllis Cooper conceived "a midnight mystery serial to catch the attention of the listeners at the witching hour" and put it on WENR, Chicago, from 3 Jan 1934 as a fifteen-minute show; by April 1934 it was thirty minutes, and on 17 Apr 1935 it went coast-to-coast on NBC. Cooper wrote about 120 scripts through mid-1936, when Arch Oboler took over (first script "Burial Services," Jun 1936 — over 50,000 complaint letters, after which NBC banned the show before 11 p.m.). The opening: a deep voice, "Lights out, everybody!", then thirteen chimes. Sound men built a real gallows for one episode; Oboler dictated scripts into a Dictaphone in bed, some in thirty minutes. "Chicken Heart" (1937, a heart that grows to engulf the world, thump-thump) and "Catwife" became the famous ones. By mid-1936 there were about 600 fan clubs. It ran, on and off, to 1947.
  • The odd detail: the first true American horror programme aired only at midnight, from Chicago, and its author begged listeners with weak nerves to switch off before it started.
  • People: Wyllis Cooper — creator, 1934–36; Arch Oboler — writer-director from June 1936, Chicago-born.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lights_Out_(radio_show)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (Oboler's later "anti-fascist" themes lose their target in a no-Nazi world — his villains would be different.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A Wednesday-midnight broadcast that the whole city listens to in the dark is the perfect cover for a working: 600 fan clubs are 600 cells. Section 13 gets a letter from a listener who heard a voice under the chimes.

6. Weather and disasters

United Air Lines Trip 23 — the first proven bombing of an airliner — 10 Oct 1933

  • Where: James Smiley's farm, Jackson Township, about 5 miles from Chesterton, Porter County, Indiana (Chicago-bound).
  • What happened: A Boeing 247, NC13304, flying Newark–Cleveland–Chicago–Oakland, exploded at about 1,000 feet shortly after 9 p.m. and burned in a field. Three crew died — the pilot (Capt. Terrant), co-pilot A. T. Ruby, and stewardess Alice Scribner, the first United stewardess killed — and four passengers. Porter County coroner Dr. Carl Davis and experts from the Northwestern crime laboratory established that a nitroglycerin device in the rear lavatory/baggage area had blown the tail off: "everything in front of the compartment was blown forward, everything behind blown backward," in the words of Melvin Purvis, who ran the Bureau of Investigation inquiry from Chicago. A brown package and a rifle in the wreck were cleared. No suspect and no motive were ever found; the FBI released 324 pages on 16 Nov 2017. It is the first proven act of sabotage against a commercial aircraft.
  • The odd detail: the crime lab's chemists worked the crater in a cornfield — the bomb case was the Northwestern lab's, and it never closed.
  • People: Melvin Purvis — Chicago SAC of the Bureau of Investigation; Alice Scribner — stewardess; Carl Davis — Porter County coroner.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Air_Lines_Flight_23 ; https://www.nbcchicago.com/news/local/80-years-later-plane-bombing-remains-a-mystery/1964534/ (search result)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (In the setting Purvis's Bureau is K. Edgar Hoover's FBI, whose Section 13 is the supernatural division; the Secret Service is a separate rival.) ⟨fixed: factions — the Secret Service and FBI Section 13 are separate; K. Edgar Hoover runs the FBI⟩
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Seven dead in a cornfield, a nitroglycerin bomb, no suspect. Who was the fourth passenger, and what was in the baggage compartment that had to be destroyed before it reached Chicago?

Fingerprints replace Bertillon in Chicago — 1 Jan 1905, first conviction upheld 1910–11

  • Where: Chicago Police Bureau of Identification, City Hall / Police Headquarters.
  • What happened: Chicago adopted fingerprint identification on 1 January 1905, replacing the Bertillon system of body measurements (head length, ear, forearm) that the city had used since the 1890s — Chicago, Cleveland, Detroit, Buffalo and Toledo were all in the ten-city Bertillon association of 1898, and from 1897 to 1902 the National Bureau of Criminal Identification, the police chiefs' national mugshot-and-measurement file, sat in Chicago's City Hall before moving to Washington. The fingerprint method was "ridiculed" until a murderer left prints at a crime scene in 1910; the Illinois Supreme Court upheld the prints as evidence and the conviction stood — the first such ruling in an American appellate court. By the 1930s the Bertillon legacy survived as the mugshot, the scars-and-tattoos card, and the habit of recording height and eye colour, while the card index of prints was the working tool.
  • The odd detail: the national criminal-identification file was a Chicago institution for five years; the national mugshot archive was born on LaSalle Street.

Wiretapping: legal, then not — Olmstead (4 Jun 1928) to Nardone (1937)

  • Where: national law, applied in Chicago's federal court (the Capone-era Prohibition cases).
  • What happened: In Olmstead v. United States (decided 4 Jun 1928, 5–4, Chief Justice Taft) the Supreme Court held that federal agents who tapped Seattle bootlegger Roy Olmstead's telephones without a warrant had made no "search" — "There was no searching. There was no seizure. The evidence was secured by the use of the sense of hearing and that only." Justice Brandeis dissented that a telephone call was like a sealed letter and that government lawbreaking "breeds contempt for law." For the whole early 1930s, then, a federal or city investigator could tap a line freely and use it in court. The Communications Act of 1934 (section 605) and the Supreme Court's Nardone decision of 1937 barred wiretap evidence in federal courts (UNVERIFIED this session — from memory; the Olmstead page mentions only the 1967 Katz reversal).
  • The odd detail: the tap was legal because a wire is not a room.
  • People: William Howard Taft — Chief Justice; Louis Brandeis — dissenter; Roy Olmstead — Seattle bootlegger with 50 employees and a fleet.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olmstead_v._United_States
  • Confidence: High on Olmstead; Medium on the 1934/1937 sequel.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Between 1928 and 1934 Section 13 can tap any telephone in Chicago and use it in federal court; from 1934 §605 makes intercepting and divulging a call a federal offence, and from Nardone (Dec 1937) federal courts refuse the evidence ⟨fixed: V_R13⟩. Yerkes Observatory under Otto Struve — Williams Bay, Wisconsin, 1932 onward

Arthur Compton's cosmic-ray survey and the Millikan feud — 1931–1936

  • Where: Ryerson Physical Laboratory, University of Chicago; expeditions worldwide.
  • What happened: Compton (Nobel 1927 for the Compton effect, at Chicago from 1923) organised a world survey of cosmic-ray intensity: eight cooperating teams with identical ionisation chambers measured in Greenland, Denmark, India, Ceylon, Java, Tibet, South Africa, Eritrea, Spitsbergen and Switzerland; Compton himself covered 50,000 miles — a Hawaiian volcano, Mt Cook in New Zealand, Panama, Peru — and flew within 350 miles of the north magnetic pole. Two members of the 1932 Mt McKinley cosmic-ray party died on the mountain. The result (about 15–20% more intensity at the poles than the equator at sea level) showed cosmic rays were charged particles, contradicting Robert Millikan's "birth cries of atoms" photon theory; the two men had a public altercation at the December 1932 Atlantic City meeting, and by 1936 Millikan had "steadily, almost imperceptibly, retreated." Compton was a Presbyterian minister's son who served as a Baptist deacon and lectured on science and God.
  • The odd detail: the men who died on McKinley were carrying an ionisation chamber up a mountain to measure rays from beyond the solar system, in 1932, for a Chicago professor.
  • People: Arthur H. Compton — physicist, U of Chicago; Robert A. Millikan — Caltech rival; Karl T. Compton — brother, MIT president 1930–48.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Compton ; https://time.com/archive/6754878/science-cosmic-clearance/ (13 Jan 1936)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Compton's Manhattan Project role is post-window).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Cosmic rays are strongest near the magnetic poles and at altitude; a Chicago physicist's chamber logs a spike over a certain building on the Near North Side that has no altitude at all.

The ESP craze: Rhine, the Zener deck, and Zenith's radio telepathy — 1934–1939

  • Where: Duke University (Durham, N.C.); Zenith Radio Corporation, Chicago; Northwestern University, Evanston.
  • What happened: J. B. His stars were undergraduates Adam Linzmayer (1931: two perfect nine-card runs, 39.6% on 300 cards against 20% chance) and Hubert Pearce (1932: about 40%) — Pearce was usually allowed to shuffle and cut the cards himself. The World's Fair dysentery: a wooden plug and two hotels — Jun 1933 to Jun 1934 (also in 09-22 archive: H01)

  • Where: the Congress Hotel and the Auditorium Hotel, South Michigan Avenue at Congress Street, Chicago.
  • What happened: Beyond the archive entry: the federal-state investigation (Chicago Board of Health, the city Division of Water Purification, the U.S. Public Health Service and the State University of Iowa's preventive-medicine department) traced 1,409 cases and 98 deaths between 1 Jun 1933 and 30 Jun 1934, spread to over 400 cities in 43 states, Hawaii and three Canadian provinces — the first recognised water-borne outbreak of amoebic dysentery and the only large civilian epidemic of it on record. The mechanism: a wooden plug in a lateral sewer above the Congress's cooling tank — so loose "it was easily removed by the finger tips" — plus corroded cross-connections between the two hotels' water systems and their refrigeration condensers. On 29 Jun 1933 a storm dropped 1.56 inches between 5:10 and 8:20 p.m., and during its heaviest 47 minutes an estimated 9.4 gallons of sewage could have drained into the tank ⟨fixed: V_R13⟩; a broken street sewer on 2 Jul relieved pressure and probably prevented worse. Infections peaked 13–22 Aug (22 Aug was the worst single day) and again around 17 Sep, 3 Oct and 11 Oct, tracking the hotels' occupancy and the condensers' running. A convention of lumbermen present on 29 June gave 20 cases and 5 deaths; 11 hotel employees were infected. The plug was found on 8 Feb 1934. The 12–30-day incubation meant most victims sickened at home in other states, which is why nobody in Chicago saw it for two months.
  • The odd detail: a single wooden plug a fingertip could remove; the fair drew 22,565,859 visitors in its first season, about 8.5 million from out of town, and Chicago exported the disease to 400 towns.
  • The cover-up question: the outbreak began in mid-August but was revealed publicly only in November 1933, as the fair closed; by February 1934 the count then stood at 721 cases and 41 deaths nationwide (the final federal tally above is nearly double). Dr. Clarence Boren and his wife, infected at the Congress, sued Bundesen and the hotel for $300,000 each, alleging they "wilfully and wantonly" suppressed the news to protect the fair and the hotel's business. The U.S. Public Health Service's Dr. Roscoe R. Spencer praised Bundesen's "promptness, aggressiveness and thoroughness"; the Chicago Medical Society charged him with "negligence" and "petty politics" — "the mixing of political ambitions with the serious matter of health protection." Many victims died before their home-town doctors recognised a "comparatively rare ailment."
  • People: Herman Bundesen — President of the Board of Health (see §2); Roscoe R. Spencer — U.S. Public Health Service; Clarence Boren — physician-plaintiff.
  • Sources: https://www.edmcdevitt.org/the-plague-at-the-chicago-1933-century-of-progress/ (summarising the federal report, H. Doc. 74-376) ; https://time.com/archive/6894431/medicine-act-iii/ (Time, 1934) ; https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/SERIALSET-10041_00_00-002-0376-0000 (search result)

Brinkley the goat-gland doctor: a Chicago diploma, a million-watt station — 1907 to 1939

  • Where: Bennett Medical College, Chicago (1907–09); Milford, Kansas; Del Rio, Texas / Villa Acuña, Mexico.
  • What happened: Brinkley attended Chicago's unaccredited Bennett Medical College (eclectic medicine) in 1907–09 and bought his final year at the Kansas City Eclectic Medical University for $100, graduating 7 May 1915. His goat-testicle transplants cost $750 and by 1930 were advertised for "27 ailments, ranging from dementia to emphysema to flatulence"; he had signed 42 death certificates when Kansas revoked his licence in 1930 ("organized charlatanism ... quite beyond the invention of the humble mountebank") and the Federal Radio Commission pulled his station KFKB six months later. He ran for governor of Kansas as a write-in in 1930 (180,000 votes, 29.5%, tens of thousands of ballots thrown out for misspelling) and 1932 (244,607, 30.6%, losing to Alf Landon), then built XER/XERA across the Rio Grande — 50,000 watts, then 150,000, then a million, "far and away the most powerful radio station on the planet," heard in Canada on clear nights, said to make bedsprings hum and car headlights glow. Prostate operations went for up to $1,000; his Brinkley Pharmaceutical Association of pharmacies cleared about $14,000 a week. He lost the 1939 Fishbein libel trial and was ruined. In 1939 he gave $629 to the Silver Legion.

6 (continued). The utilities — a line that stopped

The North Shore Line stands still for seven weeks — 1938 (Insull's road, in receivership since 1932)

  • Where: the Chicago North Shore & Milwaukee Railroad — the electric interurban from the Loop (over the "L") through Evanston, the Skokie Valley bypass (opened 1926, 18 miles of double track, 20 minutes faster) and the Shore Line through Winnetka and Glencoe to Waukegan, Kenosha, Racine and Milwaukee.
  • What happened: Samuel Insull's showpiece interurban went into receivership in 1932 as his empire crumbled, with ridership and revenue falling through the Depression. In 1938 a strike "precipitated by a 15% reduction in wages" halted the whole line for seven weeks — a Montana labour paper of 20 Oct 1938 noted "a dandy electric line between Chicago and Milwaukee that has been collecting rust for more than two months." Even so, the road spent $4.3 million of Public Works Administration money on grade separation through Winnetka and Glencoe, built under 200-plus daily trains and finished in late 1941.
  • The odd detail: for seven weeks in the late summer and autumn of 1938 there was no electric train between Chicago and Milwaukee — the North Shore's towns cut off from the Loop except by the steam road and the highway.
  • People: Samuel Insull — owner to 1932.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_North_Shore_and_Milwaukee_Railroad ; Montana Labor News (Butte), 20 Oct 1938, p. 4, via the Library of Congress search index https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn86075141/1938-10-20/ed-1/?sp=4 (snippet read in the search JSON; OCR page 403 from this machine)
