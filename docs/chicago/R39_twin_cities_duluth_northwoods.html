R39 — The Twin Cities, Duluth, the Iron Range and the Northwoods, 1920–1940, and their communities

Lane: R39 (Umbra research library, Chicago & the Great Lakes sweep). Date: 2026-09-30. Model: Sonnet 5.5. Brief followed: BRIEF.md (entry format, sourcing, progressive writing, factions paragraph as corrected).

Overview

42 entries. The library already held the gangster story of the Twin Cities (the O'Connor "layover," the Barker–Karpis kidnappings, Dillinger's Lincoln Court shootout) and outlines of the 1934 strike and the 1920 Duluth lynching; what it lacked was the communities — and they turn out to be the richest material. Minneapolis was ruled by a membership-listed employers' association (the Citizens' Alliance, 166 firms in 1934) that also shut Jews out of its jobs and clubs ("an iron curtain," said Carey McWilliams in 1946), while a Silver Shirt chapter recruited businessmen; St. Paul's Rondo organized the Pullman porters and a Black family in south Minneapolis survived a mob of 4,000 in 1931. On the Range, ore shipments fell from 16.5 million tons (1931) to 2.2 million (1932), a whole town (Hibbing, 188 buildings) had been dragged two miles to get out of the way of a mine, and the Finns — barred from the public halls — built their own park, college and paper. The Ojibwe nations' record is a land ledger (White Earth: about 93 per cent lost by 1920; Red Lake: the one reservation never allotted) and a set of period sources (Densmore's cylinders, the 1928 Meriam Report). The Northwoods gives the fun: a Wisconsin high school as the executive branch (1928), the first snowmobile (1924/27), Hurley's Silver Street, and a border hotel sold for $1,000 and four barrels of whiskey. The divergence hits are heavy and specific: no Minnesota Commission of Public Safety (no American war), no Finnish "Red Exodus" to Karelia (no Soviet Union), a Silver Legion without Hitler or Bolshevism to point at, and — the neatest — the 1928 Summer White House on the Brule would be Dawes's, not Coolidge's. Sourcing note: WebSearch worked this run, but several MinnPost and Star Tribune pages returned HTTP 429 to the fetch tool; entries resting on search-result summaries say so and carry Medium or Low confidence.

PART 1 — THE TWIN CITIES: LABOR, POLITICS AND THE PEOPLES

Local 574 builds a strike city: the garage, the kitchen, the hospital, the cruising pickets — 7 February – 22 August 1934 (deepens R07)

  • Where: Minneapolis — the Market District (today's Warehouse District); strike headquarters at 215 South Eighth Street, a big garage fitted out as commissary, infirmary and dispatch centre (address per MNopedia; a second read describes only "a big garage").
  • What happened: Teamsters Local 574 had fewer than 75 members when 1934 began. Its Trotskyist organizers (Vincent "Ray" Dunne, brothers Miles and Grant, Carl Skoglund) started with a coal-yard strike on 7 February that shut 65 of the city's 67 yards; by 15 May the local claimed 6,000 members and voted the general strike (16 May). The union ran the strike like a small state: a strike committee of one hundred, a hospital and commissary, a Women's Auxiliary of members' wives who staffed the kitchen, and "cruising picket squads" — pickets in cars patrolling the city, dispatched from headquarters — a tactic the employers struggled to answer. On 21–22 May about 600 pickets came out and 20,000 strikers, sympathizers and spectators gathered around the market. Local president was William Brown; Harry DeBoer, Farrell Dobbs and Jack Maloney were in the leadership.
  • The odd detail: A local with fewer than 75 members shut 65 of 67 coal yards in February, and ran its army of pickets from a garage that doubled as a field hospital.
  • People: Vincent R. "Ray" Dunne, Miles Dunne, Grant Dunne — organizers (Communist League of America); Carl Skoglund — Swedish-born Trotskyist, Local 574 leader; Farrell Dobbs — coal-yard worker who became strike-committee organizer (later a national Teamsters organizer); William Brown — Local 574 president; Daniel Tobin — Teamsters international president (opposed the local); Floyd B. Olson — governor.
  • Sources: https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/minneapolis-teamsters-strike-1934 ; https://libcom.org/article/one-day-july-remembering-1934-minneapolis-teamster-strike ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minneapolis_general_strike_of_1934
  • Confidence: High on the dates and numbers; Medium on the HQ address (two of three sources give no address).
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — the leaders were Trotskyists (Communist League of America → later the Socialist Workers Party); with no Soviet Union there is no Stalin–Trotsky split, so their label and their enemies need reinventing (a Germany-patronized Left? see R23).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A garage with a kitchen, a surgeon and a dispatcher on the telephone is a ready-made investigator base — and a union whose real power is that it can put fifty cars on any street in ten minutes. Who else in the setting would want that network to owe them a favour: the Secret Service (old guard, federal) or FBI Section 13?

The Battle of Deputies' Run — 22 May 1934

  • Where: The Market District, Minneapolis (the exact block is not specified in the sources read).
  • What happened: The employers' Citizens Alliance answered the strike by swearing in "special police" — businessmen and clerks in civilian clothes with clubs — alongside the regular force. On 22 May the pickets attacked: the deputies were driven off the market streets in a running fight. Two deputized men were killed — C. Arthur Lyman (a Citizens Alliance leader) and Peter Erath — and about two dozen were injured. The name commemorates the rout (the deputies were run off the market).
  • The odd detail: Both dead were businessmen who had put a deputy's badge on over a civilian suit — the strike's first deaths were on the employers' side.
  • People: C. Arthur Lyman — businessman-deputy, killed; Peter Erath — deputy, killed; Ray Dunne — strike leader; Carl Skoglund — strike leader.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minneapolis_general_strike_of_1934 ; https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/minneapolis-teamsters-strike-1934 ; https://libcom.org/article/one-day-july-remembering-1934-minneapolis-teamster-strike
  • Confidence: High on date, names and result; Low on the exact street.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A city where the business elite deputized itself is a city where the elite had a private army long before 1934 — the Alliance's "spy network" and blacklist are the raw material for a conspiracy that thinks in filing cabinets.

Bloody Friday, 20 July 1934, and Olson's martial law (deepens R07)

  • Where: Third Street North in front of the Slocum, Bergren & Co. dock, Market District (one source: "3rd and 6th"); state fairgrounds, St. Paul (where arrested leaders were held).
  • What happened: On 20 July police, with about a dozen squad cars carrying shotgun-armed officers, cordoned the area while trucks loaded under escort. A pickets' truck was cut off and police "opened fire with shotguns on the pickets' truck." Sixty-seven strikers were wounded, most shot in the back; two died — John Belor and Henry Ness. The governor's investigating board concluded that "police took direct aim at the pickets and fired to kill" and that no picket carried a weapon. Ness's funeral procession on 24 July drew a crowd counted at 100,000 (Wikipedia) or about 40,000 mourners (MNopedia) — sources disagree. On 25 July federal mediators issued the Haas-Dunnigan proposal; Olson declared martial law on 26 July (banning picketing, issuing truck permits — about 4,000 permits), and the Guard raided both the union and the Citizens Alliance headquarters, arresting union leaders (held at the State Fairgrounds). About 4,000 Guardsmen were mobilized on 1 August [UNVERIFIED — V_R39]. Employers accepted the settlement, with the Alliance's A. W. Strong [UNVERIFIED — V_R39: name not found on any page read] agreeing on 22 August (R07 has 21 Aug; the sources read say 22 Aug).
  • The odd detail: A Farmer-Labor governor who called himself "a radical" was the man who put the Guard on the pickets — and also on the employers' club.
  • People: John Belor, Henry Ness — the dead; Floyd B. Olson — governor; A. W. Strong — Citizens Alliance negotiator; Haas & Dunnigan — federal mediators.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloody_Friday_(Minneapolis) ; https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/minneapolis-teamsters-strike-1934
  • Confidence: High (funeral attendance conflicts between sources).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the strikers' Trotskyism BENDS lightly, see first entry).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A funeral for a man shot in the back that fills a city's main streets is a ritual with its own occult weight — a mob, a crowd, a procession; a good place for something that feeds on a crowd's grief and then waits for the next one.

The Citizens' Alliance — Minneapolis's open-shop oligarchy, 1903–1936 (renamed Associated Industries of Minneapolis, December 1936) ⟨fixed: V_R39⟩

  • Where: Minneapolis; the employers' clubs and Chamber of Commerce circles downtown.
  • What happened: In 1901 the city's machine-shop owners crushed a machinists' strike; in 1903 they expanded into the Citizens' Alliance, an association that soon enlisted more than 200 Minneapolis manufacturers, millers and banks and set out to keep the city "open shop" — no unions. The Alliance blacklisted union workers, ran a spy network in workplaces, published anti-union propaganda and, when needed, raised a private force. It won for thirty years; at the 1934 strike it had 166 member businesses and refused to bargain with Local 574. It lost — the union recognition of August 1934 broke its hold. MNopedia says it changed its name to Associated Industries of Minneapolis in December 1936; that it was still active in 1947 is UNVERIFIED — V_R39 ⟨fixed: V_R39⟩.
  • The odd detail: The city's top firms conspired as a formal, membership-listed body — and the same membership rolls, in the 1930s, are the establishment that also barred Jews from its clubs and jobs (next entries).
  • People: A. W. Strong — Alliance negotiator, 1934; the unnamed Alliance directors — banks, flour millers, department stores.
  • Sources: https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/citizens-alliance-minneapolis (as summarized in a search result; not fetched in full) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloody_Friday_(Minneapolis)
  • Confidence: Medium-High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A conspiracy of respectable businessmen with a spy network and a blacklist is exactly the shape of a cult that never needs to hold a ritual — the ledger is the ritual. Perfect front for a vampire cabal.

Floyd B. Olson, the "radical" governor who might have been president — died 22 August 1936

  • Where: Minneapolis (North Side boy); the Capitol, St. Paul; Mayo Clinic, Rochester; buried at Lakewood Cemetery, Minneapolis.
  • What happened: Born 13 November 1891 to Scandinavian immigrants in North Minneapolis, Olson worked for the Northern Pacific, brushed the IWW, took a law degree and was Hennepin County attorney 1920–31 (after a corruption scandal). He won the governorship on the Farmer-Labor ticket on 4 November 1930 with 59 per cent, was sworn in 6 January 1931, re-elected 1932 and 1934, and described himself as "a radical" rather than "a liberal"; his second inaugural (4 January 1933) proposed a graduated income tax and unemployment insurance. He was diagnosed with stomach cancer in late 1935 (one source says pancreatic), planned a Senate run for 1936 and, many believed, could have run as the third-party challenger to Roosevelt — though he had no wish to. He died 22 August 1936 at the Mayo Clinic, aged 44, having moved to his Gull Lake summer home three weeks before. Hjalmar Petersen filled the office; Elmer Benson won in November.
  • The odd detail: The man who ordered the Guard against the Teamsters' pickets also ran a state whose crime-and-politics tangle produced the Liggett murder (Dec 1935) while he was already dying.
  • People: Floyd B. Olson — governor; Ada Krejci Olson — wife; Hjalmar Petersen — lieutenant governor, successor; Elmer Benson — next governor; Franklin Roosevelt — sent condolences ("a personality of singular force and courage").
  • Sources: https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/olson-floyd-b-1891-1936 ; https://time.com/archive/6755807/minnesota-death-of-olson/
  • Confidence: High on dates; Medium on diagnosis (stomach vs pancreatic).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (FDR is still president in the setting).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A governor dying in the same summer as Chicago's Church-heavy autumn of 1936, whose funeral draws a state — someone who needed him gone before he could run. FBI Section 13 files it, the Secret Service wants to know who was in the hospital.

Kid Cann and the Minneapolis Syndicate — Isadore Blumenfeld, North Minneapolis, 1900–1981 (deepens R07's Liggett entry)

  • Where: North Minneapolis (Plymouth Avenue, the Jewish quarter); Vienna Cafe, Nicollet Avenue; the Cotton Club; the Hennepin County workhouse.
  • What happened: Isadore Blumenfeld, born 8 September 1900 at Râmnicu Sărat, Romania, was brought to North Minneapolis in 1902 and grew up in its Jewish enclave. First arrested in 1920 in a brothel; 1924 arrested for the death of taxi driver Charles Goldberg outside the Vienna Cafe on Nicollet (MNopedia: he admitted the shooting, claimed it was an accident and was never tried — Blumenfeld would be buried near Goldberg in 1981) ⟨fixed: V_R39⟩; 1928 arrested for shooting two police officers at the Cotton Club (not charged). Bootlegging, then gambling and labour racketeering, then liquor licensing through shell companies. 1933: charged in New Orleans with rum smuggling; tried in Oklahoma for laundering ransom from the Urschel kidnapping — acquitted. 1934: convicted of running a still, served a year in the Hennepin County workhouse. Walter Liggett was murdered in December 1935; Blumenfeld was tried and acquitted on 19 February 1936 despite identification by Liggett's widow. He disliked "Kid Cann" (no one knows its origin) and used "Dr. Ferguson" or "Fergie." He died at Mount Sinai Hospital, Minneapolis, on 24 June 1981 per MNopedia (Wikipedia says 21 June; the sources differ) ⟨fixed: V_R39⟩.
  • The odd detail: The Twin Cities' great Jewish mob boss was buried in a Jewish cemetery near the taxi driver he was once arrested for killing.
  • People: Isadore "Kid Cann" Blumenfeld — Syndicate boss; Charles Goldberg — taxi driver killed 1924; Walter Liggett — editor, killed Dec 1935; Edith Liggett — widow, witness; the Blumenfeld brothers — family enterprise.
  • Sources: https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/blumenfeld-isadore-kid-cann-1900-1981

The Silver Shirts in Minnesota — William Dudley Pelley's Silver Legion, Twin Cities chapter, 1933–1940

  • Where: Minneapolis–St. Paul (Pelley's headquarters: Asheville, North Carolina).
  • What happened: William Dudley Pelley founded the Silver Legion of America in Asheville, an antisemitic, avowedly "Christian and Aryan" fascist militia; Jews and African Americans could not join. One of Pelley's principal organizers worked in the Twin Cities, and a period count credits the Minneapolis chapter with about 800 members out of ~6,000 statewide (1936) (figures confirmed by Wikipedia's Silver Legion of America — about 6,000 statewide, about 800 in Minneapolis — and the UMN page for the statewide figure; neither states the year 1936) ⟨fixed: V_R39⟩, with prominent businessmen and state officials among active participants. A Twin Cities Jewish response was organized by Samuel Scheiner and the Jewish community's council; in 1936 a recent Minnesota graduate, Arnold Eric Sevareid, published a six-part exposé (another source says five) in the Minneapolis Journal on the chapter. The group opposed the Farmer-Labor Party in the 1938 governor's race, was investigated by the Dies Committee, and dissolved in 1940 (the UMN page; Wikipedia says 1941, after Pearl Harbor). (Pelley himself was a spiritualist who claimed a death-and-return revelation — Seven Minutes in Eternity, 1928 — general knowledge, UNVERIFIED this session; see R10 for the Silver Shirts' broader reach.)
  • The odd detail: The man who blew the chapter open was a recent university graduate — Eric Sevareid, later the famous CBS correspondent.
  • People: William Dudley Pelley — founder, occultist; Eric Sevareid — student journalist, exposed the chapter; Samuel Scheiner — Jewish community organizer against it.
  • Sources: https://acampusdivided.umn.edu/organizations/silver-shirts/ ; https://archive.mpr.org/stories/1992/09/21/no-jews-allowed
  • Confidence: Medium-High (membership figures Medium).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Pelley founded the Legion on 31 January 1933, the day after Hitler became German chancellor (the 31 January 1933 founding date is confirmed in V_R39 from Wikipedia), and its enemy was "Jewish Bolshevism." In the setting Hitler holds no office and there is no Soviet Union: the founding trigger and the target need reinventing — the Golden Circle (R10) is the obvious substitute patron.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A "Christian and Aryan" militia led by a man who says he died and came back and received a revelation is a cult in the open — and a ready front for a Golden Circle recruiting drive in the upper Midwest.

"An iron curtain separates Jews from non-Jews": Minneapolis's Jewish community and its exclusions — 1930s–1946

  • Where: North Minneapolis (Plymouth Avenue North — synagogues "every few blocks," the Talmud Torah, Malcoff's delicatessen, Fidelman's butcher shop, Zeesman Mortuary); the Calhoun Beach Club; downtown corporate offices.
  • What happened: In 1946 the writer Carey McWilliams called Minneapolis "the capital of anti-Semitism in the United States," with "an iron curtain" between Jews and non-Jews. The record (Minnesota Public Radio's oral-history documentary No Jews Allowed) lists what was closed to Jews in the 1930s: jobs at the banks, insurance companies and big corporations (Pillsbury, General Mills, 3M — which barred Jews from public-facing sales), membership in Kiwanis, Rotary, Lions and other service clubs, country clubs, and — for the Calhoun Beach Club — a sign that read "No Jews, peddlers, dogs allowed." A 1946 survey found 40 per cent of the city's new subdivisions carried covenants against selling to Jews or African Americans. ⟨fixed: V_R39⟩ The pulpit voices included William Bell Riley (First Baptist Church, 1897–1942) and Luke Rader (River Lake Tabernacle; UNVERIFIED this session — brother of Chicago's Paul Rader, see R05), with Father Coughlin's radio sermons and Charles Lindbergh's name in the mix. The Jewish response was to build: Plymouth Avenue's institutions, a Jewish council (1940s) and Mount Sinai Hospital (late 1940s), founded after Jewish doctors were refused privileges elsewhere.
  • The odd detail: The Twin Cities' biggest Jewish employer of the 1930s was, effectively, the Jewish community itself — because the flour mills and banks would not hire them.
  • People: Carey McWilliams — writer, 1946 verdict; William Bell Riley — First Baptist pastor, fundamentalist leader; Luke Rader — River Lake Tabernacle; Samuel Scheiner — Jewish council founder.
  • Sources: https://archive.mpr.org/stories/1992/09/21/no-jews-allowed ; https://acampusdivided.umn.edu/organizations/silver-shirts/
  • Confidence: High on the pattern; the quotes are from an MPR documentary (1992) summarizing the period.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — Coughlin and Lindbergh hold; the "Jewish Bolshevik" strand of the rhetoric has no Soviet Union to hang on. Ford's Dearborn Independent (1920–27) sat behind much of this; check R08/R05 for that entry.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A community shut out of the corporate city and running its own hospital, hall and butcher is the exact soil for a Kabbalah tradition the Church hires quietly — a rabbi on Plymouth Avenue whom the Alliance-era elite would never invite to dinner and the Church needs at three in the morning.

Rondo: the heart of St. Paul's Black community — 1930s

  • Where: St. Paul — the Rondo neighborhood (Rondo Avenue and Rondo Street; Farrington and St. Anthony Avenue); churches at 445 North Farrington and 624 Central Avenue West; Hallie Q. Brown Community Center, 553 Aurora Avenue.
  • What happened: Black residents settled Rondo from the late 1800s, after Jewish families moved out; by the 1930s about half of St. Paul's Black population lived there. The neighborhood's institutions: St. Peter Claver Church (Catholic, established 1892 at 445 North Farrington), St. James African Methodist Episcopal (moved to 624 Central Ave. West in 1926), Pilgrim Baptist Church, the NAACP chapter (1913), the Sterling Club (1919, for African American professionals), the Credjafawn Social Club (1928, 678 Rondo — a grocery cooperative and credit union), and the Hallie Q. Brown Community Center (1929); newspapers included The Appeal and the St. Paul Recorder. Residents were Pullman porters and maids, doctors and lawyers, packinghouse hands and tradesmen. The Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters' St. Paul local (Local #3) held its first meetings on 13–14 January 1926 at Welcome Hall, Farrington and St. Anthony, with over 50 men attending.   • People: Joseph Rondeau — namesake, moved there from Fort Snelling in the late 1850s; Roy Wilkins — notable Rondo resident, later led the national NAACP; Nellie Francis — St. Paul activist who drafted the 1921 anti-lynching law (see the Duluth entry); Lena O. Smith — Minneapolis NAACP attorney.

The Lee house: 4,000 people on a lawn in south Minneapolis — 11 July 1931 (and the two years after)

  • Where: 4600 Columbus Avenue South, Minneapolis (Field neighborhood), a 1923 house.
  • What happened: Arthur Lee, a U.S. Postal Service worker and World War I veteran (divergence flag: in the setting America never fought the Great War, so his service must be rewritten — the "holds" label below should read BENDS lightly) ⟨fixed: V_R39⟩, and his wife Edith and daughter Mary moved into the all-white Field neighborhood in late June 1931. Neighbors first offered to buy them out, then vandalized the house with paint and poisoned Mary's dog. A mob of about 150 gathered on 11 July; hundreds threw stones on 14 July; on 16 July over 4,000 people assembled a block away. Mayor William A. Anderson asked residents to keep away and did little; the police eventually formed protective circles and escorted Mary to kindergarten. The Lees' first lawyer urged them to sell; on 18 July the NAACP attorney Lena Olive Smith took over and declared there would be "no further negotiations with any committee"; her public statements in three papers helped end the violence after 20 July. The Lees slept in the basement and stayed until late 1933; a police presence continued into September 1932. The house was listed on the National Register in 2014.
  • The odd detail: Their own first attorney told them to sell; the woman who saved their right to stay was the NAACP's attorney Lena Olive Smith (reputedly Minnesota's first Black woman lawyer — UNVERIFIED this session).
  • People: Arthur and Edith Lee — the family; Mary Lee — daughter; Lena Olive Smith — NAACP attorney; William A. Anderson — mayor.
  • Sources: https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/structure/arthur-and-edith-lee-house-minneapolis ; https://www.minnpost.com/mnopedia/2025/12/in-1931-a-black-family-moved-into-segregated-south-minneapolis-then-the-terror-began/ (search summary)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A crowd of 4,000 turning on one house is a cult without a leader — a "population versus cult" demonstration for the design principle; the true horror is that nothing supernatural was needed.

After Duluth: the photograph, the trials, Max Mason and Minnesota's 1921 anti-lynching law (living memory; deepens R07)

  • Where: Duluth — the city jail; the intersection of First Street and Second Avenue East; the State Capitol in St. Paul (the law).
  • What happened: After the 15 June 1920 lynching of Elias Clayton, Elmer Jackson and Isaac McGhie (mob estimated at up to 10,000), a grand jury indicted 37 white men; only 8 were tried; three were convicted of rioting — Louis Dondino, Carl Hammerberg and Gilbert Stephenson — and served less than 18 months; no one was convicted of murder. Seven Black men were indicted on the original rape accusation; Max Mason was convicted and sentenced to up to 30 years; William Miller was acquitted; Mason served four years and was released on condition he leave Minnesota. The mob had posed with the bodies for photographs that were sold as postcards. A Duluth NAACP branch was founded and, with St. Paul activist Nellie Francis, who "initiated, drafted, and lobbied for" it, Minnesota passed an anti-lynching law on 21 April 1921. A memorial at First Street and Second Avenue was dedicated in 2003; Mason received a posthumous pardon on 12 June 2020 (the state's first). The postcard shot is the reason a Depression-era Duluthian could see what happened.
  • The odd detail: The photograph was a commercial product — "sold as souvenirs" — so in 1930 there were people in Duluth who owned a picture of a lynching they had not attended.
  • People: Elias Clayton, Elmer Jackson, Isaac McGhie — the dead; Max Mason — convicted, later pardoned; Nellie Francis — drafted the 1921 law; Louis Dondino, Carl Hammerberg, Gilbert Stephenson — convicted of rioting.
  • Sources: https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/duluth-lynchings ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duluth_lynchings (via R07)
  • Confidence: High (Wikipedia and MNopedia agree).
  • Divergence check: Living memory; holds — domestic (note: 1920 falls after the 1916 break, but the Great Migration and the Klan's second rise run on the domestic calendar).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A photograph that is also a document — a mob's faces, dated and captioned — is a perfect "cursed object" without a curse: the investigators find it, and the faces in it are the city's 1930s establishment.

The Minnesota Commission of Public Safety — the war measure that would not exist (1917–1919)

  • Where: St. Paul (the Capitol); New Ulm (Brown County); the Iron Range; the Minnesota Editorial Association.
  • What happened: In April 1917, under Governor J. A. A. PART 4 — THE OJIBWE NATIONS AND THEIR RECORD (period sources; treated with care)

Red Lake: the reservation that was never allotted, and the Chippewa Tribe it did not join — 1889–1936

  • Where: Red Lake Reservation, northern Minnesota (Upper and Lower Red Lake); the Red Lake Agency.
  • What happened: When the 1889 Nelson Act set up allotment across Minnesota's Ojibwe lands, Red Lake "successfully resisted" it — the Nation's own account — and remains "untouched Indian land, as it has never left tribal control." In 1889 Red Lake was forced to cede 2,905,000 acres of its original 3,260,000 as "surplus"; further agreements in 1902 and 1904 fixed the present boundaries ("the 1904 Land Act"). When the 1934 Indian Reorganization Act invited Red Lake to join six other Chippewa bands, it resisted joining and kept its own government; those six bands became the Minnesota Chippewa Tribe (constitution approved 1936). (A search-result summary adds that Red Lake sued the Office of Indian Affairs in the mid-1930s over trust funds, that the outcome affirmed its exclusive land rights in 1935, and that its constitution blended hereditary chiefs with elections in 1936 — UNVERIFIED; the Nation's own history says a democratic constitution came in the 1950s and the first elections were in 1959 with Roger Jourdain as chairman.) The anthropologist Ruth Landes worked among the Ojibwe in 1932–36 (see R22).
  • The odd detail: While neighbouring White Earth was losing 93 per cent of its land, Red Lake kept its remaining reservation intact by refusing allotment — and refused the 1934 reorganization machinery too.
  • People: Roger Jourdain — first elected tribal chairman, 1959; the Red Lake hereditary chiefs (unnamed in the source read).

White Earth and the "White Earth Tragedy": the land that went in a generation — 1867–1934

  • Where: White Earth Reservation, northwestern Minnesota.
  • What happened: The reservation was created on 19 March 1867 when ten Ojibwe chiefs met President Andrew Johnson; it covered about 1,300 square miles. The Nelson Act of 1889 aimed to move all Minnesota Ojibwe except Red Lake here and to open their timber. What followed is documented as fraud: by 1920 non-Indians had acquired roughly 93 per cent of the original ~836,000 acres [UNVERIFIED — V_R39: not on the Wikipedia page cited as fetched, which says only that under ten per cent was tribal-owned by 1989]; a 1909 investigation by the federal Board of Indian Commissioners documented over 1,200 fraud cases [UNVERIFIED — V_R39] — forged deeds, sales coerced by alcohol or threat, collusion with local officials — and lumber companies used mixed-descent men to buy timbered allotments cheaply. By the 1930s Indians owned under 10 per cent of White Earth, about 4 per cent of Leech Lake and about 7 per cent of Mille Lacs (search summary), and a large share of tribal members had left the reservation. Winona LaDuke's White Earth Land Recovery Project (1989) began the slow buying back.

Sugar Point, Leech Lake: "the last Indian uprising" and the man who was never taken — 5 October 1898 (living memory)

  • Where: Sugar Point, Leech Lake, Minnesota (near Bear Island), Cass County; the Leech Lake Reservation.
  • What happened: In 1898 (not 1895) a Pillager Ojibwe man, Bugonaygeshig ("Old Bug," "Hole-in-the-Day," born 1835/36/39, died 17 May 1916), who had been arrested for bootlegging in April and left to walk home from Duluth, was seized on 15 September 1898 at Onigum as a witness in another bootlegging case; when marshals tried to take him to Duluth, other Pillagers freed him. ⟨fixed: V_R39⟩ In October 1898 soldiers of the 3rd U.S. Infantry were sent to re-arrest him — 78 in the column at Sugar Point and 215 reinforcements arriving the next day (Wikipedia, Bugonaygeshig); about 500 is the strength figure for the whole expedition, not the force at the fight. ⟨fixed: V_R39⟩ At Sugar Point on 5 October a soldier's rifle discharged (General Bacon's account; the Pillagers said soldiers fired at a canoe carrying women), shooting began, and six soldiers were killed, including Major Melville Wilkinson; ten were wounded; one Indian policeman was killed by friendly fire. Frances Densmore's wax cylinders: the Ojibwe songs she recorded, 1905–1910 and after

Ernest Oberholtzer and Mallard Island: the man who saved a wilderness and lived on a rock — Rainy Lake, 1913–1977

  • Where: Mallard Island, Rainy Lake, near Ranier (Koochiching County, Minnesota), a mile south of the Canadian border.
  • What happened: Ernest Carl Oberholtzer (1884–1977, born in Davenport, Iowa) left Harvard after one year and first came to the border lakes in 1906; he made a 3,000-mile canoe voyage in 1909 and moved to Rainy Lake in 1913; he bought Mallard Island in 1922 and lived there over fifty years. With the Ojibwe guide Dedaabaswewidang ("He Who Echoes Far Off," Billy Magee) he canoed 2,000 miles to Hudson Bay in 1912 (search summary). He founded the Quetico-Superior Council in 1928 to oppose the dam-and-industrialization plans of Edward Backus; the Shipstead-Newton-Nolan Act, signed by Herbert Hoover in 1930, barred logging and lake-level changes on federal land in the Rainy Lake watershed — the first statute in which Congress explicitly ordered federal land kept in its wilderness state (per a search summary). FDR named him first chairman of the President's Quetico-Superior Committee in 1934 (he held it until 1968); he was one of eight founding members of the Wilderness Society. He spoke fluent Ojibwe and never married.
  • The odd detail: A Harvard-educated aesthete stopped a paper-and-power empire's dams — with a canoe, an island and an Ojibwe partner.
  • People: Ernest Oberholtzer — conservationist, island hermit; Billy Magee (Dedaabaswewidang) — Ojibwe guide and companion; Edward Backus — timber-and-paper magnate who wanted the dams; Herbert Hoover — signed the 1930 Act; Franklin Roosevelt — named him committee chairman, 1934.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Oberholtzer (fetched) ; https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/oberholtzer-ernest-1884-1977 (search summary)

The 1928 "Summer White House" on the Brule — Coolidge in the record, Dawes in the setting (Cedar Island Lodge and Superior Central High School)

  • Where: Cedar Island Lodge (part of Henry Clay Pierce's 4,000-acre estate) on the Brule River, northwestern Wisconsin; Superior Central High School, Superior, Wisconsin (the executive offices).

Carl Eliason's motor toboggan: the first snowmobile — Sayner, Wisconsin, 1922–1927

  • Where: Sayner, Vilas County (Northwoods), Wisconsin.
  • What happened: Carl J. E. Eliason of Sayner, who had a chronic foot problem that made snowshoeing along his trap line impossible, began work in 1922 on a "motor toboggan" and had a working prototype in 1924: a wooden toboggan with two skis steered by ropes, pushed along by a rear steel-cleated endless track driven by a 2.5-horsepower Johnson outboard motor. He was granted a patent on 22 November 1927; he made about 40 machines. On receiving an order for 200 from Finland, he sold his patent to the FWD Company of Clintonville (date of the order not stated in the sources read).
  • The odd detail: The first snowmobile came from a trapper with a bad foot — and one of its earliest large orders (200 machines) came from Finland.

Oneida County, May 1933: America's first rural zoning law and the end of the cutover farm

  • Where: Oneida County (Rhinelander), Wisconsin; the northern "cutover" counties.
  • What happened: In May 1933 the Oneida County Board passed the nation's first rural zoning ordinance, asserting public control of land use in the county; University of Wisconsin college-extension staff, including Dean Christensen, helped organize and draft it. The idea was to keep settlers off marginal, logged-over ("cutover") farmland that could not support them. It worked as policy: by 1934 eighteen more county ordinances were in effect (thirty-seven by 1948), taking nearly five million marginal acres out of cultivation.
  • The odd detail: The Northwoods' stump farms, sold by lumber and land companies to hopeful immigrants after the trees were gone, were legally zoned back to forest in 1933.
  • People: Dean Christensen — University of Wisconsin college staff who helped draft it; the Oneida County Board.
  • Sources: https://www3.uwsp.edu/cnr-ap/clue/Documents/Zoning/Zoning_Ordinances.pdf (search summary)
  • Confidence: Medium (search summary; "cutover" background is general knowledge).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A county that zoned its own farms out of existence has a ghost-farm landscape: abandoned homesteads, families relocated, tax-delinquent land — a good place for something that can't be evicted.
