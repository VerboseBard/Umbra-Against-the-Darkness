  • Lane: R17 — the seven mission seeds in the owner's Google design doc ("Umbra Transcript", section MISSION STUFF)
  • Date: 2026-09-26
  • Model: Claude Fable 5.1 (research agent, 2026-09-26 Chicago & Great Lakes sweep)
  • Source of the seeds: the owner's earlier Umbra story notes, retained in the local archive. The original research discussion is reproduced below; optional seeds remain unadopted proposals.

Overview

All seven of the owner's seeds are real, and this file identifies the one nobody had: the "Chicago College Kidnappers" were the ten-man ring of Theodore "Handsome Jack" Klutas (University of Illinois, 1923), who kidnapped Capone-world gamblers and bootleggers for about $500,000 in 1931–33, was rumoured to have had his face and fingerprints altered (a prosecutor passed on "stories"; his prints still identified him at death) ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩, posed as a "Government Secret Service agent," and was machine-gunned by the state's attorney's police at 619 South 24th Avenue, Bellwood, on 6 January 1934 — the owner's "2 years" and his doppelganger instinct both fit the record. The Ukrainian riot is real (Sunday 17 December 1933, West Side, ~100 hurt, nobody killed; the Daily Worker's own version found in Chronicling America) but the owner's "right after this unemployment reached 50%" is an artefact of two adjacent Wikipedia sentences. O'Hare was, in the record, the undercover man of Frank J. Wilson — Chief of the U.S. Secret Service 1937–46 — and died with a rosary, a crucifix and a list of women's names in his pockets — the poem about the hour of death was framed over his desk at home ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩ — eight days before Capone's parole. The Stock Yards fire (4:14 p.m., 19 May 1934; 80 acres; one dead; $8M) has honest parallels with 1871 — drought, straw, an unproved spark, a stone survivor — and burned the Saddle and Sirloin Club's portrait gallery (about 350 portraits is the gallery's size at its 2003 centenary; the number lost in 1934 is not given), 104 of which one painter replaced in eighteen months ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩. The Memorial Day numbers are checked one by one (10 dead holds; "85,000" is high; "five mills" is right — the owner's source reads "Five Chicago-area mills were shut with 22,000 workers going on strike" ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩; "40 police hurt" is the police's claim); Commissioner Allman's testimony is quoted from the Washington Times. The Merchandise Mart is a wholesale city that lost its purpose within five years and kept a battery room to broadcast through a blackout. Dillinger's afterlife — blood mopped up for sale, four death masks (one never on the face), an autopsy lost fifty years in a shopping bag, a kept brain, a lost fingerprint card, 2½ tons of concrete — is the "something" the owner can make. 27 entries; one is folklore, labelled.

§1 — "College kidnappers" — the real gang

Owner's words (verbatim): "College kidnappers / Chicago College Kidnappers(2 years of kidnappings)1931-1933 / Skinwalkers? Shapeshifters? Doppelgangers? Implanting themself into society for some reason."

Identification: the owner's phrase is the real press name of a real gang. The "College Kidnappers" were the ten-man Chicago ring of Theodore "Handsome Jack" Klutas, so called because Klutas had been a University of Illinois man (Urbana–Champaign, 1923, no degree). They worked the "snatch racket" from 1931 to the end of 1933 — kidnapping gamblers, bootleggers and rich hoodlums, mostly Capone people, because those victims paid and did not call the police — and took something near half a million dollars. Klutas died under a police machine gun on a Bellwood sidewalk on 6 January 1934; the rest were rounded up by February. Nothing on disk (repo, archive, Master Reference) had identified them before this file. The two-year span the owner wrote down is roughly right, not exact: the earliest dated kidnapping found is Hackett's first (May 1931), the first victim (Urban) is undated, and the AP called the hunt for Klutas a "seven-year search" ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩.

The College Kidnappers of "Handsome Jack" Klutas — 1931–January 1934

  • Where: Chicago and its ring — Blue Island (victim Hackett), Peoria (members Swolley and Hughes; first victim Urban), St. Charles (Jones), Benton, Ill. (Souder), and the last hideout at 619 South 24th Avenue, Bellwood (western suburb, Proviso Township)
  • What happened: Theodore "Handsome Jack" Klutas (born John Edward Klutas, 1900, Lyndon Township, Whiteside County, Ill., to William, German-born, and Ida E. May Klutas) led a gang the papers called the College Kidnappers. Regular members: Edward Doll alias "Eddie LaRue", Russell Hughes, Frank Souder, Gale Swolley (also spelt Swoley), Ernest Rossi, Eddie Wagner, Earl McMahon, Julius "Babe" Jones and Walter Dietrich; a chauffeur, Adolphe Anzone; and a Paul Stroud named in the press. They chose their victims from the underworld — "gamblers, bootleggers, and quite wealthy mobsters," most of them of the Capone organisation — precisely because such men "were less likely to approach the police." Named victims: James Hackett, a Blue Island gambler, kidnapped twice — the first ransom, $75,000, paid by his wife (May 1931); for the second the gang demanded double (Sifakis, via Wikipedia, says it bankrupted him), but the period press reports the gang "was paid $1500" for it ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩; William Urban of Peoria, the first victim; a James Ward; and one manufacturer held for $50,000 and released when he proved unable to pay. Total take: "nearly $500,000" / "over half a million dollars" in roughly two years. Klutas also robbed banks and was indicted for counterfeiting $5 bills (24 Nov 1933, with Rex Howard, George Milligan and others).
  • The odd detail: by November 1933 the Cook County assistant state's attorney (Mal Coghlan) told the press that "stories had come to him" that Klutas had "had his finger prints changed, and his visage … marred to make identification difficult" — a rumour, not a finding (Wikipedia's "one of the first to try this tactic" rests on it), and at Bellwood "fingerprints established the identities" anyway ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩; and the Evening Star's wire story of the raid (7 Jan 1934, p. 2) says he had "posed to the owner" of the Bellwood bungalow "as a Government Secret Service agent." A kidnapper said to have changed his face and his fingerprints ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩ and who goes about as a Secret Service man is already a doppelganger story before the occult is added.
  • People: Theodore "Handsome Jack" Klutas — leader, U. of I. man, d. 6 Jan 1934 aged 34 · Walter Dietrich — ex-Baron Lamm bank robber, Michigan City lifer, escaped 26 Sept 1933 with the guns Dillinger smuggled in · Julius "Babe" Jones — the member who turned · Edward Doll ("Eddie LaRue") · Russell Hughes — killed in a Peoria barber shop · Frank Souder, Gale Swolley, Ernest Rossi, Eddie Wagner, Earl McMahon · James Hackett — the twice-kidnapped Blue Island gambler · Mal Coghlan — assistant state's attorney
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_Kidnappers (cites Sifakis, The Mafia Encyclopedia pp. 244–45; Newton, The Encyclopedia of Kidnappings pp. 65–67) · http://genealogytrails.com/ill/whiteside/crimeklutas.html (transcribed 1933–34 wire-service stories: Fitchburg Sentinel, Edwardsville Intelligencer, Helena Independent) · https://www.rarenewspapers.com/view/664404 (New York Times, 7 Jan 1934, p. 24, "KIDNAPPER SLAIN BY CHICAGO POLICE")
  • Confidence: High that this is the gang and on the frame (leader, name, years, Bellwood death); Medium on the ransom figures and the full victim list (the encyclopaedias and 1933 wire stories differ in small ways — the Evening Star credits the "collegiate band" with "nearly a dozen kidnappings and extortions which netted more than a half million dollars"; the fingerprint surgery is a single Nov 1933 wire report — a period claim; the Secret Service pose is in the Star's raid story, a primary wire account). Primary: https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1934-01-07/ed-1/?sp=2 (Evening Star, 7 Jan 1934, p. 2; OCR read via the tile.loc.gov text service)
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (a Chicago crime story with no foreign policy in it)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the owner's instinct — implanting themselves into society — fits the record uncannily: the gang's whole method was to take people the law would never look for, and its leader was said to have remade his face and prints ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩ and wore the Secret Service as a mask. Play it as the owner wrote it: something that wears men. The two-year run ends the day the "Klutas" the police shoot is not the thing they were hunting — and Section 13 has a man on its rolls with that face.

The end at Bellwood — 619 South 24th Avenue, Saturday 6 January 1934

  • Where: a rented apartment/bungalow at 619 South 24th Avenue, Bellwood, Ill.
  • What happened: State's attorney's police had watched the Bellwood flat for ten days on information from the turned member Babe Jones. Two squads under Lt. Frank Johnson and Sgt. Joe Healy, with Capt. Daniel "Tubbo" Gilbert of the state's attorney's office over the operation, went in on the afternoon of 6 January. They found Walter Dietrich shaving in the bathroom and took him without a shot; Klutas came out of the bungalow (both wire stories: "mowed down as he came out of the bungalow"; "Finally Klutas and two others came out" — the came-home-and-rang-the-bell version is the hobby blog's) ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩, was ordered to surrender, reached for his pistol ("Klutas' pistol barked," said the wire copy), and Healy's Thompson put .45 slugs through his chest — "bullets splattered through his body." He died on the sidewalk, aged 34. Tear-gas bombs and weapons were found inside; five people were arrested. The Evening Star's wire account of the next morning has it fuller: seven men taken, "several machine guns," tear gas and ammunition seized; the bungalow's owner, Ray Nischwitz, released once police were satisfied he was not involved — Klutas had "posed to the owner as a Government Secret Service agent"; and "fingerprints established the identities of Klutas and Detrick, officers said" — so whatever had been done to his fingers had not worked. The Washington Times (8 Jan 1934, p. 12) adds that Gale Swolley and Frank Souder went on trial that Monday for the second Hackett kidnapping with the state demanding the death penalty, and that the informer — "Augustus Jones" in the wire copy — "turned State's evidence because Klutas assertedly cheated him of $6,500 ransom money" and sat under guard in the county jail as the star witness. The New York Times ran it on p. 24 the next morning. He was buried in Lyndon Township Cemetery, Whiteside County, beside his mother. Police "captured the rest of these gangsters in February 1934."
  • The odd detail: the man who ran the operation, Capt. Daniel Gilbert, is the same state's attorney's investigator who ran the O'Hare murder inquiry five years later (§3) — one police career threads two of the owner's seven seeds.
  • People: Lt. Frank Johnson — state's attorney's police · Sgt. Joe Healy — fired the machine gun · Capt. Daniel Gilbert — chief investigator, state's attorney's office · Walter Dietrich — taken shaving
  • Sources: https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83045462/1934-01-07/ed-1/?sp=2 (Evening Star, 7 Jan 1934, p. 2 — primary) · https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84026749/1934-01-08/ed-1/?sp=12 (Washington Times, 8 Jan 1934, p. 12, "KLUTAS & PALS…" — primary) · http://genealogytrails.com/ill/whiteside/crimeklutas.html (Helena Independent, 7 Jan 1934) · http://homebrewedmojo.blogspot.com/2013/01/handsome-jack-gets-gunned.html (hobby blog, reproduces period photographs; used only where it agrees with the wire stories) · https://www.rarenewspapers.com/view/664404
  • Confidence: High on date/place/officers; Medium on the shot-by-shot (the blog supplies Healy and the machine gun; the wire story names only Johnson's squad)
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the ten days of surveillance are the module: the watchers write down who goes in and out of 619 South 24th, and the list does not stay the same size as the number of people who come out.

The betrayal — Babe Jones, the Outfit's message, and the ambush that missed — late 1933

  • Where: Chicago; Jones was of St. Charles, Ill.
  • What happened: In 1933 Capone-syndicate men caught Julius "Babe" Jones and sent him back with a "payoff" message: stop kidnapping our people. Klutas accepted the message but decided Jones, having been caught, was weak and would betray the gang, and ordered him killed. Jones arrived at the meeting, saw the two gunmen waiting for him, and ran — straight to the police, to whom he gave several hideouts in exchange for protection. That is the chain that put the state's attorney's men on the Bellwood door.
  • The odd detail: the Outfit, not the police, broke the gang: the victims' organisation delivered the ultimatum that started the unravelling.
  • People: Julius "Babe" Jones — the weak link who lived
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_Kidnappers · http://homebrewedmojo.blogspot.com/2013/01/handsome-jack-gets-gunned.html
  • Confidence: Medium (encyclopaedia-level narrative; no primary press story read for the ambush itself)
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the rulebook already has the Outfit hunting monsters "because monsters are bad for business" — here is the real-record shape of it: the syndicate's ultimatum to a gang that preys on its own, and the one member who saw what was waiting for him and ran to the law.

The Peoria barber shop, the counterfeit fives and the Brooke Hart suspicion — November 1933

  • Where: a barber shop in Peoria, Ill.; a grand jury (the indictment story is datelined Peoria; no page read places it in Chicago federal court) ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩; (San Jose, Calif., for the Hart case)
  • What happened: On 13 November 1933 Peoria detectives (Detective Chief Fred Montgomery and Detective Robert Moran) questioned Russell Hughes, "intimate friend of Klutas," in a barber shop; Hughes drew, two detectives were wounded and Hughes was shot dead. The same day three alleged gang members were under arrest in Chicago. On 24 November Klutas was indicted for counterfeiting $5 notes with Rex Howard, George Milligan and others. In the same fortnight — because the whole country was hunting kidnappers after the Brooke Hart abduction in San Jose (Hart's two killers were lynched on 26 Nov 1933) — Klutas's name was floated in the Hart case; the Hart kidnappers proved to be two local men and Klutas had nothing to do with it.
  • The odd detail: a kidnapping gang whose second trade was printing money — the Secret Service's real jurisdiction (counterfeiting) reaches the gang before the police do.
  • People: Russell Hughes — d. 13 Nov 1933 · Fred Montgomery, Robert Moran — Peoria detectives · Rex Howard, George Milligan — co-indicted counterfeiters
  • Sources: http://genealogytrails.com/ill/whiteside/crimeklutas.html (Fitchburg Sentinel and Edwardsville Intelligencer, 13, 16 and 24 Nov 1933; Helena Independent, 21 Nov 1933) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_Kidnappers
  • Confidence: Medium-High (wire-service stories as transcribed on a genealogy site)
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the counterfeit indictment is the Secret Service's real doorway into the case — counterfeiting was Treasury's (the Secret Service's) jurisdiction, not the Bureau's; and since the Secret Service and Section 13 (K. Edgar Hoover's Bureau) are rival factions, the same paper ("tax evasion and counterfeiting," the cover story in the owner's own transcript) gives both of them a claim on Klutas ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩.

Walter Dietrich — the Michigan City escapee in the bathroom

  • Where: Indiana State Prison, Michigan City; 619 S. 24th Ave, Bellwood
  • What happened: Dietrich had ridden with the Baron Lamm bank gang and was serving life in Indiana when he went out with the ten men of the 26 September 1933 Michigan City break — the escape made with the pistols John Dillinger had had smuggled into the prison, the break that produced the Dillinger gang of that autumn (Pierpont, Makley, Clark, Hamilton). Rather than join Dillinger, Dietrich went to his old friend Klutas, and was taken shaving at Bellwood on 6 January 1934 — after the Dillinger gang's own Chicago weeks (their 13 Dec 1933 safe-deposit raid; Hamilton's killing of Det. William Shanley on the 14th), and before the 15 Jan East Chicago robbery and Tucson ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩ (Dillinger himself was in Crown Point jail from late January 1934). The Wikipedia article adds that on 25 June 1930 a car theft by Klutas and Dietrich ended in a gunfight that killed two officers (ODMP lists a Sheriff W. Sweet and a Marshal Aaron Bailey) — UNVERIFIED here beyond that citation.
  • The odd detail: the College Kidnappers and the Dillinger gang share a man; the same police force (Gilbert's state's attorney's squads, the Dillinger Squad) hunted both in the same weeks.
  • People: Walter Dietrich — the link man · Baron Lamm — the German ex-officer bank-robbery "planner," d. Dec 1930
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_Kidnappers · http://homebrewedmojo.blogspot.com/2013/01/handsome-jack-gets-gunned.html
  • Confidence: Medium (the 1930 double killing is single-cited; the Michigan City break and Dillinger's guns are well known but read here only in these two pages)
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): if the owner wants Dillinger in the campaign (§7), Dietrich is the hinge between the two seeds — a man who walked out of Michigan City with Dillinger's pistols and into the doppelgangers' house.

§3 — Edward J. "Easy Eddie" O'Hare, shot on Ogden Avenue, 8 November 1939

Owner's words (verbatim): "Edward O'Hare / On 8 November 1939, two unknown gunman assassinated Chicago's Attorney Edward O'Hare. They drove close to his car on Ogden Avenue and shot him. Apparently, O'Hare had double crossed The Outfit (secretive organized crime cartel) while managing some of its affairs had leaked information about the Outfit to authorities for years.[27]"

Note for the owner: every clause of this is right, and the paragraph is copied word for word from the Wikipedia article Chicago in the 1930s (ref: Russo, The Outfit, pp. 508–09) — the same article the Mart, Stock Yards, steel-strike and Ukrainian seeds come from; the Timeline of organized crime in Chicago words it differently ("drove alongside… double-dealt the Outfit") ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩. What the summary leaves out is the best part: the man O'Hare leaked to was Frank J. Wilson of the Treasury's Intelligence Unit — who became Chief of the United States Secret Service in 1937 and held the post when O'Hare was killed. In this setting the Secret Service is a faction of its own, separate from and rival to Section 13 (K. Edgar Hoover's Bureau) ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩. O'Hare was, in the real record, the Secret Service chief's man inside the Outfit.

The killing — Ogden Avenue between Talman and Rockwell, 3:20 p.m., Wednesday 8 November 1939

  • Where: Ogden Avenue (a diagonal running north-east from Cicero toward the Loop), between Talman Ave and Rockwell St, Chicago's West Side; O'Hare had just left his office at Sportsman's Park, 33rd and Laramie, Cicero
  • What happened: O'Hare, 46, was driving his black 1939 [UNVERIFIED — V_R17] Lincoln-Zephyr coupe north-east up Ogden toward the city. A dark sedan drew level and two men with shotguns fired into his head and neck (Wikipedia: "a volley of big-game slugs"; the Tribune of 9 Nov 1939: "Two charges of buckshot passed thru the glass") ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩; the coupe ran on into a trolley pole and an electric-light pole. The killers went east on Ogden [UNVERIFIED — V_R17] and were never caught. On the seat lay a cleaned and oiled Spanish-made .32 automatic, unfired — a gun he was not known to carry. In his pockets: $53, a crucifix, a rosary, a religious medallion, memoranda listing women's names, and notes on religious matters. The verse "The Clock of Life" ("The clock of life is wound but once / and no man has the power / to tell just when the hands will stop / at late or early hour") was not in his pocket: the 1939 Tribune says "Investigators who visited the Glencoe home found a framed verse over O'Hare's desk," and names no author — the pocket clipping and the Robert H. Smith attribution are in no page read ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩. Capt. Daniel Gilbert of the state's attorney's office (the same Gilbert who ran the Klutas raid in 1934, §1) took the case. Eight days later, on 16 November 1939, Al Capone was paroled from Terminal Island. No arrest was ever made; in January 2010 Alderman Ed Burke asked the police cold-case squad to reopen it after Jonathan Eig's Get Capone argued the Outfit had killed him for informing.
  • The odd detail: the poem framed over the dead man's desk ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩ about not knowing the hour, and the gun he had cleaned and oiled that week and never fired.
  • People: Edward J. O'Hare — the victim · Capt. Daniel "Tubbo" Gilbert — chief investigator, state's attorney's office · Aaron Harvey — Sportsman's Park photo-finish operator, named in the first Tribune story · "Kinky" Beckman — O'Hare's chauffeur/bodyguard, not with him · Leo Seltzer — Roller Derby promoter, an associate questioned
  • Sources: https://chicagology.com/notorious-chicago/edwardohare/ (Chicago Tribune, 9 Nov 1939, "GANGLAND SLAYS O'HARE, RACING TRACK CHIEF", and 14 Nov 1939) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_J._O'Hare · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Capone (parole 16 Nov 1939) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_organized_crime_in_Chicago
  • Confidence: High
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (note only: the killing is three years after the alpha's 1936; usable as a later module or as a foreshadowed death)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the module is the pockets. A rosary, a medal, a list of women's names, "notes on religious concerns" — and, framed over his desk at home, a verse about the hour of death ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩ — the real inventory reads like a man who knew what was coming and was making arrangements with something other than the Outfit. The informant — John Rogers, Frank J. Wilson and the switched jury, 1930–31

  • Where: St. Louis (the Post-Dispatch); Chicago federal court (Judge James H. Wilkerson, October 1931)
  • What happened: In 1930 O'Hare, then Capone's dog-track lawyer and business front, went to John Rogers, a St. Louis Post-Dispatch reporter, and asked to be put in touch with the Treasury. Rogers brought him to Frank J. Wilson of the Bureau of Internal Revenue's Intelligence Unit — the agent who would read two million documents to build the tax case. Wilson later wrote: "On the inside of the gang I had one of the best undercover men I have ever known: Eddie O'Hare." O'Hare led Wilson to the Capone bookkeeper who became a key witness, helped decode the ledgers, and — the decisive act — warned the government that Capone had fixed the jury. Judge Wilkerson swapped the venire with another federal trial's on the morning the case opened; Capone was convicted (October 1931) and sentenced to eleven years. Wilson became Chief of the U.S. Secret Service in 1937 and held it until 1946 — so when O'Hare died, his handler ran the Service.
  • The odd detail: the jury switch — the one courtroom trick that beat Capone — came from Capone's own lawyer.
  • People: Frank J. Wilson — IRS Intelligence Unit; Secret Service Chief 1937–46 (already a name in the rulebook's Capone ruling) · John Rogers — Post-Dispatch reporter, the go-between · Judge James H. Wilkerson · Elmer Irey — Wilson's chief
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_J._O'Hare (cites Ewing, Fateful Rendezvous, 1997; Collier's, 26 Apr 1947) · https://chicagology.com/notorious-chicago/edwardohare/
  • Confidence: High on the outline (Wilson's own memoir is the source for the quotation); Medium on exactly which bookkeeper (the page does not name him)

The fire — from a straw-lined cattle pen under the 43rd Street viaduct to eight blocks of ash, 4:14–8:30 p.m.

  • Where: Union Stock Yards, Halsted St between 41st and 43rd; the fire crossed Halsted eastward and burned the blocks between Halsted, Emerald, 41st and 42nd Streets; the surviving gate stands at Exchange Ave and Peoria St
  • What happened: At 4:14 p.m. on Saturday 19 May 1934 fire broke out in the straw-bedded wooden cattle pens below the 43rd Street viaduct — a still-lit cigarette dropped from the viaduct is the accepted cause; the fire marshal also allowed a match or spontaneous combustion. Chicago had had less than four inches of rain since 1 January; it was 92°F; the wind gusted to 60 mph and drove the fire "faster than a man could run." Alarms went from 5-11 to fifteen special calls; some 2,200 firemen and five-sixths of the city's engines and trucks came, and at least 31 suburban departments (Blue Island, Chicago Heights, Oak Lawn, Harvey…) sat in the emptied city firehouses. Six pumpers and a hook-and-ladder burned where they stood at the hydrants, with Engine Company 59's own firehouse; the Halsted Street "L" station burned and a streetcar with it ($500,000 to the Rapid Transit). Destroyed: the International Amphitheatre (the old Dexter Park Pavilion), the Stock Yard Inn at 42nd and Halsted (the stockmen's hotel), the Saddle and Sirloin Club on Dexter Park Avenue, the Drovers National Bank (4201 S. Halsted), the Live Stock National Bank (Exchange and Halsted), the new Exchange Building with a hundred-odd commission firms in it, radio station WAAF and the Daily Drovers' Journal. Telephones, electricity and gas in the district went out. One yards employee, about 33, was found under a fallen wall; more than 50 were hurt, most of them firemen overcome by smoke; several hundred cattle died (the sheep were mostly got out). Losses: $8,000,000 (≈$193M today). The column of smoke was seen by airmen over South Bend, 95 miles away; twenty police captains were sent to hold back the crowds; about 150 families were burned out. Mayor Kelly called it "the worst fire Chicago has known since the great one of 1871" — Fire Marshal Michael Corrigan said the same — and turned down Governor Horner's radio offer of the National Guard. The fire was held by 8:30 p.m.; the packing plants themselves were saved. Rebuilding began under engineer A. Epstein and the new International Amphitheatre opened on 1 December 1934, in time for the stock show. The first morning's wire figures were wilder — the Evening Star's front page of 20 May read "BLAZE SWEEPS CHICAGO RAZING 42 BLOCKS … FIRE MOST DISASTROUS SINCE 1871 HOLOCAUST," with "500 injured, 1,200 left homeless," three missing, $10,000,000 and 2,000–3,000 firemen; its inside page put the origin in a hay barn of the Union Stock Yards & Transit Co., added the Manhattan Brewery, P.   • Where: Sam's Place, a former dance hall (some say tavern) at 113th St and Green Bay Ave, the union's temporary headquarters; the prairie between it and the Republic Steel gate at 117th and Burley
  • What happened: After a rally at Sam's Place some 1,500–2,500 people — strikers, wives (about 200 women), children, sympathisers and the curious; white, Black and Mexican — set off across the open ground toward the mill to picket. §6 — The Merchandise Mart, 1930–39 — what an investigator would find inside

Owner's words (verbatim): "MERCHANDISE MART / 1930-Merchandise Mart was completed and was the world's largest commercial building at the time.(4 million sq ft)"

(Also in 09-22 archive: W05, NBC's "radio city" and battery room, 20 Oct 1930 — the entries below go around it rather than repeat it.)

The building — a wholesale city on the site of a dead railway station, opened 5 May 1930

  • Where: north bank of the Chicago River between Wells and Orleans, on the old Chicago & North Western Wells Street Station (abandoned 1911); today 222 W. Merchandise Mart Plaza
  • What happened: Marshall Field & Co. announced a $15 million wholesale building on 13 March 1927, doubled it to $30 million on 26 May 1928, and broke ground on 16 August 1928. Graham, Anderson, Probst & White (chief designer Alfred P. Shaw) put 458 concrete caissons 80–100 feet down to bedrock; John W. Griffiths & Sons employed up to 5,700 men over eighteen months. It opened on 5 May 1930, six months after the Crash: about 4,000,000 square feet, roughly 200,000 to a floor, eighteen floors plus a central tower (25 storeys, 340 feet in all), 577 feet along the river and 724 feet on Kinzie Street, corridors 650 feet long lined with showrooms, a private railway freight station with conveyors at ground level, restaurants, banks, a post office, a telegraph office and the Merchants' Club in the tower — "the world's largest business building," until the Pentagon in 1943. Field's president James Simpson, who also chaired the Chicago Plan Commission, built it to fold thirteen scattered warehouses into one wholesale market; Field's took floors three to six and leased the rest to hundreds of mercantile firms. The Depression gutted the idea: Field's wholesale lost $5 million in 1931 and $8 million in 1932 and shrank from four floors to a floor and a half; before the decade ended the company had quit wholesaling and found the building "burdensome" to manage. The federal government filled empty floors during the war, and Joseph P. Kennedy bought the whole thing in 1945 (reports say $12.5–13 million — less than half what it cost) — note only, per the owner. Kennedy's 1953 "Hall of Fame" busts came later still.
  • The odd detail: a building bigger than any in the world, opened into the worst year to open it, whose owner had abandoned its purpose within five years — floor after floor of showrooms for goods nobody was ordering.
  • People: James Simpson — Field's president, Plan Commission chairman · Alfred P. Shaw — chief designer · John W. Griffiths — contractor · Joseph P. Kennedy — buyer, 1945 (note only)
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchandise_Mart · https://chicagology.com/skyscrapers/skyscrapers033/ (period articles: the 1927–28 announcements, the statistics, the NBC layout) · https://chicagodetours.com/the-evolution-of-the-merchandise-mart/ · https://drloihjournal.blogspot.com/2024/01/the-history-of-merchandise-mart-chicago.html (hobby history page; used only for the "self-contained city" list) · https://lakefronthistorian.com/2021/10/11/the-merchandise-mart-hall-of-fame-and-the-evolution-of-selling/
  • Confidence: High on dates and dimensions (cost figures vary: $26M Wikipedia, $30M the 1928 announcement, $32M Frommer's); Medium on the wartime government tenancy (secondary pages only)

§7 — Dillinger's death, 22 July 1934 — "WE CAN MAKE SOMETHING FROM THAT"

Owner's words (verbatim): "JOHN DILLINGER / July 22nd 1934 John Dillinger killed by FBI. WE CAN MAKE SOMETHING FROM THAT."

(Also in 09-22 archive: C05, the Biograph ambush itself — Anna Sage, Polly Hamilton, Purvis. Other agents in this sweep cover the ambush; this section is what came after: the body, the morgue, the masks, the missing autopsy, the concrete, the relics and the doubts. One correction the archive already flagged: in July 1934 the Bureau was the Justice Department's Division of Investigation; it became the FBI in July 1935 — "killed by FBI" is a year early.)

The night of the body — Lincoln Avenue to the Cook County morgue, 22–24 July 1934

  • Where: the alley beside the Biograph, 2433 N. Lincoln Ave; Alexian Brothers Hospital; the Cook County morgue, Polk and Wood Streets; McCready's funeral home, 4506 Sheridan Road
  • What happened: At about 10:40 p.m. Dillinger came out of Manhattan Melodrama with Polly Hamilton (her orange skirt looked red under the marquee — hence Ana Cumpănaș's "Lady in Red") and turned toward the alley. Three agents fired — Clarence Hurt twice, Charles Winstead three times, Herman Hollis once (the Tribune's later retelling gives the killing shot to East Chicago police Sgt. Martin Zarkovich's .38; the Washington Times of 24 July named Inspector Samuel Cowley as the slayer — the Bureau's own story moved in the first days). One round entered the back of the neck, cut the cord and came out under the right eye. Two women bystanders, Theresa Paulas and Etta Natalsky, were hit. Before the body was cold the crowd was on its knees: "handkerchiefs were whipped out and used to mop up the blood," newspapers were dipped in it, scraps of blood-stained paper sold for a quarter, someone offered $100 for a stained brick and $1,000 for the shirt [UNVERIFIED — V_R17]. The body went to Alexian Brothers, then to the county morgue, where the Tribune found "lively" scenes — thousands "pushing and shoving" down the basement stairs to file past the slab and, later, a glass window; a 19-year-old girl peering through a ground-level pane; a woman "disappointed" that he "looks just like any other dead man" but planning to come back. Some 15,000 viewed him in a day and a half. The FBI's file says "every politician and his friend in Chicago crowded into the morgue" and that the deputy coroner took bribes for viewings and mask-making [UNVERIFIED — V_R17]. His sister Audrey Hancock identified him by a thigh scar from childhood barbed wire. When the body moved to McCready's on Sheridan Road, 5,000 people filled the 4500 block; six men made a "flying wedge" to get the casket to the hearse.
  • The odd detail: the blood on the pavement was gone within the hour — into handkerchiefs, newspapers and a quarter-a-scrap souvenir trade.
  • People: Clarence Hurt, Charles Winstead, Herman Hollis — the agents who fired · Samuel Cowley — inspector, publicly credited on 24 July · Martin Zarkovich — East Chicago police sergeant · Melvin Purvis — SAC Chicago · Audrey Hancock — sister · Theresa Paulas, Etta Natalsky — wounded bystanders

  • Confidence: High (the count of who fired what is contested between sources, as noted)
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar; BENDS in one name: the Bureau's agents (Purvis, Cowley, Hurt, Winstead, Hollis) work for K. Edgar Hoover's Bureau — Section 13's house, a rival of the Secret Service faction — not J. Edgar's ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): in a world with vampires, a crowd mopping up a dead man's blood on a summer night is not colour, it is a plot: who collected the handkerchiefs, and what were they for?

The death masks — four faces, two makers, one that never touched him

  • Where: the Cook County morgue basement; Reliance Dental Manufacturing Co., Chicago; Worsham College of Embalming
  • What happened: As many as four moulds were taken from the face within hours. Prof. D. E. Ashworth of the Worsham College of Embalming cast one — confiscated by Chicago police officer Alfred Mulvaney. Harold N. Parsons of the Bureau exhibited a cast at an American Association for the Advancement of Science meeting in St. Louis. The funeral director complained that the moulds had stripped hair and skin from the face. The plaster shows the exit wound under the right eye. A cast (McCrone does not say whose, or which of the four it descends from) ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩ was examined by McCrone Associates: plaster over an interior black-rubber mask carrying wax particles and no hairs or skin cells — a copy of a copy, not a face-cast; "the black rubber… was not used for direct replication of facial features." Masks have sold at auction (CBS Chicago); the FBI keeps one on display with his straw hat, glasses and cigar [UNVERIFIED — V_R17].

The autopsy that went missing for fifty years — brown eyes, a kept brain, a lost fingerprint card

  • Where: Cook County morgue (the "coroner's protocol"); Crown Hill Cemetery, Indianapolis (the exhumation permit)
  • What happened: The official autopsy report — gunshot wounds, scars, illnesses, the last meal — vanished and turned up roughly fifty years later "stuffed into a corner" of the old morgue in a shopping bag, found by an office worker; a copy reached ABC7's I-Team. It records brown eyes; Dillinger's were blue or grey. Cook County medical examiner Dr. Robert Stein's answer: eyes cloud after death and colour is hard to read. The brain had been removed — on 1 August 1934 John W. Dillinger Sr. took out a permit to exhume his son after hearing the morgue had kept it and that "portions of the body" had gone as souvenirs; he was assured it was ordinary autopsy practice, and said "I know it's my son. I wish to the Lord Almighty it wasn't." The fingerprint card was missing from the morgue for more than thirty years — this is Jay Robert Nash's claim, reported by Wikipedia inside its account of his theory, not an independently checked fact ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩. The corpse had a rheumatic heart — which Dr. Patrick H. Weeks, the Indiana State Prison physician, had already recorded as "a distinct heart lesion" in Dillinger's prison years (published 1938), so the heart argues for the identification.
  • The odd detail: a public enemy's autopsy in a shopping bag in a corner for half a century.

The road to Crown Hill — wicker casket, 2,500 mourners, 2½ tons of concrete, 22–25 July 1934

  • Where: Mooresville, Ind. (the father's farm); the Hancock home, Maywood, Ind.; Crown Hill Cemetery, Indianapolis (entered at 32nd and Northwestern to dodge the crowd)
  • What happened: The body left Chicago in a wicker temporary casket; 3,500 people viewed it on a mortician's slab on the way, before L. W. Howe of Indianapolis embalmed it; nearly all of Mooresville's 2,300 came to look [UNVERIFIED — V_R17]. The funeral was at the home of his sister Audrey and her husband Emmett Hancock in Maywood: about 2,500 filed through on Tuesday 24 July, and the Rev. Charles M. Fillmore read a short service on Wednesday the 25th. Some 5,000 gathered at Crown Hill (Benjamin Harrison's cemetery). An oilman, H. G. Cross, "confirmed he telegraphed Dillinger an offer of $10,000 for his son's body" — Cross was the bidder, not a go-between ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩; the father refused. Hearing of plans to rob the grave, the family had 2½ tons of concrete (some accounts add scrap iron) poured over the $165 pine coffin, and a police detachment under Sgt. Otis Baker was set to guard the grave after the service ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩. The headstone has been replaced several times as visitors chip it; people still leave coins and take earth. In 2019 a nephew obtained an exhumation permit to settle the identity question; the History Channel withdrew, Crown Hill objected, and the reburial date came and went.
  • The odd detail: the $10,000 offer to tour the corpse — and the concrete poured, in the family's words, to stop exactly that.
  • People: Audrey and Emmett Hancock · Rev. Charles M. Fillmore · L. W. Howe — embalmer · H. G. Cross — the oilman who telegraphed the $10,000 offer ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩

The doubts — Jimmy Lawrence, and Jay Robert Nash's list (folklore, labelled)

  • Where: Chicago's North Side; print, 1970 and 1983
  • What happened: Dillinger had lived openly on the North Side that summer as "Jimmy Lawrence," eating at the Seminary Restaurant (Lincoln and Fullerton), going to Cubs games, greeting his lawyer while the lawyer stood talking to a policeman — with $15,000 on his head. Jay Robert Nash's Dillinger: Dead or Alive? (1970) and The Dillinger Dossier (1983) argued the Bureau had shot a small-time look-alike named Jimmy Lawrence and covered it up because it had already killed innocents in the hunt: the corpse was too tall, had brown eyes, lacked Dillinger's lip scar, had a tooth he lacked, thinner eyebrows, a rheumatic heart, and prints that (Nash says) did not match; the open cuts on the face came from agents firing down from the theatre roof and were only "described through the media as 'scars resulting from inept plastic surgery'" (Nash's claim is the reverse of what this line first said) ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩; the concrete was to prevent exhumation; the Bureau "planted" the prints. Historians and the Bureau reject it (Nash's books are error-strewn; he lost a 1990 suit against CBS over a Simon & Simon episode that used his theory); the heart, the thigh scar and the family's identification answer most of the list. The pieces, though, are all real: the eye colour in the protocol, the missing protocol, the missing card (reported only as part of Nash's case) ⟨fixed: V_R17⟩, the concrete, the acid-burned fingertips of the real 1934 plastic surgery, the kept brain. Ana Cumpănaș got $5,000 of the reward, was pardoned of her Indiana convictions by Gov. Harry G. Leslie on 15 December 1934, and was deported to Romania anyway in 1936; she died there in 1947.
  • The odd detail: the man the Bureau shot had been calling himself by another man's name for weeks.

Folklore, labelled — the sheet, the arm, and the Smithsonian

  • Where: the Cook County morgue (the photograph); Indiana (oral tradition); the Smithsonian's mailbag
  • What happened: A wire photograph from the morgue showed the body under a sheet with a tent-like rise at the midsection — almost certainly the arm, stiff in rigor mortis. The autopsy "reported nothing unusual about the man." Legend says — none of it is history, but a 1930s Chicagoan would have seen the photograph.
