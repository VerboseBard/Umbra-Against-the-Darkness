PART ONE — TOLEDO AND ERIE (Ohio and Pennsylvania shore)

The Battle of Toledo, day by day — the Auto-Lite strike, 12 April–9 June 1934 (deeper cut on R07 and R03)

  • Where: Electric Auto-Lite Company plant, North End of Toledo — the fighting centred on Elm and Champlain Streets, the Elm Street Bridge and "Chestnut Hill" (Toledo Public Library's exhibit gives the streets; no street number was found).
  • What happened: Federal Labor Union 18384 (AFL) won a five-day recognition strike on 23 Feb, then struck again on 12 April when management would not negotiate. Judge Roy Stuart's injunction capped pickets at 25 a gate; on 5 May the Lucas County Unemployed League leaders Ted Selander and Sam Pollock wrote him that they would "deliberately and specifically violate" it — and did (picket lines of 4 on 7 May, 40 on 8 May, 1,000 on 21 May, 4,000 on 22 May, 6,000 on the morning of 23 May). On 23 May deputies arrested American Workers Party organiser Louis Budenz; a crowd put at nearly 10,000 besieged the plant for seven hours with stones, bricks and bottles. About 900 Ohio Guardsmen arrived at 5:30 a.m. on 24 May (eight rifle companies, three machine-gun companies); around 10 p.m. they fired into the crowd and killed Frank Hubay (27, shot four times) and Steve Cyigon (20) — neither an Auto-Lite worker. At least 15 more were shot; the Guard ran out of tear-gas bombs on the evening of 24 May and threw the bricks back ⟨fixed: V_R40⟩. The Central Labor Council counted 85 of 103 unions pledged to a general strike by 31 May; a settlement on 2–3 June gave recognition, a 5 per cent raise and a 35-cent minimum, and 20,000 marched in a torchlight parade before it. The final Guard troops left 5 June.
  • The odd detail: The company had bought about $11,000 of tear- and vomit-gas munitions and hired roughly 1,500 strikebreakers — and when the gas ran short, a plane from Cleveland was hired to fly low over the plant and drop a shipment of tear gas by air (Wikipedia's account). The Guard included high-school-aged boys, one of whom was supposed to be receiving his diploma that evening; and the city had laid off 150 police the year before, so its own cops were half-sympathetic.
  • People: Frank Hubay (27) and Steve Cyigon (20) — the two killed; Louis Budenz — AWP secretary, arrested twice; Ted Selander and Sam Pollock — Lucas County Unemployed League leaders; Clement O. Miniger — Auto-Lite president, himself arrested for the gas nuisance; Judge Roy R. Stuart — injunction; Gov. George White — sent the Guard; Charles Phelps Taft II — presidential mediator, son of the ex-president; Frances Perkins — Labor Secretary.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auto-Lite_strike_(1934) ; https://tlcpllaborhistory.omeka.net/exhibits/show/toledo-labor-history/auto-lite-strike
  • Confidence: High on dates and deaths; troop totals differ (900 arriving on 24 May; about 1,300–1,350 in all). The Toledo Public Library exhibit dates the strike start to 25 Feb and settlement to 1 June; Wikipedia gives 23 Feb and 2–3 June — use Wikipedia's.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar. (Mediator Taft is Wm. H. Taft's son — pre-1916 president, unaffected.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Ohio's largest peacetime call-up, machine guns on the bridges, teenage soldiers, a plane over the plant: a real event with the mise-en-scène of a haunting. Ask who ordered the second volley at 10 p.m. Neither dead man worked there — why were two strangers in the crowd?

"Toledo's Summer of Hell" — the bank crash of 16 June–17 August 1931

  • Where: Downtown Toledo — Security-Home Trust, Ohio Savings Bank & Trust, Commerce Guardian, Commercial Savings, American Bank.
  • What happened: Security-Home Trust failed to open on 16 June 1931; Ohio Savings froze deposits for 60 days on 18 June (the EH.Net review dates the moratorium 17 June — sources differ); on 17 August three more banks stayed shut. Five banks with 34 branches were closed and more than $100 million in deposits frozen — about 70 per cent of the city's banking deposits, and Toledo depositors lost more per head than any other US city. Police recalled officers from vacation and the Guard was alerted. Insiders had moved first: Security-Home officers took out about $1.5 million in the last six weeks (confirmed: EH.Net says officers, relatives and their businesses) against $679,971 from the public on the final day, and a vice-president withdrew $1,000 (more than his balance) [UNVERIFIED — V_R40: the $679,971 and $1,000 figures are on none of the pages read]. Three bankers were indicted in 1931; no one was convicted. Toledo ended with one in three workers on relief (a figure the search summaries repeat; check before quoting).
  • The odd detail: The county prosecutor who later broke the Licavoli gang (see next entry) — Frazier Reams — appears in one account as a director of Commercial Savings, one of the failed banks. (Sources spell his first name Frazier, Frazer and Frank; the director link rests on one review — the EH.Net review does state it — "Frank Reams", a Commercial Savings director who later led the prosecution — so it is single-sourced rather than unconfirmed.) ⟨fixed: V_R40⟩
  • People: Stacey McNary — Security-Home president; L. L. Marshall — state senator who ran the 1933 inquiry; Frazier Reams — Commercial Savings director / later prosecutor; Harold Fraser — director who defended the bankers.
  • Sources: https://eh.net/book_reviews/banksters-bosses-and-smart-money-a-social-history-of-the-great-toledo-bank-crash-of-1931/ ; https://toledogazette.wordpress.com/2011/06/21/toledos-summer-of-hell/
  • Confidence: High on the collapse (two sources agree on 16 June/17 Aug); Medium on totals; UNVERIFIED on Reams's exact role.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the setting's Depression is "caused differently" but the bank panics land on the real record).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The banker who cleans out his own account while the doors are still open is the low-level version of every "no one is clean" villain. Put the vault of a failed Toledo bank in the ninth month of its receivership — and a Toledo prosecutor who owes someone.

Birmingham — Toledo's Hungarian quarter, St. Stephen's, and Tony Packo's — 1890–1930s

  • Where: East Toledo, Front Street and Paine Avenue; St. Stephen, King of Hungary parish; Tony Packo's Café, 1902 Front Street.
  • What happened: In 1890 the National Malleable Castings Company moved about 200 Hungarian workers from its Cleveland plant to its new East Toledo foundry on Front Street; the neighbourhood took the name of the English steel town. Most settlers came from the "Paloc" counties of north-central Hungary (Heves, Abaúj, Gömör). They got their own parish in 1898 — St. Stephen, King of Hungary — with about a hundred families registered the next year, having first worshipped at Sacred Heart. In 1932, in the worst of the Depression, Tony and Rose Packo opened a sandwich shop at 1902 Front Street on a $100 loan; Packo trademarked his Hungarian sausage on rye with chili sauce in 1935. Library photos show a Birmingham branch library on Paine Avenue (about 1930) and Birmingham School at Paine and Valentine (about 1935).
  • The odd detail: Both men the Guard killed in May 1934 carry what look like Hungarian surnames (Hubay; Cyigon ≈ Csigány) — but no source read this session says they were from Birmingham; treat any link as UNVERIFIED and worth one afternoon in the Blade's 1934 files.
  • People: Tony and Rose Packo — café owners; National Malleable Castings Co. — the employer that made the colony.
  • Sources: https://tlcpleasttoledo.omeka.net/exhibits/show/east-toledo/birmingham ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Packo%27s_Cafe ; a search summary from toledosattic.org "Birmingham Days" (page itself returned 404)
  • Confidence: High on the settlement and the café; Low on any connection to the Auto-Lite dead.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — Hungary was inside Austria-Hungary; the setting has Austria-Hungary surviving as Germany's junior partner, so the "old country" was never broken up; the Hungarian parish of 1930s Toledo still has an emperor in Vienna to pray for.

The murder of Jack Kennedy and the Licavoli gang — Point Place, Toledo, 7 July 1933 (deeper cut on R07 and R26)

  • Where: Edgewater Drive at Point Place, north Toledo; Kennedy's Studio Club, 512 Superior Street; his saloon at 513 St. Clair; Licavoli's house on Pemberton Drive.
  • What happened: Jack Kennedy — Irish bootlegger and nightclub owner — was shot on Edgewater Drive at about 9:40 p.m. on 7 July 1933 while walking home to his Point Place cottage with his girlfriend Audrey Ralls. A Point Place history page counts twelve dum-dum bullets and twenty-two wounds and says the two guns (.38 and .45) were found on 11 July. Police arrested Sarafino "Wop English" Sinatra, Ralph Carsello and John Mirabella as they played croquet on Yonnie Licavoli's front lawn [UNVERIFIED — V_R40: the croquet detail is on neither the Toledo police museum page nor the Point Place page read; both do confirm 7 July 1933, 12 dum-dum bullets, 22 wounds and the death sentence for English]. Kennedy's funeral drew more than 500. Kennedy's price war was the cause: he sold beer at 15 cents a glass against the Licavolis' 25 cents. Prosecutor Frazier Reams then seized slot machines and shut the gang's gambling to starve it; English was convicted and sentenced to death late in 1933, and Licavoli got life in 1934 — the Toledo police museum credits the gang with 14 murders in all.
  • The odd detail: The arrests were made during a lawn croquet game — in the leader's own front garden — three days after the killing. Kennedy's earlier girlfriend, Louise Bell, had been machine-gunned six months before.
  • People: Jack Kennedy — bootlegger/club owner, killed; Audrey Ralls — his girlfriend, eyewitness; Yonnie Licavoli — Purple Gang boss; Sarafino "Wop English" Sinatra — chief enforcer; Ralph Carsello, John Mirabella — gang; Frazier Reams — prosecutor.

  • Confidence: High on date/place; Medium on the ballistics detail (one amateur local-history page); parole year differs (Wikipedia 1971, the local page 1972 — Wikipedia adds that Gov. Rhodes commuted his sentence in 1969 and that he died 17 Sept 1973).

The Semple brothers and Westfield Jimmy Salamone — Erie, Pennsylvania's rum run, 1920–1933

  • Where: Erie, Pa. (West Third Street neighbourhood; Six Mile Creek; Cherry Street garage; Freeport Beach in North East); Port Dover, Ontario, across the lake; the speakeasies Pickwick Club, Killarney Yacht Club, Laura's, and "the 1008."
  • What happened: Joe Semple Sr., a Hungarian immigrant and self-taught mechanic, ran Erie's most successful smuggling outfit with brothers Mike, John and Eddie, employing about 35 men from his West Third Street neighbourhood as drivers, guards and delivery hands. On a peak night they made three round trips to Port Dover, Ontario, bringing about 1,000 cases and clearing about $11,000. In July 1931 the youngest, Eddie "the Kid" Semple, was shot in the face on a run near Ripley, New York — set up, the local telling goes, by Westfield Jimmy Salamone, an Erie-area gangster, at a time when the Magaddino family of Buffalo had noticed Semple's profits. Sheriff Pat Brown had seized fishing tugs in late 1925. When repeal came on 5 Dec 1933, Erie's first state liquor store opened at 618 State Street on 27 Dec.
  • The odd detail: An Erie Reader piece proposes unopened Prohibition whisky may still lie buried or sunk along the shore — a rum boat capsized unloading on Presque Isle's frozen beach in late 1926, and cases were dropped offshore "with lines attached" for later retrieval.
  • People: Joe Semple Sr. — Hungarian-born smuggler; Eddie "the Kid" Semple — shot 1931; "Westfield Jimmy" Salamone — local gangster; Miles B. Kitts — Erie's mayor at the peak; Pat Brown — sheriff; David Frew — author of Midnight Herring (2006), the standard book on Lake Erie rum-running.
  • Sources: https://eriehistory.blogspot.com/2015/07/prohibition-eries-rebellion.html ; https://www.eriereader.com/article/upfront-free-whiskey-bring-shovel
  • Confidence: Medium — a local history blog and a weekly; the Magaddino link and who set up Eddie are the blog's telling.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the supply ran from Canada, whose own status in the setting is Britain-adjacent; the run itself is unaffected).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Cases dropped offshore on a line to be pulled up later is a tradecraft a Secret Service or IHS team might find — a cache marked by a length of cord under the ice. A Hungarian smuggler in a Pennsylvania port, a Hungarian parish in Toledo: a Hungarian crescent along the lake that neither Church nor Bureau maps.

The Erie Land Light — Erie's oldest lighthouse becomes a park — 1818 / 1867 / transfer 1934

  • Where: Bluff overlooking Presque Isle Bay, Erie, Pa. (the "Land Lighthouse," today in Lighthouse Park; street address not confirmed).
  • What happened: The first tower went up in 1818 — one of the first the United States built on the Great Lakes — and had to be replaced twice because of settling (a brick tower in 1858, then the present sandstone tower in 1867 on "eight layers of solid oak twelve-inch-square timbers"). When the lake-side Presque Isle Light opened in 1873 the old tower became "the Land Light"; it was discontinued in 1880, but local protest made Congress vote $7,000 in 1884 and it was relit on 1 July 1885. The last keeper, George W. Miller, served to 1899. By 1934 the federal government had transferred the property to the city of Erie for public-park use; caretakers lived in the keeper's dwelling.
  • The odd detail: The lantern room was later shipped to Marblehead Lighthouse in Ohio, and archaeologists found the original 1818 foundation 200 yards west of the tower in 2000. A folk story (from a search summary, not confirmed on the lighthouse page) says a workman was drowned crossing Misery Bay in a storm while building the Presque Isle light and haunts it — "legend says."
  • People: John Bone — first keeper (1818–1833); George W. Miller — last keeper (1885–1899); Alice J. Fargo — assistant keeper (1873–1881).
  • Sources: https://www.lighthousefriends.com/light.asp?ID=289
  • Confidence: High on the tower history; the ghost story is unconfirmed.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened; the 1934 transfer holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A lighthouse decommissioned by Washington and handed to a city in a Depression year, its own first tower buried nearby: an ideal drop or a shrine, with a foundation the map does not show.

PART TWO — BUFFALO, LACKAWANNA AND EAST AURORA

Father Nelson Baker and the Basilica at Lackawanna — the "Padre of the Poor," d. 29 July 1936

  • Where: Our Lady of Victory Institutions, Lackawanna, N.Y. (just south of Buffalo, in the Bethlehem Steel town).
  • What happened: Baker was born in Buffalo in 1842, served briefly with the 74th New York Militia at Gettysburg in July 1863, and was ordained in 1876. He built a "City of Charity" of about fifteen buildings — orphanage, protectory, maternity hospital, nurses' school, a 275-bed hospital, a working farm; he is said to have sheltered some 100,000 children, who arrived with "Father Baker's" on a tag round the neck. He funded it with the Association of Our Lady of Victory (25 cents a year), built by writing to postmasters across the country for the names of local Catholic women — described as the first direct-mail campaign in the US. The basilica (ground broken 1921, consecrated 25 May 1926 by Bishop William Turner and Cardinal Hayes, made a minor basilica two months later) cost $3.2 million with no parish debt; a 165-foot dome, four 18-foot copper angels, white Georgia marble outside, 40-plus kinds of Italian marble inside, a grotto of black lava rock from Vesuvius. He died on 29 July 1936 at 94; about half a million people came to view the body; Pius XI and President Roosevelt sent condolences and the BBC broadcast the requiem. He was declared Venerable on 14 January 2011; his casket was reinterred in the grotto in 1999 with about 6,000 present.
  • The odd detail: In the Depression, some men who had already been given help would run to the back of the line to be given more; Baker is quoted: "do you think I will be asked if I gave?" The Association mailed a nation's worth of small donations to a steel town's back road.
  • People: Nelson Baker — priest; Bishop William Turner — Buffalo, consecrated the basilica; Pius XI — pope; Emile Ulrich — basilica architect.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson_Baker ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Lady_of_Victory_Basilica_(Lackawanna,_New_York) ; https://www.americamagazine.org/faith/2021/07/29/american-civil-war-veteran-saint-baker-orphans-241133/
  • Confidence: High on the outline; the half-million figure is from America magazine (other sources give lower numbers for the funeral Mass itself — a search summary says about 50,000 on 3 Aug 1936; UNVERIFIED which figure is which).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar; pre-1916 for the founding. (BBC broadcast: Britain is beaten, not gone; plausible.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A saintly priest whose mailing list is a country-wide network of Catholic women who reply to a letter is a gift to IHS — a Church intelligence net hidden inside a charity. And a candidate for "the saint next to the steel mill": what does the basilica's angel-topped dome guard?

Broadway–Fillmore: Buffalo's Polish East Side and the 300 houses the Terminal ate — 1870–1940

  • Where: East Side, Buffalo — St. Stanislaus (1886 building), Broadway Market (1894), Dom Polski, Corpus Christi Church (161 Clark Street, built 1907–09), "Kaisertown."
  • What happened: After 1870 a permanent Polish settlement grew around St. Stanislaus, "Mother Church of Polonia in Buffalo"; the East Side became the second-largest Polish-American community in the country, with over 75,000 people of Polish descent in the city by 1940, mostly labourers in the rail yards, foundries and tanneries. Corpus Christi (architects Schmill and Gould, dark sandstone, copper domes; now Pauline Fathers, on the National Register since 2007) was the seventh of fourteen Polish Catholic parishes. A search-summary of the parish history says that in 1929 about 300 homes within the parish were demolished to make way for the New York Central Terminal. Dom Polski ("Polish Home") opened in 1889 as "The Polish Literary and Assembly Rooms Association, Inc." (same source). A Dyngus Day parade (Easter Monday) is anecdotally the country's largest.
  • The odd detail: The Terminal (R07) was built for 200 trains a day on land that was, until 1929, a neighbourhood — the 300 houses are the price of the "Babylonian" city's grand entrance.
  • People: Karl G. Schmill and Gould — Corpus Christi architects; Pauline Fathers — current owners.
  • Sources: https://nyheritage.org/exhibits/buffalos-neighborhoods-exploring-our-migrant-immigrant-heritage/polonia-polish-legacy-east ; https://www.preservationready.org/Buildings/CorpusChristiChurch ; (300-houses and Dom Polski lines from search summaries of Buffalo parish histories — pages not read in full)
  • Confidence: High on population and buildings; Medium on the 300 houses.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Poland in the setting is a client crown of Germany (Brest-Litovsk shape, ALTERNATE_HISTORY §30). The Buffalo Polish parish still has a homeland to send money to, but no 1918 rebirth — an old country under a German-appointed king is a different grievance from a reborn republic.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A church-plus-club-plus-hall neighbourhood with its own radio priest (next entry) is the ordinary population; the Terminal that cut it in half is the place a courier passes without noticing whose rooftops he crosses.

Father Justin Figas's Rosary Hour — Corpus Christi, Buffalo, 6 December 1931 onward

  • Where: Corpus Christi Church and the Kolbe Center ("Girls Club"), 174–176 Clark Street, Buffalo; station WEBR.
  • What happened: Fr. Justin (Justyn) Figas, a Conventual Franciscan born in McClure, Pa., in 1886, had begun a Polish-language rosary broadcast in 1927 over WKEN and formed a six-station network from Buffalo's WEBR in 1931. On Sunday 6 December 1931 his Rosary Hour went out from Corpus Christi to Polish communities across America; it grew to about 79 stations and an estimated 5 million listeners, the oldest Polish-language Catholic radio programme in the world. He hosted it until his death in 1959. The parish built the Kolbe Center in 1928; the programme went out from its second floor.
  • The odd detail: A Polish-American weekly says he was "one of only 50 trained exorcists in the Catholic Church at his time" and that the author's aunt was his secretary — a claim only on that page; treat as UNVERIFIED family lore. (The same piece links the 1949 St. Louis exorcism that inspired The Exorcist to a different Polish priest, Fr. Gregory Holewinski, not Figas.)
  • People: Fr. Justin Figas OFM Conv. (1886–1959) — radio priest; Fr. Gregory Holewinski — St. Louis, 1949 case (not Buffalo).
  • Sources: https://ampoleagle.com/exorcist-based-on-story-of-polish-priest-p17620-239.htm ; (dates, station names and audience figures from search summaries of poloniatrail.com and corpuschristibuffalo.org — pages not read in full; olaprovince.org returned 404)

Canal Street and "The Hooks" — the wickedest street in the world, buried by the thirties — 1829–1940

  • Where: Two blocks of Canal Street between Commercial and Erie Streets, Buffalo harbour (with Dante Place, "The Hooks," the Italian quarter that replaced it); the Commercial Slip, the Erie Canal's western terminus.
  • What happened: Laid out in 1829 as Cross Street and called Canal Street by 1854, the street was the end of the Erie Canal: by the 1880s it held about 400 working women, 93 saloons and 15 concert-hall dives, and "a murder every day" is local lore. Dug's Dive, run by William Douglas, a former slave, is the supposed first "dive bar" (patrons went down steps from the towpath). The 1895 deepening of the canal and the railroads killed the trade; immigrant families — mainly Italian — moved in; the harbour end of the canal was mostly filled in by the 1930s, and later the Thruway, the Skyway and Memorial Auditorium were built over the district, leaving the Commercial Slip "buried and forgotten" until archaeologists dug it up.
  • The odd detail: The tale told of the saloonkeepers is a poisoned drink, robbed clothes, and a body pushed into the canal — and in 1901 the neighbourhood's women attacked visiting sex workers from New York City and police had to escort them back north.
  • People: William Douglas — Dug's Dive proprietor.
  • Sources: https://buffalostreets.com/2014/03/31/canalpart1/ ; search summary of https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canalside (fill-in dates)
  • Confidence: Medium — colourful local-history blog; "a murder a day" is lore.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened; the 1930s filling in is domestic.

Two power stations: Adams (1895) and Queenston–Chippawa (1922) — how the Falls lit the lakes

  • Where: Edward Dean Adams Power Plant, Niagara Falls, N.Y.; Queenston–Chippawa (later Sir Adam Beck) Generating Station, Niagara Falls, Ontario.
  • What happened: The Adams plant (1895) was "the first large-scale, alternating current electric generating plant in the world": ten Westinghouse generators of 5,000 hp each, 50,000 hp in all, at 25 Hz, on Tesla's and Lamme's polyphase design; power was stepped up to 10,000 volts for medium distances and 20,000 volts to reach Buffalo, Lockport and Tonawanda, and the water returned through a 7,000-foot tailrace tunnel; it ran until 1961.   • The odd detail: The world's first big AC plant ran at 25 Hz — the frequency the Niagara region's old equipment still used long after the rest of North America went to 60.
  • People: Edward Dean Adams — financier; Nikola Tesla and Benjamin G. Lamme — polyphase designers; Sir Adam Beck — Ontario Hydro chairman, d. 1925.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adams_Power_Plant_Transformer_House ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Adam_Beck_Hydroelectric_Generating_Stations
