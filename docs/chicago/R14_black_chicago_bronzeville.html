Overview

The Black Belt of the 1930s was a city of roughly a quarter-million people packed into a seven-mile strip from 22nd to 63rd Street between State and Cottage Grove, renamed "Bronzeville" by a cosmetics magnate's newspaper in 1930 — the year the Binga State Bank failed ⟨fixed: V_R14⟩. It ran on its own institutions and this file maps them with dates and addresses: the policy wheels (the Jones brothers, Teddy Roe, Big Jim Martin; 38 wheels and 400 stations; relief money going into the drum; the Outfit's takeover waiting at the decade's end), the press (Abbott's Defender, Overton's Bee, Bibb's Whip, Davis's ANP wire), the Pullman porters as a 10,000-man national courier network, the churches from Olivet's 20,000 Baptists to nine Spiritualists in a basement and the healer whose funeral drew 60,000, gospel born in a synagogue in the month Dorsey's wife died, the Stroll and its clubs (the Regal and Savoy at 47th, Capone's Grand Terrace with its white Bechstein, the Sunset, the 24-hour Club DeLisa), race records and the man who owned 3,000 of their copyrights, sport (Joe Louis owned by two policy banks; Rube Foster dying in the Kankakee asylum; 40,000 at the East–West game; the Savoy Big Five becoming the Globetrotters), politics (De Priest, Mitchell, Dawson, Dickerson — two of them Southern War officers in the setting), the colour line (Binga bombed five times, Hansberry v. Lee, the Wells Homes built to federal segregation rules), the 1931 eviction riot with its unexplained third corpse, Detroit's Black Bottom and Ossian Sweet's consumptive family, and the strangest find — a 1936 Chicago hotel murder signed "Black Legion Game" in lipstick. Where the 1916 break bites, it is flagged: no France for the 370th, no Nazis for Owens and Schmeling, no Moscow for the eviction fighters or Richard Wright's John Reed Club, and a Haiti still occupied when Katherine Dunham went to learn Vodou.

A. The policy kings and the underground economy

Policy — the Black Belt's own lottery, and what it was worth — 1930–1942

  • Where: The whole South Side "Black Belt", State Street to Cottage Grove, 22nd to 63rd; wheels and "stations" (drop-in shops, back rooms of tailors, barbers, candy stores) everywhere; the West Side Black district along Lake Street had its own king.
  • What happened: "Policy" was the illegal but tolerated numbers lottery of Black Chicago: a player picked numbers (a "gig" of three), bet a nickel or a dime at a station, and the "wheel" — a named operation such as the Erie-Buffalo, Belmont or "Old Reliable" books — drew numbered balls from a drum twice or more a day. Slips came back in printed lists; runners, writers, pick-up men, cashiers and printers gave the Black Belt thousands of jobs when nothing else did. Edward P. Jones's operation alone was said to be taking $2,000 a day in 1930 and $10,000 a day by 1938; by 1946 the Jones-Roe wheels netted more than $25 million a year. A brochure printed by an anti-vice organisation (undated in Chepesiuk) ⟨fixed: V_R14⟩ counted 38 wheels and about 400 stations on the South Side and reckoned Black Chicagoans spent roughly $600,000 a month on policy, of which 17–20 percent came out of relief money. The game was protected: the machine bagman Billy Skidmore took $250 at a time from wheel operators (a witness, Ezra Leake, described the payments to a grand jury and then refused to testify at the May 1943 trial; the case collapsed). On 2 February 1942, 26 major policy operators surrendered and posted $5,000 bonds in a rare crackdown; a Crime Commission count afterward found only 9 of 28 big wheels still turning.
  • The odd detail: Relief money going straight into the wheels — one in five policy nickels was a government nickel — and a bagman collecting "sometimes twice a day."
  • People: Edward P. "Ed" Jones — biggest South Side wheel operator, ex-tailor; Billy Skidmore — junk-dealer and political bagman for gambling protection; Ezra Leake — grand-jury witness who went silent at trial (fined $500); Jake "Greasy Thumb" Guzik — the Outfit's payoff man.
  • Sources: http://www.crimemagazine.com/chicago-outfit-makes-its-move-0 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roe
  • Confidence: High on the figures quoted (Chepesiuk / Wikipedia); the names of individual wheels (Erie-Buffalo, Belmont, Old Reliable) are from the Chepesiuk article; the Encyclopedia of Chicago's own policy entry could not be fetched this session (site unreachable) — its summary in search results says policy was "integral to the economy of Bronzeville" and protected by politicians courting Black votes. Ben Franklin store date UNVERIFIED (see next entry).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The wheel is a divination engine that 100,000 people feed twice a day. A "gig" that keeps hitting for one Spiritualist mother, a wheel whose numbers someone is choosing rather than drawing, a policy-slip cipher used by a cult to pass orders through 400 stations — and every faction wants the printer's plates. Section 13 already knows the Outfit is coming for the wheels.

The Jones brothers — Ed, George and McKissack, "the Policy Kings" — 1920s–1946

  • Where: Jones Brothers Tailor Shop (policy taken at the back door); later the Jones Brothers Ben Franklin Store on 47th Street, Bronzeville; homes on South Michigan Avenue.
  • What happened: Three brothers — Edward P. (the leader), George and McKissack "Mack" Jones — turned a nickel game run out of the back of their tailor shop into the largest policy syndicate in the country. They bought a Ben Franklin variety store on 47th Street (about 150 employees, described as the only Black-owned department store in the world) which doubled as the front for the policy bank, and they poured policy money into Bronzeville businesses, professionals and artists. Mack Jones died in May 1944 in a car crash caused by a drunk driver; his estate was probated in March 1948 at $444,826.77. On 11 May 1946 the Outfit (Sam Giancana directing from a second car) kidnapped Ed Jones in front of his wife; a policeman who gave chase was hit by shotgun fire. George negotiated the ransom down from $250,000 to $100,000; Ed was released on 24 May 1946, after about thirteen days (the source gives no release location) ⟨fixed: V_R14⟩, with adhesive tape over his eyes and cotton in his ears, told "cooperate or die." The Joneses left for Mexico, reportedly on a $200,000-a-year allowance from the Outfit, and their partner Teddy Roe kept the wheels.
  • The odd detail: Ed Jones met Giancana in federal prison (a tax case, late 1930s/1940 — UNVERIFIED this session) and, according to Chepesiuk, later lent him $100,000 to start a stolen-goods vending-machine racket (12,000 machines, 500 employees, about $8 million a year) — the loan that financed the men who kidnapped him.
  • People: Edward P. Jones — the "King of Kings"; George Jones — brother, negotiator; McKissack "Mack" Jones — brother, d. May 1944; Sam "Mooney" Giancana — Outfit rising man who took the wheels; Theodore "Teddy" Roe — the Joneses' lieutenant and successor.
  • Sources: http://www.crimemagazine.com/chicago-outfit-makes-its-move-0 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roe
  • Confidence: Medium-High. The kidnapping and estate figures are well attested; the tailor-shop origin and the Ben Franklin store (150 employees) come from search-result summaries of WTTW's "Policy Kings" page and the Block Club Chicago review of the 2022 "King of Kings" exhibit, which returned 403/429 when fetched — the store's opening year and the 1940 tax conviction are UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the Outfit's move comes after the 1930s window; in the decade the Joneses are at their height).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A Black-owned department store on 47th Street whose stockroom is a bank vault. If the Golden Circle or the vampires of high society want a foothold in the Black Belt, the wheel is the door — and the Joneses are the men who decide who walks through it.

Theodore "Teddy" Roe — the lieutenant who would not sell — 1929–1952

  • Where: Bronzeville; later his apartment at 5239 South Michigan Avenue (decorated at a cost of $50,000); the Boston Club, 4234 Wabash Avenue (a club he was said to share with wheel-maker Robert Wilkins).
  • What happened: Roe was born 26 August 1898 in Galliano, Louisiana, raised in Little Rock, and came to Chicago at 31 (c. 1929) to work for tailor-turned-policy-king Ed Jones. Through the 1930s he rose to be the Joneses' operating partner. When the Outfit kidnapped Ed Jones in 1946 Roe paid the $100,000 ransom, then refused to hand over his own share; offered $250,000 by Giancana to quit, he answered "I'll die first." [UNVERIFIED — V_R14] On 19 June 1951 he shot and killed Outfit man "Fat Lenny" Caifano during an attempt to kidnap him, was charged, and was acquitted after the evidence was suppressed. Already diagnosed with stomach cancer (1 August 1947), he was shotgunned outside his home at about 10 p.m. on 4 August 1952; Leonard Patrick, David Yaras and William Block were arrested for it. His funeral drew an 81-car procession, the largest for a Black Chicagoan since Jack Johnson's in 1946.
  • The odd detail: His partner Robert Wilkins ran a welding shop that manufactured the policy wheels for the Belmont and "Old Reliable" books — and was murdered in mid-September 1946, a week after an attempt on Roe.
  • People: Theodore "Teddy" Roe — policy operator, d. 1952; Robert Wilkins — wheel-maker, murdered Sept 1946; "Fat Lenny" Caifano — Outfit muscle, killed 1951; Leonard Patrick, David Yaras, William Block — arrested for Roe's murder.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roe ; http://www.crimemagazine.com/chicago-outfit-makes-its-move-0
  • Confidence: High (the two sources agree on dates and addresses).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (in the 1930s he is the rising man, not yet the martyr).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Section 13's inside man in the wheels. A man who says "they'll have to kill me" and means it is exactly the ally an investigator needs and exactly the man the Hunter Guild would try to recruit.

"Big Jim" Martin — the West Side policy king — 1920s–1950

  • Where: Martin's Corner, 1900 Lake Street (his gambling house) and his tavern at 1904 Lake Street, on the near West Side — Chicago's smaller, older Black district.
  • What happened: James Martin, born 8 March 1883 in Stanton, Tennessee, came to Chicago at 14 and made money in real estate in the 1920s before running the West Side's policy and gambling. His independence drew the same pressure the South Side kings later felt: in October 1940 a bomb of six sticks of dynamite was found at his tavern; in May 1941 three gunmen kidnapped him demanding $50,000 (they left with $300); on 15 November 1950 John Philip Cerone shot him in the arm, shoulder and neck, after which Martin fled to Mexico.
  • The odd detail: The kidnappers wanted $50,000 and settled for $300 — the West Side king kept his money somewhere else.
  • People: James "Big Jim" Martin — West Side policy king; John Philip "Jackie" Cerone — Outfit gunman, later boss.
  • Sources: http://www.crimemagazine.com/chicago-outfit-makes-its-move-0
  • Confidence: Medium (single source; the 1930s scale of Martin's operation is not given).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The West Side Black district — Lake Street under the L tracks — is a second, smaller Black Chicago most modules forget; a gambling house that survives a six-stick bomb has either luck or a ward.

Jesse Binga and the fall of the Binga State Bank — 1908–1941

  • Where: Binga State Bank and the Binga Arcade, 35th and State streets; Binga's home at 5922 South Park Avenue (a white block when he bought it).
  • What happened: Born in Detroit on 10 April 1865, Binga came to Chicago for the 1893 fair, went into real estate (1896–98), opened a private bank in 1908 and a state-chartered one in 1921, moving to new offices in 1924 and building the $325,000 Binga Arcade at 35th and State. His purchase of a house on South Park Avenue got his home and offices bombed at least five times. Heavily loaded with Black Belt real-estate loans, the bank could not survive the crash; in 1930 the Chicago Clearinghouse — to which he had paid dues for a decade [UNVERIFIED — V_R14] — refused to help, and the Illinois Auditor General closed it (Wikipedia gives June 1930; another account says state auditors shut it on 31 July 1930). Binga was indicted for embezzlement in March 1931; the first trial (1932) hung, the second (1933) convicted him, and he drew ten years. Paroled from Joliet in 1938 after a 10,000-signature petition and a plea to the parole board by his attorney, Clarence Darrow ⟨fixed: V_R14⟩, he was fully pardoned by Governor Dwight H. Green in 1941 and died 13 June 1950 at St. Luke's Hospital; buried Oak Woods Cemetery.
  • The odd detail: The richest Black man in Chicago, bombed five times for buying a house, ended the decade as a church handyman — the fall was total and public, and everyone in Bronzeville had money in that vault.
  • People: Jesse Binga — banker and real-estate man; Eudora Johnson Binga — his wife, heiress of gambler "Mushmouth" Johnson (from memory; UNVERIFIED this session); Clarence Darrow — his attorney, who pleaded for him at the parole-board hearing ⟨fixed: V_R14⟩; Dwight H. Green — governor who pardoned him, 1941.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesse_Binga
  • Confidence: High on the dated events; the two closure dates (June vs 31 July 1930) conflict between sources — the state closure order and the final shuttering may be different events.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (bank panics are in the setting's own account of the Depression).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): What was in the Binga vault at 35th and State when the auditors sealed it? Depositors' savings — and, in a module, something a customer left in a safe-deposit box in 1929 that nobody can now get at legally. The Kabbalahs contract the Church for work it forbids its own; a bank in receivership is work nobody wants.

Anthony Overton — cosmetics, the Douglass National Bank and the Chicago Bee — 1898–1946

  • Where: Overton Hygienic Building, 3619–27 South State Street; Chicago Bee Building, 3647 South State (both Chicago Landmarks, 1998).
  • What happened: Overton (born 21 March 1865, or 18 March 1864 by another source) founded the Hygienic Manufacturing Company in 1898; its "High Brown Face Powder" was the first commercial success in cosmetics for Black women. He built a conglomerate — the Overton Hygienic Company, the Douglass National Bank (1923, the second nationally chartered Black-owned bank in the United States), Victory Life Insurance, the Great Northern Realty Company, Half-Century Magazine (1916) and the Chicago Bee newspaper (1925), which had a woman managing editor, Olive M. Diggs. In 1927 he received both the NAACP's Spingarn Medal and the first Harmon gold medal for business. The Depression broke the bank and cost him control of Victory Life (from memory: the Douglass National Bank closed in 1932 — UNVERIFIED this session). He died 2 July 1946, aged 81.
  • The odd detail: The Bee is the paper whose theatre editor, James J. Gentry, coined the name "Bronzeville" in 1930 — a cosmetics fortune named the neighbourhood.
  • People: Anthony Overton — cosmetics magnate, banker, publisher; Olive M. Diggs — managing editor of the Bee; James J. Gentry — Bee theatre editor, coiner of "Bronzeville."
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Overton ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Metropolis%E2%80%93Bronzeville_District ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronzeville,_Chicago
  • Confidence: Medium-High; the bank's failure date and the loss of Victory Life are UNVERIFIED this session.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Two Black banks on State Street failed within two years; the "Old Settler" elite who ran them lost everything to the newcomers' crash. The bitterness is real and specific — a good root for a cult versus population story where the cult is a handful of ruined men.

B. The Stroll, the Regal and the nightclubs

The Regal Theater — 4 February 1928 onward

  • Where: 4719 South Parkway (now King Drive), at 47th Street, Bronzeville — the south-east corner complex shared with the Savoy Ballroom and the South Center department store.
  • What happened: The Regal opened on 4 February 1928 to long lines in cold rain [UNVERIFIED — V_R14]: a $1.5 million, roughly 3,000-seat movie-and-stage palace in the Balaban & Katz chain (designed by Levy & Klein; Edward Eichenbaum is also credited), decked in Moorish, Spanish and "Eastern" fantasy [UNVERIFIED — V_R14] with velvet seats and great pillars. It was owned by a white business association but staffed by Black employees and, with the Savoy next door, made this stretch of South Parkway "the Harlem of Chicago." [UNVERIFIED — V_R14] Through the 1930s it played Louis Armstrong, Duke Ellington, Ella Fitzgerald and the big bands alongside first-run films; the chorus line was the Regalettes (from memory — UNVERIFIED). It closed c. 1968 and was demolished in 1973; the Harold Washington Cultural Center stands there now.
  • The odd detail: A 3,000-seat Moorish palace opened before the Depression by the same chain that ran the Loop's Chicago Theatre — the Black Belt got its movie palace only some twenty months before the crash ⟨fixed: V_R14⟩.
  • People: Balaban & Katz — owners; Levy & Klein / Edward Eichenbaum — architects.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regal_Theater,_Chicago
  • Confidence: High on dates and address; architect attribution conflicting.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A 3,000-seat fantasy of the Orient with a stage door on 47th Street — the obvious venue for a hypnotist's act, a missing chorus girl, or a vampire's box seat.

The Savoy Ballroom — 23 November 1927 to 1948

  • Where: 4733 South Parkway, next to the Regal.
  • What happened: Opened on Thanksgiving Eve 1927 as the largest dance hall on the South Side, surpassing Lincoln Gardens, with a triple sub-floor, lavish decor and a checkroom for 6,000 hats and coats. From 1927 to 1940 it ran two bands a night, continuous music — Armstrong, Basie, Ellington, Hines, Holiday, Fitzgerald — and, as a hall for hire, staged boxing exhibitions, figure skating, roller skating and basketball: the house team, the Savoy Big Five, was renamed the Harlem Globetrotters. Most patrons were Black, but a growing white crowd came south to dance. It closed in 1948 and was demolished in the early 1970s.
  • The odd detail: The checkroom for 6,000 coats — a room-sized inventory of who is in the building on any given night.
  • People: Abe Saperstein — promoter of the Savoy Big Five / Globetrotters (from memory; UNVERIFIED this session).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy_Ballroom_(Chicago)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Political rallies, boxing, basketball and 6,000 dancers under one roof: a crowd that big is where a werewolf hides or a cult recruits. The band never stops.

The Grand Terrace Café — Earl Hines in Capone's club — 28 December 1928 to December 1940

  • Where: 3955 South Parkway (1928–37); moved in 1937 to 315 East 35th Street, the old Sunset Café. Telephone Douglas 3600.
  • What happened: Earl "Fatha" Hines opened at the Grand Terrace on his 25th birthday, 28 December 1928, and his orchestra (15–20 men, at times 28) was the house band for twelve years through the worst of the Depression: three shows a night, four on Saturdays. The club was controlled by Al Capone's organisation and fronted by manager-impresario Ed Fox; a 1931 guide called it "the swankiest of the Blackbelt night clubs, and one of the oldest," with floor shows of chorus girls, tap dancers and vaudeville for a wealthy, mixed-race crowd. Capone had the upright piano replaced with a white $3,000 Bechstein grand and told Hines the rule of the house: "be like the three monkeys: you hear nothing and you see nothing and you say nothing." Open microphones sent the band coast to coast many nights a week — sometimes seven — making it "the most broadcast band in America" (a Chicago boy named Nat Cole listened). Each summer Hines took the whole band south for three months, the first Black big band to do so — bomb threats and police confrontations included; a stage in Alabama was bombed. The club closed suddenly in December 1940 when its manager disappeared.
  • The odd detail: The white Bechstein, the "three monkeys" speech, and a club that ended when the manager vanished.
  • People: Earl "Fatha" Hines — bandleader (1903–83); Ed Fox — manager/impresario; Al Capone — silent owner; Nat "King" Cole — teenage listener.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_Hines ; https://therestaurantproject.wordpress.com/2012/05/31/grand-terrace-cafe/ ; https://chicagojazzsite.wordpress.com/2017/03/10/the-cafe-earl-hines-and-the-grand-terrace/
  • Confidence: High on Hines/Capone (Hines's own recollections via Wikipedia); the manager's 1940 disappearance is stated without detail — who and why is UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The radio wire from the Grand Terrace goes into every parlour in America seven nights a week — a cult could put a signal under the music. And a manager who "disappeared" in December 1940 is a cold case waiting for an investigator.

C. The press and the Migration network

Robert S. Abbott and the Chicago Defender — 5 May 1905 to 29 February 1940

  • Where: Founded in a boarding-house room; the Defender Building at 3435 South Indiana Avenue (a Chicago Landmark); Abbott's home on South Parkway.
  • What happened: Abbott started the paper on 5 May 1905 with 25 cents; his landlady's kindness in those years earned her an eight-room house later. By 1916 the Defender had 50,000 readers, 125,000 by 1918, over 200,000 in the early 1920s — the most widely circulated Black newspaper in the country, with each copy read by four or five people. Two-thirds of the readership lay outside Chicago because Pullman porters and dining-car men carried bundles south and dropped them along the line, in defiance of Southern laws and confiscations; the paper printed the lynchings the white press ignored, ran Northern job advertisements aimed squarely at Southerners, and on 15 May 1917 declared "The Great Northern Drive." Abbott created the Bud Billiken children's page in 1923 and the parade in 1929, and led the first parade in his Rolls-Royce. He died of Bright's disease on 29 February 1940 and his nephew John H. Sengstacke inherited the paper.
  • The odd detail: A distribution system run by railway porters — hidden bundles, passed hand to hand — that the South could not stop. The porters were the Defender's and therefore Black America's postal service.
  • People: Robert Sengstacke Abbott — founder-publisher; John H. Sengstacke — nephew and heir, 1940; the Pullman porters — couriers.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Sengstacke_Abbott ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chicago_Defender ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Metropolis%E2%80%93Bronzeville_District
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the real Great Northern Drive rode on the Great War's labour hunger (European immigration cut off, war plants hiring). With America out of the war, the pull north would come from the Southern War's supply industries and the empire's trade instead; the Defender's campaign and date can stand, the "why" underneath changes. The rulebook already keeps the Migration.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Defender is the intelligence service of Black America: a porter's bundle can carry a coded column, a warning, a name. Section 13 would want a friend on the city desk at 3435 South Indiana; so would the Golden Circle.

The Bud Billiken Parade — first held 11 August 1929

  • Where: Originally up Michigan Avenue from 31st Street, then east into Washington Park; later along South Parkway.
  • What happened: Abbott, planning a youth page in 1923, noticed a Billiken — the grinning good-luck charm figure — in a Chinese restaurant and named his page mascot "Bud Billiken"; some early columns were written by a boy named Willard Motley, later the novelist. David Kellum, who ran the Bud Billiken Club, suggested a parade; the first stepped off on 11 August 1929, with Abbott in his Rolls-Royce at its head and, as first guests, Freeman Gosden and Charles Correll — the two white Chicagoans who voiced Amos 'n' Andy. Between 1930 and 1934 some 10,000 children's names appeared in the Billiken pages. The parade grew into the second-largest in the country.
  • The odd detail: The mascot of Black Chicago's children is a luck-charm idol from a Chinese restaurant, and the guests of honour at the first parade were the white men behind Amos 'n' Andy.
  • People: Robert S. Abbott — founder; David Kellum — Bud Billiken Club director, parade originator; Willard Motley — early "Bud Billiken" columnist; Gosden and Correll — first parade guests.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bud_Billiken_Parade_and_Picnic
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A luck-idol at the head of a children's parade, every August, through Washington Park — the folklore layer writes itself. What does the Billiken actually grant, and what is it fed?

D. Politics — the first Black congressmen and the ward bosses

Oscar De Priest — the first Black congressman of the century — 1928–1935 (also in 09-22 archive: R01)

  • Where: Illinois 1st Congressional District (the South Side); Washington, D.C.
  • What happened: A Republican alderman indicted for graft in 1917 (he resigned from the Council, hired Clarence Darrow, and was acquitted), De Priest won the 1st District in 1928 (47.8% to Harry Baker's 40.3%) and served three terms, 1929–35 — the only Black member of Congress. In 1929 First Lady Lou Hoover's invitation of Jessie De Priest to the congressional wives' tea set off a national storm; De Priest fought the segregation of the House restaurant, insisting Howard students eat in the public room rather than the basement reserved for Black employees. In September 1930 he received an extortion letter signed "the Brooklyn Rats" demanding $10,000; investigators traced it to a Chicago political figure, Julius Link, who got six months. He lost in 1934 to Democrat Arthur Mitchell, and died 12 May 1951, buried at Graceland.
  • The odd detail: The "Brooklyn Rats" letter — a Chicago politician writing a mob-style shakedown to the only Black congressman.
  • People: Oscar Stanton De Priest — congressman 1929–35; Jessie De Priest — wife, the 1929 tea; Julius Link — extortion-letter writer; Clarence Darrow — his 1917 defence lawyer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Stanton_De_Priest

Arthur W. Mitchell — the first Black Democrat in Congress — 1934–1943

  • Where: Illinois 1st District; the Illinois Central / Rock Island line into Arkansas (the 1937 incident).
  • What happened: Born 22 December 1883 in Lafayette, Alabama, a Tuskegee man who had worked as Booker T. Washington's office assistant, Mitchell came to Chicago as a Republican, switched parties in 1932 ("ambitious and impatient with the entrenched black Republican leadership"), and beat De Priest in 1934 with 53% — De Priest himself congratulated "the first Negro Democratic congressman." He served 1935–43 as the only Black member of Congress, introduced anti-lynching bills, and in 1937, forced from a first-class Pullman into a Jim Crow car on entering Arkansas, sued; Mitchell v. United States (1941) held the railroad had violated the Interstate Commerce Act. He retired in 1942 to a 12-acre Virginia farm and died 9 May 1968.

E. Landmarks, housing and the colour line

"Bronzeville" gets its name — 1930

  • Where: The Chicago Bee offices, 3647 South State Street; the Douglas and Grand Boulevard community areas.
  • What happened: In 1930 James J. Gentry, theatre editor of Anthony Overton's Chicago Bee, coined "Bronzeville" for the district the city called the "Black Belt" (and worse), after the bronze skin of its residents; the Defender took the name up and ran an annual "Mayor of Bronzeville" contest (from memory — UNVERIFIED this session). The district's core, Douglas, counted 50,285 people in 1930 and 53,124 in 1940; the wider Black Belt ran from Cermak Road (22nd) south toward 60th, between State and the lake, and by the 1930s held most of the city's Black population — a narrow seven-mile strip. The Ida B. Wells-Barnett House stands at 3624 South King Drive; Forum Hall (1897, Samuel Atwater Treat) on 43rd Street was a meeting hall.
  • The odd detail: The name was a newspaperman's act of rebranding in the first year of the Depression — the neighbourhood renamed itself in the Bee while its banks were failing on the same block.

The Rosenwald Apartments (Michigan Boulevard Garden Apartments) — 1929–1930

  • Where: 47th Street and Michigan Avenue (4600 block of South Michigan), Bronzeville; four acres.

The eviction riot at Diana Gross's flat — 3 August 1931 (also in 09-22 archive: H04)

  • Where: 5016 South Dearborn Street (the Daily Worker's address), near 51st and Dearborn; Washington Park.

F. Churches, gospel and the Spiritual mothers

Thomas A. Dorsey writes "Take My Hand, Precious Lord" — August 1932 (also in 09-22 archive: C07)

  • Where: Pilgrim Baptist Church, 3301 South Indiana Avenue; Dorsey's home on the South Side (he was in St. Louis when the news came [UNVERIFIED — V_R14]).

Provident Hospital moves and Dr. Daniel Hale Williams dies — 1930–1931

  • Where: Provident Hospital, from 36th and Dearborn to the former Chicago Lying-In Hospital building at 426 East 51st Street (1930–33); Idlewild, Michigan (Williams's death).
  • What happened: Founded in 1891 by Dr. Daniel Hale Williams after nursing student Emma Reynolds was refused by every white school, Provident — 12 beds at first — was where Williams opened James Cornish's chest on 10 July 1893 to repair a stab wound to the pericardium, one of the first successful heart operations. The affiliation collapsed between 1939 and 1944 under Depression debt, mismanagement, and community objection that it perpetuated segregation. Williams himself, a charter member of the American College of Surgeons (1913), widowed in 1924, died of a stroke on 4 August 1931 at Idlewild, the Black resort in Michigan's northwoods, aged 75; buried Graceland.

I. The colour line — covenants, bombs and the Southern War regiment

M. The Pullman porters — a national network on rails

The Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters and the 1937 contract — 1925–1937 (also in 09-22 archive: H07)

  • Where: Chicago division of the BSCP (Milton P. Webster); the Pullman Company's headquarters and shops, Chicago; every sleeping car on every line out of Chicago.
  • What happened: Pullman was one of the largest single employers of Black Americans in the country ⟨fixed: V_R14⟩ — about 10,000 porters and 200 maids in 1926 — and every one of them was "George" to the passengers (of 12,000 porters and waiters that year only 362 were actually named George; the white "Society for the Prevention of Calling Sleeping Car Porters George" claimed 31,000 members and in 1926 got Pullman to put name racks in the cars). A porter worked 400 hours or 11,000 miles a month for a $72.50 minimum (average wage $78.11 plus $58.15 in tips, against $33.82 in expenses for meals, lodging, uniform and supplies), slept three or four hours deducted from pay, was charged for anything a passenger stole, and could never be a conductor ($150 for 240 hours, white only). Five hundred porters founded the Brotherhood in Harlem on 25 August 1925 under A. Philip Randolph — who did not work for Pullman and so could not be fired; Webster ran Chicago. Pullman answered with spies, firings, a company union, and friendly ministers and Black newspapers. A 1928 strike was called and postponed (the Chicago History Museum holds the broadside); membership fell to 658 in 1933; then the amended Railway Labor Act (1934) let the porters vote, the Brotherhood beat the company union and was certified on 1 June 1935, took an AFL charter that year — the first Black-led union to get one — and signed its first contract on 25 August 1937 (effective October): a 240-hour month, time-and-a-half after 260, about $1.25–1.5 million a year in raises, and Pullman paying for uniforms after ten years.
  • The odd detail: The museum's 1929 AFL membership certificate belongs to Archibald Motley — the porter whose son painted Bronzeville's nightlife. And in 1934 a porter averaged 73 hours a week at 27.8 cents an hour while a factory hand did 37 hours at 54.8 cents.
  • People: A. Philip Randolph — president; Milton P. Webster — Chicago division chief, first vice-president; Archibald Motley Sr. — porter, union member, father of the painter.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brotherhood_of_Sleeping_Car_Porters ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pullman_porter ; https://www.chicagohistory.org/brotherhood-sleeping-car-porters/

  • Divergence check: holds — the New Deal labour law (1934–35) exists under Roosevelt 1933–.

A porter who saw something on the Panama Limited between Chicago and New Orleans is the best witness in the game — and the one the Pullman spies report on.

Valmor Products and King Novelty — mail-order beauty and hoodoo from the South Side — 1926–1984

  • Where: Chicago's South Side (street addresses not in the source read; from memory, the 2200 block of South Indiana and later South Wabash — UNVERIFIED).
  • What happened: Rose and Morton Neumann — he a Hungarian-Jewish chemist (1898–1985) — founded Valmor Products Co. in 1926 to sell perfumes, hair pomades, incense and beauty goods to Black customers by mail and through the drugstores of the Black Belt, under sub-brands Lucky Brown, Madam Jones, Famous Products and King Novelty. Black commercial artists Charles C. Dawson and Jay Jackson drew the labels and advertisements whose glamorous couples became the company's signature (the Rolling Stones lifted them for Some Girls; the Chicago Cultural Center mounted "Love For Sale" in 2015). The Neumanns ran it until 1984 and spent the profits on one of the great modern-art collections in America (from memory). King Novelty was the hoodoo arm — the curio catalogue of lucky oils, incense, roots, lodestones and dream books sold to policy players and the Spiritual churches' "mothers" (from memory — not in the Wikipedia article; UNVERIFIED this session).
  • The odd detail: Bronzeville's luck — the oils and dream books a policy player bought before picking a gig — was manufactured by a Jewish chemist and drawn by the Defender's cartoonists.
  • People: Morton and Rose Neumann — founders; Charles C. Dawson, Jay Jackson — illustrators.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valmor_Products

Elder Lucy Smith — the faith healer of Langley Avenue — 1916–1952

  • Where: All Nations Pentecostal Church, Langley Avenue (the 1926 building; 3716 South Langley — from memory); a new church built after 1938.
  • What happened: Born Lucinda Madden on 14 January 1874 in Woodstock, Georgia, married to William Smith in 1896 with nine children, she came to Chicago in 1910, began prayer meetings with two other women in her home in 1916, founded the Langley Avenue All Nations Pentecostal Church in 1920 and finished its building in December 1926 — the first church in Chicago ever established by a woman pastor. Membership reached 3,000; her weekly healing services claimed over 200,000 healed; in the Depression the church fed thousands; All Nations was among the first Black churches to broadcast its services on the radio (from memory: "The Glorious Church of the Air," from 1933). She sold the building in 1938 and built again. When she died on 18 June 1952 more than 60,000 came to her funeral — the largest Chicago had seen to that date; she lies at Lincoln Cemetery. Her granddaughter "Little Lucy" Smith Collier, a classically trained pianist, led the music from the age of twelve.
  • The odd detail: A Georgia-born mother of nine who had earned her living taking in sewing ⟨fixed: V_R14⟩, whose radio healing service reached the whole city, and whose funeral outdrew every gangster's, mayor's and prophet's.
  • People: Elder Lucy Smith (1874–1952); "Little Lucy" Smith Collier — granddaughter, pianist.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elder_Lucy_Smith
  • Confidence: High on the dated facts; the radio programme's name, date and station are from memory.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Two hundred thousand healed is the period's reading; the folklore is the woman who could; what is actually there is a question the IHS would send a priest to Langley Avenue to answer — and he would find a soup line first. Cult versus population: 3,000 members, one healer.
