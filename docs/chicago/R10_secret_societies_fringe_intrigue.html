  • Lane: R10 — the real history under the Golden Circle: Civil War secret orders in Illinois/Indiana, 1930s hate groups and shirt movements, fringe economics movements, plots, spy cases, fraternal orders, and foreign-agent intrigue in Chicago and the Great Lakes ring.
  • Date: 2026-09-26

Overview

The Golden Circle's real history runs straight through Chicago. The Knights of the Golden Circle (1854) drew their empire as a 2,400-mile-wide circle (16° radius) centred on Havana ⟨fixed: V_R10⟩ — almost exactly the empire the setting's America has since taken by other means. Their wartime heirs, the Sons of Liberty, had a Chicago "brigadier" (Charles Walsh), a former mayor as treasurer (Buckner Morris), loaded buckshot guns stored a long rifle-shot from Camp Douglas, and a plan to free 8,000 prisoners on Election Day 1864; the English mercenary arrested at the Richmond House vanished from the Dry Tortugas in 1868, and the last rumour of him came from Havana. The 1930s successors are all here and well documented: the Black Legion's army-shaped org chart, its oath sworn "In the name of God and the Devil" at gunpoint, and its prosecutors who were members; Pelley, the medium who founded the Silver Shirts (with riots in Chicago in 1938); the Chicago-founded Teutonia (1924) that raised money for Hitler's Munich movement years before he held office — a perfect fit for the setting's occult, office-less Hitler; and a string of Chicago–Germany–Ireland intrigues (Clan na Gael's 1889 Cronin murder, the 1900 and 1916 Welland Canal plots, Oscar Pfaus carrying the Abwehr's message to the IRA). The fraternal city gives settings: the Masonic Temple (tallest in Chicago, demolished 1939), the Moorish Medinah Temple, Mooseheart's "Child City," and the Black Shriners founded at the 1893 fair who beat the white Shrine in the Supreme Court in 1929. The biggest divergence finds: with America neutral in 1917 there is no Espionage Act, no 1918 IWW mass trial and no German-American renaming panic — the IWW and the Germania Club both enter the 1930s strong — and with Austria-Hungary surviving, Chicago's Bohemian National Alliance (founded here in 1914) never dissolves: in the 1930s Chicago is the capital of a Czech independence movement, and Mayor Cermak is its friend.

Part 1 — Living memory: the Golden Circle's real roots (1854–1871)

These are pre-1916 and happened as recorded. For a 1930s Chicagoan they are grandfather-memory: the last Camp Douglas guards and Copperhead sons were old men in 1935, and the Confederate Mound at Oak Woods was a place you could walk to.

The Knights of the Golden Circle founded — the Havana map — July 4, 1854

  • Where: Cincinnati, Ohio (first "castle") — or Lexington, Kentucky (an 1860 convention record claims the order began there on the same date); the founding city is disputed.
  • What happened: George Washington Lafayette Bickley, a Virginia doctor and newspaper editor, founded the Knights of the Golden Circle on July 4, 1854. The "circle" was literal: a ring centred on Havana with a radius of 16 degrees (about 1,200 miles — roughly 2,400 miles across; Handbook of Texas: "some 2,400 miles in diameter", https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/knights-of-the-golden-circle) ⟨fixed: V_R10⟩, taking in the American South, Mexico, Central America, Cuba, Haiti, the Dominican Republic and the Caribbean — a slave empire that could have yielded some 25 new slave states. The order had three degrees: the Knights of the Columbian Star (political), the True Faith (financial) and the Knights of the Iron Hand (military). Local lodges were called "castles." During the war it spread into Kentucky, Tennessee, and the southern parts of Indiana, Ohio, Illinois and Missouri, then reorganised as the Order of American Knights (late 1863) and the Order of the Sons of Liberty (February 1864).
  • The odd detail: the organisation was drawn as a compass circle on a map with a Caribbean capital at its centre — the Golden Circle's geography is a radius, not a country.
  • People: George W. L. Bickley — founder, doctor-editor; alleged members (unproven) include John C. Breckinridge, Robert Toombs, John B. Floyd, Howell Cobb, William L. Yancey.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knights_of_the_Golden_Circle
  • Confidence: High for founding, map, degrees and successor names; membership lists are unverified (the article itself says so).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Note the setting's own empire (Cuba occupied, Mexico's north ceded, Haiti/DR held) is almost exactly the KGC's circle — America has achieved the Golden Circle's map by other means.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Golden Circle of the 1930s can read its own old map and see its prophecy fulfilled by Washington — the question for the order is who now sits at the centre of the circle in Havana.

The Golden Circle's gold — legend, and Bickley's fate — 1863 onward

  • Where: "across a dozen states" (legend); Bickley jailed by the Union.
  • What happened: Founder George Bickley gave up leadership in early 1863 to serve as a surgeon in a North Carolina regiment, was arrested by the Union as a Confederate spy in 1863, and was held until October 1865 without trial. Legend — not history — says the KGC buried millions in ill-gotten gold across a dozen states to finance a second Civil War; treasure hunters have tied the order to Jesse James (named as an alleged member) and even to California's Saddle Ridge Hoard of gold coins. The legend is modern and the evidence is nil, but it is exactly the kind of story told about the order.
  • The odd detail: the order's founder spent two years in a Union cell without ever being tried — the first Golden Circle man to simply vanish into federal custody.
  • People: George W. L. Bickley — founder; Jesse James — alleged member (legend).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knights_of_the_Golden_Circle
  • Confidence: High on Bickley's arrest; the gold is legend (Low).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened (the arrest); folklore (the gold).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): three layers of truth — the period says "crank legend," folklore says "buried gold for the second war," and underneath the caches hold something other than gold. The Secret Service's oldest file could be Bickley's untried detention. ⟨fixed: factions⟩

Order of American Knights → Order of the Sons of Liberty: the Copperhead lodges — 1863–1864

  • Where: Illinois, Indiana, Ohio; Chicago lodge commanded by Charles Walsh.
  • What happened: The KGC's northern successor, the Order of American Knights, re-formed in February 1864 as the Order of the Sons of Liberty (OSL), an oath-bound paramilitary society. Clement Vallandigham became its Supreme Commander around March 1864, while in exile. The OSL and Confederate commissioners in Canada worked toward a "Northwestern Confederacy" of Ohio, Indiana and Illinois. Chicago's OSL was estimated at "about 2,000" members by a contemporary account (another account says 4,000), under "Brigadier-General" Charles Walsh, with former mayor Buckner S. Morris as treasurer.
  • The odd detail: a secret society with military ranks — a Chicago Copperhead held the title "Brigadier-General" of an army that existed only in its oath.
  • People: Clement Vallandigham — Supreme Commander; Charles Walsh — Chicago commander; Buckner S. Morris — treasurer; Harrison H. Dodd — Indiana founder/leader.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knights_of_the_Golden_Circle ; https://chicagology.com/prefire/prefire155/ ; https://acws.co.uk/archives-history-camp_douglas_conspiracy
  • Confidence: High on names and succession; Medium on membership numbers (two sources disagree: 2,000 vs 4,000).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Golden Circle renames itself every generation (KGC → OAK → OSL → …); a 1930s lodge might still use the OSL's ranks and pass-words, and a GM can have an old OSL commission turn up in a dead man's trunk.

Copperhead badges and the Charleston Riot — 1861–March 28, 1864

  • Where: Illinois, Indiana and Ohio; the riot at Charleston, Coles County, Illinois.
  • What happened: The Peace Democrats — "Copperheads," after the snake — turned the insult into a badge: they cut the Liberty Head out of copper large cents and wore it openly, reinterpreting the copper "head" as Liberty's. Their strength lay in the lower Midwest and the southern counties of Illinois, Indiana and Ohio. On March 28, 1864, a fight between Union soldiers on furlough and Copperheads at Charleston, Illinois, ended with six soldiers and two Copperheads dead; it drew Lincoln's attention and national headlines.
  • The odd detail: the recognition token was a coin with its face cut out — anyone could carry one, and only the initiated knew it meant anything.
  • People: (none named on the page read.)
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copperhead_(politics)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Liberty-head disc cut from an 1850s cent, found on a 1930s corpse — the Golden Circle's recognition sign never changed.

The Chicago Times seized by the Army — June 3, 1863

  • Where: the Chicago Times office, downtown Chicago; protest at Court House Square.
  • What happened: Wilbur F. Storey's Chicago Times had turned Copperhead after the Emancipation Proclamation. On Maj.-Gen. Ambrose Burnside's order ("On account of the repeated expression of disloyal and incendiary sentiments, the publication of the newspaper known as the Chicago Times is hereby suppressed"), federal cavalry arrived at 3 a.m. on June 3, 1863, followed an hour later by two infantry companies from Camp Douglas; they stopped the presses and destroyed the printed papers. Mayor F. C. Sherman called a noon meeting demanding revocation, the Illinois legislature condemned the act, and about 20,000 people gathered at Court House Square that evening. Lincoln had Stanton make Burnside revoke the order, and the paper was back on June 5.
  • The odd detail: the soldiers who shut the newspaper came from the same prison camp the Copperheads would later plot to break open.
  • People: Wilbur F. Storey — Copperhead editor; Ambrose Burnside — general; F. C. Sherman — mayor.
  • Sources: https://civilwarmonths.com/2023/06/03/the-chicago-times-suppression/
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Section 13 file on "press suppression precedents" could cite this night; a Golden Circle-friendly newspaper dynasty in 1930s Chicago might trace its founding grudge to it.

Clement Vallandigham — the exile in Windsor who led the Sons of Liberty — 1863–1871

  • Where: Dayton and Mount Vernon, Ohio; exile at a hotel in Windsor, Ontario (across the river from Detroit); Chicago (Aug 1864); death at Lebanon, Ohio.
  • What happened: Arrested May 5, 1863 under Burnside's General Order No. 38 after a May 1 speech at Mount Vernon, Vallandigham was tried by military court (May 6–7) and deported through Confederate lines on Lincoln's order (May 19, 1863). He went to Canada via Bermuda, settled in Windsor, won the Ohio Democratic nomination for governor in absentia 411–11 and ran his campaign from a Windsor hotel, losing 288,374 to 187,492. Around March 1864 he led the Sons of Liberty; he slipped back into Ohio "under heavy disguise" on June 14, 1864, and wrote the peace plank at the Chicago convention. On June 17, 1871, defending an accused murderer in Lebanon, Ohio, he demonstrated how the victim could have shot himself — with a pistol he thought was unloaded. It fired into his abdomen; he died the next day. His client was acquitted.
  • The odd detail: the Copperhead chief killed himself proving a man could kill himself by accident — and won the case.
  • People: Clement L. Vallandigham — Ohio congressman, Copperhead leader; Thomas McGehean — the acquitted client.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clement_Vallandigham
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Windsor as the Golden Circle's traditional safe shore — a 1930s cell runs its business from the same Ontario riverfront (cross-ref R07 for Windsor rum-running).

The 1864 Democratic convention and the Confederate "visitors" — August 25–31, 1864

  • Where: the Amphitheatre, Chicago (convention); agents lodged in the city.
  • What happened: The Democratic National Convention met in Chicago August 29–31, 1864, nominating George B. McClellan and George H. Pendleton on a platform Vallandigham wrote declaring the war a "failure"; Vallandigham was booed by War Democrats on the first day. Under cover of the crowds, Confederate Capt. Thomas H. Hines led about sixty men down from Toronto on August 25, expecting "50,000 Copperheads" in town to help free the prisoners at Camp Douglas. The rising never came (only 25 volunteers reported for the planned assault, against a target of 100), and Hines's men had to flee Chicago on August 30.
  • The odd detail: a Confederate raiding party checked into Chicago as convention tourists.
  • People: Thomas H. Hines — Confederate agent (Morgan's raider); Horatio Seymour — convention chairman; George B. McClellan — nominee.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1864_Democratic_National_Convention ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Conspiracy ; https://acws.co.uk/archives-history-camp_douglas_conspiracy
  • Confidence: High on dates; Medium on the "25 volunteers" figure (single source).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Chicago conventions as cover for the Golden Circle — the 1932 Democratic convention (Chicago Stadium, FDR nominated) could carry an echo of 1864 in its hotels.

Camp Douglas — "Eighty Acres of Hell" on the South Side — 1861–1865

  • Where: from Cottage Grove Avenue west four blocks to present Martin Luther King Drive, between E. 31st Street and E. 33rd Place (then College Place), Douglas/Bronzeville.
  • What happened: Built in 1861 as a training camp, it became a prisoner-of-war camp in February 1862 and held prisoners until May 1865. About 26,060 Confederate prisoners passed through; the official death toll was 4,454 (estimates 5,000–6,000; the United Confederate Veterans estimated about 6,000 in 1892), a 17 percent death rate. About 4,275 dead were later reinterred at "Confederate Mound" in Oak Woods Cemetery.
  • The odd detail: the Oxford English Dictionary's apparent first recorded use of "to hell in a hand basket" is an 1865 account of the Camp Douglas conspiracy.
  • People: Benjamin J. Sweet — commandant.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_Douglas_(Chicago)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. By the 1930s the site is residential Bronzeville (cross-ref R09 gazetteer).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): four thousand dead under a South Side mound, with a Golden Circle that swore to free them and failed — a ghost-and-oath hook at Oak Woods.

The Camp Douglas Conspiracy — the Election Eve arrests — November 6–7, 1864

  • Where: the Richmond House hotel; Dr. E. W. Edwards's house at 70 Adams Street; Charles Walsh's house and barn about "30 rods" (~165 yards) from Camp Douglas; Buckner Morris's house at 6 Washington Street (1864 numbering).
  • What happened: The plan was to strike on Election Day, November 8, 1864: free the roughly 8,000 prisoners, sack the city, then move on the prisoners at Rock Island, Springfield and Alton. An informant told Col. Benjamin Sweet, who had only about 900 guards, and after midnight on November 6–7 his men made simultaneous arrests. At the Richmond House they took the English soldier of fortune George St. Leger Grenfell and J. T. Shanks; at 70 Adams Street, Col. Vincent Marmaduke; at Walsh's, Walsh himself, Capt. Cantrill of Morgan's command and Charles Traverse. Walsh's house was searched without a warrant [UNVERIFIED — V_R10] and yielded shotguns "all loaded with cartridges, composed of from nine to twelve largest-sized buckshot, and capped", plus 10-inch Joslyn revolvers, also loaded. 106 men were arrested; over half were released, and the rest went before a military commission in Cincinnati.
  • The odd detail: the loaded, capped shotguns were stored in a house within a long rifle-shot of the camp fence — the arsenal was next door to its target.
  • People: Benjamin J. Sweet — commandant who broke the plot; Charles Walsh — OSL "brigadier"; Vincent Marmaduke — arrested, acquitted; J. T. Shanks ("the Texan") — prisoner-turned-informant, later a Federal captain; Thomas Hines — escaped to Canada.
  • Sources: https://chicagology.com/prefire/prefire155/ ; https://acws.co.uk/archives-history-camp_douglas_conspiracy ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_Douglas_(Chicago)
  • Confidence: High on arrests, date, and arms; historians still debate how real the plot was versus Sweet's exaggeration (Wikipedia notes "real or a hoax" debate).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Secret Service's 1865 founding (Col. William P. Wood) lands months after this plot — the Secret Service could date its Golden Circle file to November 1864, and the unresolved "was it a hoax?" gives the three-layers-of-truth structure for free. ⟨fixed: factions⟩

Confederate Mound, Oak Woods Cemetery — the mass grave on the South Side — reinterred after 1865

  • Where: Oak Woods Cemetery, 1035 E. 67th Street area (Greater Grand Crossing / Woodlawn edge), South Side.
  • What happened: The Camp Douglas dead were moved from the camp cemetery to Oak Woods and buried in concentric trenches — "reputedly the largest documented mass grave in the Western Hemisphere." A monument and marker that former Kentucky lieutenant governor John C. Underwood helped build "probably inflates" the number buried as 6,000; the plaques list more than 4,000 names. The same cemetery holds Big Jim Colosimo (1920) and, later, Mayor William Hale Thompson (1944).
  • The odd detail: the dead lie in rings — concentric trenches around a single monument — a literal circle of the Confederate dead in Chicago.
  • People: John C. Underwood — ex-lieutenant governor of Kentucky, monument promoter.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oak_Woods_Cemetery ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_Douglas_(Chicago)
  • Confidence: High on mass grave and names; monument dedicated May 30, 1895, with President Cleveland and his cabinet attending (U.S. Dept of Veterans Affairs, https://www.cem.va.gov/cems/lots/confederate_mound.asp) ⟨fixed: V_R10⟩; street address is approximate.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a ring of four thousand dead under a Golden Circle promise — the most literal "golden circle" in Chicago is a Confederate grave; a summoner would not miss it.

Buckner Stith Morris — the former mayor arrested for treason — 1864–1865

  • Where: Chicago (house at 6 Washington Street in 1864 numbering).
  • What happened: Kentucky-born Buckner Morris was Chicago's second mayor (1838–39, Whig), later an alderman, a Lake County circuit judge, and the Know Nothing candidate for governor in 1856. In 1864 he was treasurer of the Sons of Liberty; he was arrested in the Camp Douglas sweep and held nine months before a military court acquitted him. Foreclosures during his imprisonment ruined him. He died in Chicago on December 16, 1879, and his descendants refused to give his papers to the Chicago Historical Society because of his wartime treatment.
  • The odd detail: a former mayor of Chicago was the Golden Circle's paymaster — and his family hid his papers from the city ever after.
  • People: Buckner S. Morris — ex-mayor, OSL treasurer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckner_Stith_Morris ; https://chicagology.com/prefire/prefire155/
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "the Morris papers" as a MacGuffin — a family archive never given to any public collection, supposedly holding the Chicago OSL's ledger.

George St. Leger Grenfell — the prisoner who rowed into a storm — 1864–1868

  • Where: arrested at the Richmond House, Chicago; imprisoned at Fort Jefferson, Dry Tortugas (about 70 miles west of Key West).
  • What happened: Grenfell, an English soldier of fortune who claimed to have fought in Algeria ⟨fixed: V_R10⟩, Morocco, under Garibaldi in South America, in the Crimea and in the Indian Mutiny, rode with Morgan's Confederate cavalry and then joined the Chicago plot. Sentenced to hang by the Cincinnati commission, he had the sentence commuted to life by President Andrew Johnson through the efforts of the British Minister in Washington (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_St._Leger_Grenfell) ⟨fixed: V_R10⟩. At Fort Jefferson he was nursed through the September 1867 yellow-fever epidemic by fellow prisoner Dr. Samuel Mudd (the Lincoln-assassination conspirator). In March 1868 (Mudd's letter says the 6th; other sources the 7th) he and others escaped in a small open boat into a storm and were never seen again. In June 1868 the New York Times printed a report that a letter from him had arrived from Havana; historians generally treat it as fabrication.
  • The odd detail: the last rumour of the Chicago conspirator puts him in Havana — the centre point of the Golden Circle's map.
  • People: George St. Leger Grenfell — English mercenary; Samuel Mudd — his fellow prisoner and nurse.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_St._Leger_Grenfell
  • Confidence: High on escape and Havana report; the report itself is doubted.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Grenfell reached Havana and lived; the Golden Circle's 1930s Havana centre could claim a founding elder who "rowed out of the grave." Cuba is occupied in the setting, so Havana is American soil.

The Philo Parsons and the plot to take the USS Michigan — September 19, 1864

  • Where: Lake Erie — boarded at Malden (Amherstburg), Upper Canada; seized off Kelleys Island, near Sandusky; target Johnson's Island prison camp, Sandusky Bay, Ohio.
  • What happened: Confederate Acting Master John Yates Beall and his men boarded the Detroit–Sandusky steamer Philo Parsons as passengers on September 19, 1864, seized her, and also took the steamer Island Queen. The plan was to capture the USS Michigan, the only U.S. warship on the Great Lakes, and free the Confederate officers held on Johnson's Island. Their inside man, Capt. Charles H. Cole, had posed in Sandusky as secretary of the "Mount Hope Oil Company of Titusville, Pennsylvania", befriended the Michigan's officers, and meant to drug their wine at dinner. A tip reached the Union; an officer boarded the Michigan shortly before midnight and told Cole "Captain Cole, you are my prisoner." Beall's crew refused to attack without the signal, and Beall ran for Canada and scuttled the Parsons off Sandwich, Ontario.
  • The odd detail: the plan to seize a warship hinged on a fake oil-company secretary and a dinner party with drugged wine.
  • People: John Yates Beall — Confederate raider; Charles H. Cole — agent (pardoned, later a Texas rancher); Jacob Thompson — chief Confederate agent in Canada.
  • Sources: https://www.ohiocivilwarcentral.com/charles-h-cole/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Yates_Beall
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Lake Erie ferry routes and the Canadian shore as the Golden Circle's escape hatch — reused by 1930s rum-runners (cross-ref R07).

John Yates Beall hanged on Governors Island — February 24, 1865

  • Where: arrested near Niagara (Suspension Bridge), New York; hanged at Fort Columbus, Governors Island, New York.
  • What happened: On December 16, 1864, Beall and George S. Anderson were arrested near Niagara while planning to derail a passenger train to free captured Confederate officers. Anderson testified against him. A military commission convicted him in January 1865. Six senators and 91 congressmen appealed for clemency; Lincoln refused, citing the danger to Northern civilians. Beall was hanged February 24, 1865, calling his execution "absolute murder."
  • The odd detail: a Virginia planter-raider tried to wreck a passenger train at Niagara Falls to spring prisoners.
  • People: John Yates Beall — raider; George S. Anderson — companion who turned state's evidence.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Yates_Beall
  • Confidence: High. (A popular legend links Beall's hanging to John Wilkes Booth's motive; UNVERIFIED — not in the source read.)
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Buffalo/Niagara as the eastern door of the Golden Circle's Great Lakes network (cross-ref R07).

Harrison H. Dodd's "Sunday school books" — Indianapolis — August 20, 1864

  • Where: Dodd's printing office, Indianapolis, Indiana; later Fond du Lac, Wisconsin.
  • What happened: Governor Oliver P. Morton learned from an informant that arms were being shipped from New York to Harrison H. Dodd, Indiana leader of the Sons of Liberty, who had pushed for an August rising to free the prisoners at Camp Morton. On August 20, 1864, troops raided his office and found 400 revolvers and thousands of rounds (one source: 135,000 [UNVERIFIED — V_R10]) in boxes labelled "Sunday school books" (another source says "Stationary" [UNVERIFIED — V_R10]). Dodd was tried by military commission, escaped jail and fled to Canada, and was sentenced to death in absentia (commuted to life, May 31, 1865). He later returned and settled in Fond du Lac, Wisconsin, where he served several terms as mayor — as a Republican — and died June 2, 1906.
  • The odd detail: the Copperhead arsenal was shipped as Sunday-school books; its owner ended up a Republican mayor in Wisconsin.
  • People: Harrison H. Dodd — OSL founder; Oliver P. Morton — Indiana governor; William A. Bowles — associate arrested.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harrison_H._Dodd
  • Confidence: High on raid and later career; Medium on box label (sources differ: "Sunday school books" vs "Stationary").
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Golden Circle's habit of hiding guns in church crates — a 1930s shipment of "hymnals" at a Midwest rail yard is a classic lead.

The Indianapolis treason trials and Ex parte Milligan — October 1864 – April 1866

  • Where: Indianapolis (military commission); Huntington, Indiana (Milligan's home); U.S. Supreme Court.
  • What happened: Lambdin P. Milligan was arrested at home on October 6, 1864; a military commission sat from October 21. On December 10, 1864, Milligan, William A. Bowles and Stephen Horsey were convicted and sentenced to hang on May 19, 1865; President Johnson commuted the sentences on May 30, 1865. On April 3, 1866, the Supreme Court ruled in Ex parte Milligan that military tribunals could not try civilians where civil courts were open; Milligan was freed April 12, 1866. He later sued and won — damages capped by statute at five dollars plus costs (May 30, 1871).
  • The odd detail: a man sentenced to hang for treason won his lawsuit against his jailers and was awarded five dollars.

A block away, Bughouse Square's soapboxers — "revolutionary left" plus "poets, religionists, and cranks" — held forth on warm evenings from the 1910s to the 1960s, even naming their own honorary "king."
  • The odd detail: the club's own sign promised to lower your mind; it was closed not for politics but for being a dance hall too near a church.
  • People: Jack Jones — founder; Ben Reitman — "King of the Hobos," anarchist doctor; Jim Larkin — Irish labour leader.

  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar.

Part 6 — Fringe movements of money and power

Technocracy — the grey shirts and the monad — 1932–1940

  • Where: born at Columbia University, New York; spread nationwide and into Canada.
  • What happened: Howard Scott's Technical Alliance (1919–21) resurfaced in the Columbia "Energy Survey"; the "Technocracy craze" ran from mid-1932 (New York Times coverage from June 16, 1932) to January 1933, broken by Scott's rambling radio address from the Hotel Pierre on January 13, 1933 ⟨fixed: V_R10⟩. Technocracy Inc. was founded that January with M. King Hubbert. It proposed replacing money with "energy certificates," a four-day, four-hour work week, and a continental "Technate" from Panama to the North Pole divided into numbered grid squares. Members wore grey double-breasted suits and grey shirts with a blue tie and the red-and-grey monad badge; their cars were painted grey. On October 7, 1940, the RCMP arrested members in Canada and banned the group (until 1943); one arrestee was Regina chiropractor Joshua N. Haldeman.
  • The odd detail: a movement that proposed abolishing money in favour of energy units, wore grey uniforms and drove grey cars in convoy.
  • People: Howard Scott — founder (credentials disputed); M. King Hubbert — co-founder, later of "peak oil."
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technocracy_movement
  • Confidence: High; Chicago/Midwest sections not documented on the page read.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — "Panama to the North Pole" sits oddly in a setting where America already rules Mexico's north, Cuba and the Canal Zone; Technocrats would draw their Technate map over America's actual empire.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): grey cars in convoy on Midwest highways — a grid-square map where each numbered square has a reason; ideal red herring for a Golden Circle map hunt.

Townsend clubs and Share Our Wealth — the pension and the fortune cap — 1933–1936

  • Where: Long Beach, California (Townsend); Louisiana (Long); clubs nationwide including the Midwest.
  • What happened: Dr. Francis Townsend's September 1933 letter to the Long Beach Press-Telegram became Old Age Revolving Pensions, Ltd. (January 1934, with his brother Walter and realtor Robert E. Clements): $200 a month to every retiree over 60, to be spent within 30 days. By early 1936 there were about 2 million members in some 8,000 clubs; a congressional probe found Clements had personally taken over $70,000, and the McGroarty bill failed 56–206. Huey Long's Share Our Wealth (February 1934) claimed 7.5 million members in 27,000 clubs by 1935, promising a $5,000 "Household Estate" and capping fortunes; Long's office answered about 60,000 letters a week with 48 stenographers. After Long was shot in Baton Rouge on September 8, 1935 (d. two days later), Gerald L. K. Smith took over and folded it into the 1936 Union Party.
  • The odd detail: a pension movement was also a business — its co-founder pocketed $70,000 from the dues of the elderly.
  • People: Francis Townsend — physician; Robert E. Clements — co-founder; Huey Long — senator (already in the setting); Gerald L. K. Smith — organiser.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Townsend_Plan ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Share_Our_Wealth
  • Confidence: High on national figures; Midwest club numbers not found.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Long already in the setting).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Townsend club in a Chicago church basement is the most ordinary gathering in America — the perfect "population" around a cult that has learned to use its mailing list.

Coughlin's National Union for Social Justice and the Union Party — 1934–1936 (cross-ref R05)

  • Where: Shrine of the Little Flower, 2100 Twelve Mile Road, Royal Oak, Michigan; national.
  • What happened: Father Charles Coughlin founded the National Union for Social Justice on November 11, 1934 (Armistice Day), claiming 7.5 million members at its height; its paper Social Justice launched in 1936. In 1936 Coughlin, Townsend and Gerald L. K. Smith combined behind the Union Party, nominating North Dakota Congressman William Lemke with Boston labour lawyer Thomas C. O'Brien; they got 892,378 votes (1.95 percent). Jacob Coxey — leader of 1894's "Coxey's Army" — ran for Congress in Ohio on the Union ticket and got 2,384 votes. The NUSJ dissolved in 1937 and was largely replaced by the anti-Semitic Christian Front.
  • The odd detail: the NUSJ was founded on Armistice Day — a holiday that in Umbra marks a German victory.
  • People: Charles E. Coughlin — radio priest; William Lemke — candidate; Jacob S. Coxey — old-time marcher.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Union_for_Social_Justice ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Party_(United_States)
  • Confidence: High; the 7.5 million membership is the organisation's claim.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — November 11 is the Armistice; in Umbra America was never in the war and the armistice was Germany's; Coughlin's date would mean something different. Coughlin's anti-Communism would need a new target.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): IHS's Detroit problem — a priest with a radio tower and millions of listeners whom the Church can't easily silence; the Golden Circle courting his mailing list.

Mexican "repatriation" from Gary, East Chicago and Detroit — 1930–1933

  • Where: Gary and East Chicago/Indiana Harbor, Indiana; Detroit, Michigan; Chicago.
  • What happened: Between 1929 and 1939 about 82,000 people were formally deported to Mexico and an estimated 500,000–1,000,000 "repatriated," peaking in 1931 (138,000 departures by one estimate). The Midwest held only about 3 percent of the U.S. Mexican population but perhaps 10 percent of repatriates. In Gary, 1931 departures were largely voluntary, on reduced-rate train tickets; by 1932 local governments and relief agencies used "repressive measures." In Detroit, by 1932, one Mexican national told the consul that police had "dragged" him to the train station against his will after he had proved his residency the year before ⟨fixed: V_R10⟩.
  • The odd detail: the steel towns of the south shore of Lake Michigan emptied their Mexican neighbourhoods by train, at a discount.
  • People: (no individuals named on the page read.)
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Repatriation
  • Confidence: High on national figures and Gary pattern; local counts not found.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in Umbra, America annexed Mexico's mineral north after the Southern War (1917) and occupies more. Many "Mexicans" in Gary would be American nationals from the annexed provinces — deporting them would mean shipping citizens-by-conquest to an occupied country. The drive would look like a colonial "resettlement," with far uglier legal edges.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a repatriation train out of Gary in 1932 carries someone the Golden Circle wants out of the country — the Golden Circle's old map included Mexico, and now it's half-American.

Part 7 — Nations in exile: Chicago's ethnic lodges under the 1916 break

Chicago in the 1930s was a city of diaspora organisations whose home countries the 1916 break reshapes. These entries record the real organisations and flag what a German victory changes.

The Germania Club — the German singers' palace on the North Side — 1865/1889 onward

  • Where: 108 W. Germania Place (at Clark Street), Near North Side / Old Town.
  • What happened: The Germania Club began in 1865 as the Germania Männerchor, formed to sing at Abraham Lincoln's funeral. Its five-storey clubhouse, designed by August Fiedler in a neoclassical/German Renaissance style with a two-storey limestone base, arched portico, grand ballroom, banquet room and bar, opened in 1889. It was the social summit of what was once Chicago's largest ethnic community. In the mid-1930s the Friends of New Germany's Chicago leader Oscar Pfaus, Walter Kappe and Heinz Spanknöbel were members.
  • The odd detail: the club that later sheltered pro-German agitators was founded to mourn Lincoln.
  • People: August Fiedler — architect; Oscar C. Pfaus, Walter Kappe — 1930s members.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germania_Club_Building ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_C._Pfaus
  • Confidence: High. (A wartime renaming — often said to be "the Lincoln Club" in 1918 — is UNVERIFIED on the page read.)
  • Divergence check: BENDS — with America neutral in the war, German-American clubs were never pressured to rename or hide in 1917–18; in Umbra the Germania Club is a proud victor's club in the 1930s, likely hosting the German consul's receptions.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Germania ballroom as the setting's high-society battleground — German consular money, Golden Circle guests, and a vampire who has been a member since 1889.

Fritz Kuhn — Iron Cross, Mexico, and a Ford plant in Detroit — 1896–1939

  • Where: Munich; Mexico (1920s); Detroit (Ford factory, from 1928); Buffalo (Bund founding, 1936).
  • What happened: Born in Munich May 15, 1896, Kuhn won the Iron Cross as a German infantry lieutenant in the Great War, moved to Mexico in the 1920s, and came to the U.S. in 1928, working at a Ford factory in Detroit; he was naturalised in 1934. He took control of the Bund at Buffalo in 1936, led 50 followers to the Berlin Olympics, and claimed afterward that Hitler had recognised him as "American Führer" (the meeting had in fact disappointed him). Convicted December 5, 1939 of embezzling $14,548 from the Bund — part of it spent on a mistress — he went to Sing Sing the next day.
  • The odd detail: America's would-be Führer learned the country on Ford's Detroit assembly line after a stint in Mexico.
  • People: Fritz Julius Kuhn — Bundesführer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Julius_Kuhn
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in Umbra Kuhn's Iron Cross comes from a winning war, and "Mexico in the 1920s" means an American-occupied country; a victorious German veteran drifting through U.S.-held Mexico to Detroit is a natural agent profile.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a decorated German veteran on a Ford line in Dearborn, reporting to a consul — and embezzling from both.

The Bohemian National Alliance and Mayor Cermak's Chicago (also in 09-22 archive: R05, Cermak memorial) — 1914–1933

  • Where: Chicago (founded here; Czech neighbourhoods of Pilsen and "Czech California"/Lawndale); Masaryk's welcome in the Chicago streets, May 5, 1918.
  • What happened: The Bohemian National Alliance was founded in Chicago on September 6, 1914, to rally Czech-Americans for independence from Austria-Hungary; it later became the Czech National Alliance [UNVERIFIED — V_R10], published The Bohemian Review from 1917, and dissolved in 1918 once Czechoslovakia was independent. Tomáš Masaryk, who had lectured at the University of Chicago in 1902 and 1907 through his friend, Chicago industrialist Charles R. Crane, was welcomed by more than 150,000 Chicagoans on May 5, 1918; on October 18, 1918, he gave Wilson the Washington Declaration of independence. Anton Cermak, born in Kladno, Bohemia, on May 9, 1873, beat William Hale Thompson for mayor with 58 percent on April 6, 1931 ("it's true I didn't come over on the Mayflower, but I came over as soon as I could"), and was shot at Miami's Bayfront Park on February 15, 1933 by Giuseppe Zangara, dying March 6.
  • The odd detail: Chicago was the capital of a country in exile — 150,000 people in the streets for a professor who did not yet have a state.
  • People: Tomáš G. Masaryk — Czech leader; Charles R. Crane — Chicago patron; Anton Cermak — mayor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemian_National_Alliance ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom%C3%A1%C5%A1_Garrigue_Masaryk ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Cermak
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS strongly — in Umbra Austria-Hungary survives as Germany's junior partner and Wilson was gone in 1916, so there is no 1918 Washington Declaration and no Czechoslovakia. The Alliance never dissolves: in the 1930s it is a living independence movement headquartered in Chicago, and Mayor Cermak is its most powerful friend — which makes his 1933 shooting a political question.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Chicago's Czech exiles as another revolution looking for a patron — but their enemy is Berlin's ally Vienna, so the Golden Circle and the German consul would both want them watched; who really sent Zangara?

The Polish National Alliance — the fraternal nation of Chicago's Polish Triangle — 1880 onward

  • Where: headquarters in Chicago's "Polish Triangle" (Milwaukee, Ashland and Division, West Town) until the 1970s.
  • What happened: Founded in Philadelphia on February 15, 1880, by Agaton Giller's circle, the Polish National Alliance became the largest Polish fraternal organisation in America, headquartered in Chicago. It ran fraternal insurance from 1886, organised Chicago's first Polish Constitution Day parade in 1891, built the Kościuszko monument in Washington (1910), owned Alliance College (1912–91), and published Zgoda (from 1881) and the daily Dziennik Związkowy (from 1908). It stood for Polish nationalism and often clashed with the parish-centred Polish Roman Catholic Union.
  • The odd detail: Chicago's largest Polish organisation was an insurance company that was also a national movement — and it quarrelled with the Church-based union across town.
  • People: Agaton Giller — founder; Juliusz Andrzejkowicz — first president.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_National_Alliance
  • Confidence: High on history; 1930s membership figures not found.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in Umbra Germany won; a Polish state, if it exists, would be the German-sponsored Regency Kingdom proclaimed in 1916 — a client of Berlin. Chicago's Polish nationalists would be split between accepting the Kaiser's Poland and fighting it.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the PNA–Catholic Union quarrel as an IHS problem — the Church's Polish men and the nationalists' Polish men don't trust each other, and a German-sponsored Polish king wants both.

The IWW — founded in Chicago; in Umbra, never broken by the war — 1905 onward

  • Where: Chicago (founding convention June 27, 1905; General Office in Chicago through the 1930s).
  • What happened: The Industrial Workers of the World were founded in Chicago on June 27, 1905, "the Continental Congress of the working class," in Big Bill Haywood's words. In the real record, on September 5, 1917, Justice Department agents raided IWW halls nationwide, carting "five tons of material" from the Chicago General Office; 166 leaders were indicted and 101 tried together in Chicago under the Espionage Act before Judge Kenesaw Mountain Landis in 1918. Haywood fled to Soviet Russia in 1921.
  • The odd detail: 101 men tried together in one Chicago courtroom, before the judge who would become baseball's first commissioner.
  • People: William "Big Bill" Haywood — IWW leader; Kenesaw Mountain Landis — judge; George Vanderveer — defence counsel.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Workers_of_the_World
  • Confidence: High on 1905, the raids and the trial.
  • Divergence check: BENDS strongly — the 1917 raids and 1918 trial happened because America entered the war and passed the Espionage Act. In Umbra America stayed out, so there is no Espionage Act prosecution, no 1918 mass trial, and no Soviet refuge for Haywood — the IWW enters the 1930s far stronger, still run from Chicago.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Chicago where the Wobblies were never smashed: the IWW General Office as a real power in the Depression, courted by Berlin and hunted by the Golden Circle's Legion wing.

Part 8 — Esoteric orders and real analogues of the setting's factions

The Theosophical Society's American seat at Wheaton — 1926 onward

  • Where: Olcott (the estate was named in 1932), Wheaton, DuPage County, Illinois — about 25 miles west of the Loop.
  • What happened: The Theosophical Society in America bought about 10 acres of farmland at Wheaton in 1925. On August 29, 1926, Annie Besant laid the cornerstone before "a huge crowd," with Jiddu Krishnamurti — the young man the Theosophists had proclaimed as a coming World Teacher — in attendance. The Collegiate Gothic L. W. Rogers Building opened in September 1927 with offices, library, auditorium and meditation rooms. The estate was named Olcott in 1932 after co-founder Henry Steel Olcott. L. W. Rogers led the society until 1931; Sidney A. Cook from 1931 to 1945.
  • The odd detail: the "World Teacher" stood at the Wheaton cornerstone in 1926 — and three years later (1929) renounced the role and dissolved the order built around him (the Order of the Star, dissolved at Ommen on August 3, 1929 — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiddu_Krishnamurti) ⟨fixed: V_R10⟩.
  • People: Annie Besant — Theosophical president; Jiddu Krishnamurti — proclaimed World Teacher; L. W. Rogers, Sidney A. Cook — national presidents.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophical_Society_in_America

The Boone and Crockett Club — Theodore Roosevelt's hunters' society — December 21, 1887

  • Where: founded at a dinner at Roosevelt's residence in New York City (not a Chicago institution; included as the real analogue of the setting's Hunter Guild).
  • What happened: Theodore Roosevelt and George Bird Grinnell founded the Boone and Crockett Club on December 21, 1887, capped at 100 regular members, to promote hunting skill, exploration and the preservation of big game. Early officers were Roosevelt (president) and Archibald Rogers (secretary); members included the painter Albert Bierstadt, Gen. William T. Sherman, Gifford Pinchot, the scout Frederick Russell Burnham, Aldo Leopold — and the eugenicist Madison Grant. The club was central to ending market hunting and to founding the national park, forest and refuge systems.
  • The odd detail: a closed club of a hundred men who had to prove themselves as hunters shaped federal land policy — and one of its members wrote America's most influential racial-science book.
  • People: Theodore Roosevelt — founder; George Bird Grinnell — co-founder; Madison Grant — member, eugenicist.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boone_and_Crockett_Club
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Hunter Guild (founded by TR after the Amazon expedition) could grow out of the Boone and Crockett's hundred-man roll — no one is clean: the Guild's "compromise" could start with Madison Grant's wing.
