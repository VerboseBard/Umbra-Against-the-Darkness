W15 The House of Tomorrow has an airplane hangar—and overheats

1933–34 fair exhibit | documented | Experimental homes and technological failures

George Fred Keck’s House of Tomorrow presented fairgoers with a steel-framed, glass-walled future that included space for an airplane. Its climate-control story was less triumphant: the National Park Service explains that solar heat gain helped in winter but overwhelmed the new air-conditioning system in summer. After the fair, Robert Bartlett relocated the house to Beverly Shores; its adaptation involved replacing fixed glass with opening windows for ventilation. The building therefore supplies both a glamorous future-house setting and a documented technical shortcoming. Its surviving Indiana location is a useful reference, but the alterations need separating from the original exhibit.

People

George Fred Keck, Robert Bartlett

Places

  • House of Tomorrow, original fair site — Century of Progress Home and Industrial Arts area. Original Chicago site; footprint requires fair map Modern map locator
  • House of Tomorrow, surviving moved building — 214 Lake Front Drive, Beverly Shores, Indiana. Later relocated building; not its 1933 address. Number 214 follows the National Register nomination; HABS IN-243 instead prints 241. Modern map locator

Uncertainties

The exact overheating dates and operating conditions are not supplied by the overview. The National Register nomination and HABS photographs place some operable-window changes before the move. The two federal records disagree on the later street number (214 versus 241); resolve before navigation. Modern restoration and relocated details do not automatically represent the Chicago fair installation.

Fictional Umbra possibility

Fictional campaign idea: visitors report an impossible cold room in a house whose real air-conditioning system cannot keep up with the summer heat.
