M04 — Haiti and the Dominican Republic under American occupation, 1915–1940

  • Lane: M04 — Haiti (the July 1915 landing, the occupation to 1934, the corvée, the cacos and Charlemagne Péralte, Marchaterre 1929, the 1921–22 Senate inquiry, Vodou as a living religion, the American "zombie" craze and its sources, the Citadelle and Sans-Souci, the Marines' memoirs) and the Dominican Republic (the 1916–24 occupation, the gavilleros, Trujillo's rise through the US-trained Guardia, the 1930 San Zenón hurricane, the October 1937 Parsley Massacre, Dominican folklore).
  • Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent) · Brief: BRIEF.md in this folder + the Chicago sweep's BRIEF.md (entry format, sourcing rules).
  • Setting sources read first: ALTERNATE_HISTORY.md §0, §8 (esp. §8.3 "What America already held in the Caribbean, 1916–17"; §8.5 Vodou/Santería/Obeah inside the sphere; §8.6 still open), §9, §29.3–29.4 (receivership "held by the ledger"; "three layers of truth"; "never 'voodoo jungle magic'"); RESOLVED_ANSWERS.md Audit 151 (Roosevelt's retreat from empire does not happen — no withdrawal from Haiti); PEOPLE_OF_THE_TIMELINE.md (Smedley Butler, LEAD).

How to read the divergence lines. Haiti (landed 28 July 1915) and the Dominican Republic (landed May 1916) are both occupied before the 1916 break, so the landings "happened". After 1916 the occupations run as recorded except that they never end: the DR's 1924 withdrawal and Haiti's August 1934 withdrawal do not happen (Audit 151 already rules out the Haitian withdrawal). Entries say holds — occupation-era when a real 1916–34 event fits the setting unchanged, and BENDS when it depends on sovereignty (the DR 1924 elections, Trujillo's 1930 seizure of the presidency, Haitian presidents after 1934, the 1937 massacre as a border between two sovereign states).

Respect note. Vodou is a living religion with priests (houngan), priestesses (manbo), spirits (lwa), temples (ounfò) and a congregation of millions; the Parsley Massacre is a mass killing of real people with living descendants. The period's sensational framing ("voodoo", "zombie", "black magic", "savages") is quoted as the source's, never as this file's. The setting's monsters are an overlay on this record; no entry makes Vodou itself the villain.

Overview (45 entries). The real record needs almost no bending to fit the setting before 1924, and then it bends hard. Haiti is a receivership empire in miniature: the Banque Nationale's gold sailed to National City Bank's vault seven months before the landing (Dec 1914); Sam's massacre of 167 prisoners and his lynching at the French legation gave the pretext (27–28 July 1915); Butler crawled through a drain at Fort Rivière; the corvée seized men for road gangs and shot those who ran ⟨fixed: V_M04 — was "roped men together"; Johnson says "seized"⟩, which filled the caco ranks; Péralte was killed by Marines in blackface and his body photographed on a door — the image became a crucifix; Batraville wore Ogou's red badge; the Marines worked out dive-bombing over Haitian villages; the Commandant himself wrote "practically indiscriminate killing". The biggest setting finding: 200 Germans controlled 80% of Haiti's trade [UNVERIFIED — V_M04: the figure is repeated widely but carries "citation needed" on Wikipedia and no scholarly source was found], and in real history they were interned only because America went to war with Germany in 1917 — in the setting they never are. The zombie layer is well sourced and primary: The Magic Island (read in the 1929 text) prints the Hasco zombies (an American sugar company's 1918 payroll; salt breaks the spell) and the Code Pénal's poisoning clause (Art. 246, numbered "249" by Seabrook) and the anti-"sortilège" law that still names Makandal; Felicia Felix-Mentor's appearance at Ennery (24 Oct 1936) is documented by the psychiatrist who X-rayed her (Louis Mars, 1945) as well as by Hurston's "I know". Two Chicago bridges turned up: the Senate's Haiti inquiry was chaired by the Tribune family's Medill McCormick, and Voodoo Macbeth toured Chicago in 1936. In the Dominican Republic, the occupation had no native president at all (a naval military government); Papá Liborio, a healer-prophet who vanished in a 1908 hurricane, was killed in 1922 and his body displayed to prove he could die; the German-born "Tiger of Seibo" was blamed on his ancestry. Trujillo is an American personnel file (Guardia 1918 → general 1928) — and every step after 1924 (the 1930 coup, Ciudad Trujillo, the October 1937 massacre, Évian/Sosúa) depends on a withdrawal that never happens in the setting. Part G lists six decisions that belong to the owner; the massacre is the gravest. Folklore (ciguapa 1866, bacá, fukú, galipote) is labelled.

Part A — Haiti: the landing and the war against the cacos (1914–1920)

The Banque Nationale's gold leaves on the Machias — 17 December 1914

  • Where: the Banque Nationale de la République d'Haïti, Port-au-Prince → the vault of National City Bank, New York.
  • What happened: Seven months before the landing, US Marines came ashore, removed roughly $500,000 in gold from Haiti's national bank and carried it to New York aboard the gunboat USS Machias, into National City Bank's vault. National City had bought into the Haitian bank in 1910–11 and took complete control of it on 17 August 1922. Its vice-president Roger L. Farnham is named by James Weldon Johnson (1920) as "effectively instrumental" in the intervention; Farnham also became receiver of Haiti's National Railroad. Through the 1920s debt service to National City took about a quarter of Haiti's revenue.
  • The odd detail: the invasion's first act was a bank withdrawal by armed men — gold out, Marines back aboard, and the republic's reserve sitting in a Wall Street vault before a single caco was fired on.
  • People: Roger L. Farnham — National City Bank vice-president, the occupation's banker · John Avery McIlhenny — the occupation's Financial Adviser (a Louisiana man; Johnson says he held Haitian officials' salaries hostage).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_occupation_of_Haiti ; https://history.state.gov/milestones/1914-1920/haiti ; https://www.gutenberg.org/files/35025/35025-h/35025-h.htm
  • Confidence: High (date, sum, ship, bank); Medium on the "quarter of revenue" figure (Wikipedia summary).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. It is the setting's "held by the ledger" verb (ALT.HIST §29.4) in its purest form.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): What else went into the vault in the crates from Port-au-Prince? A receivership empire keeps its treasure in Manhattan; a GM can put one object in the 1914 manifest that was never Haiti's money.

Vilbrun Guillaume Sam's last day, and the landing — 27–28 July 1915

  • Where: the prison and the French legation, Port-au-Prince; the landing at Bizoton/Port-au-Prince.
  • What happened: President Vilbrun Guillaume Sam (in office 25 Feb–28 July 1915) was facing Rosalvo Bobo's revolt. On 27 July 1915 he ordered the execution of 167 political prisoners in the Port-au-Prince jail, among them the former president Oreste Zamor. Sam fled to the French legation; a mob broke in, dragged him into the street and killed him. Smedley Butler later testified that he was "cut up into 200 pieces" and dragged through the streets. On 28 July 1915 some 330–340 sailors and Marines under Rear Admiral William Banks Caperton landed; the occupation lasted nineteen years. Philippe Sudré Dartiguenave was installed as president in August 1915 — not Bobo, the man the revolution wanted.
  • The odd detail: the occupation's pretext is a massacre and a lynching inside a foreign legation — a president who sought diplomatic sanctuary and did not get it, and the Americans ashore within a day.
  • People: Vilbrun Guillaume Sam — the 24th president, killed in the street · Oreste Zamor — ex-president shot in the prison killings · Rosalvo Bobo — the caco-backed rival the Americans passed over · William B. Caperton — the admiral · Philippe Sudré Dartiguenave — installed president, Aug 1915.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vilbrun_Guillaume_Sam ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_occupation_of_Haiti
  • Confidence: High for dates, numbers, names; the "200 pieces" line is Butler's testimony, not a count.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers: the papers' "savage mob"; Haitian memory of a president who massacred prisoners; and a GM's option that the legation was not the only thing violated that night.

Fort Rivière — Butler goes in through the drain — 17 November 1915

  • Where: Fort Rivière, an old French fort on a mountaintop near Saint-Raphaël, about 20 miles south of Cap-Haïtien.
  • What happened: Major Smedley Butler, with about 100 Marines and sailors, attacked the last caco stronghold of the First Caco War. The Marines found a partially sealed drain four feet wide, three feet high and fifteen feet deep in the wall; Sgt. Ross Lindsey Iams and Pvt. Samuel Gross crawled through first, Butler behind them with his pistol. More than 50 cacos were killed, including their commander Josaphat Jean-Joseph; no Marine was killed (one lost two teeth to a thrown rock). All three men received the Medal of Honor — Butler's second. The Marines then dynamited the fort and destroyed some 60 dwellings around it.
  • The odd detail: Butler's own verdict — the defenders "could have shot us like rats" as they came out of the hole.
  • People: Smedley D. Butler — Marine major, later ran the Haitian gendarmerie 1915–18 · Ross L. Iams · Samuel Gross — the first two through the drain · Josaphat Jean-Joseph — caco commander, killed.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Fort_Rivi%C3%A8re
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Butler is already a LEAD in PEOPLE_OF_THE_TIMELINE.md.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A fort blown up and its site never rebuilt: what the dynamite opened. The drain-crawl is a ready-made flashback for any Butler-era Marine NPC.

The German colony — 200 people, 80 per cent of the trade [UNVERIFIED — V_M04: no scholarly source found for the figures] — 1897–1918

  • Where: Port-au-Prince and Cap-Haïtien (utilities, the main wharf, the import-export houses).
  • What happened: In 1910 some 200 Germans controlled about 80 per cent of Haiti's international commerce, ran utilities in Port-au-Prince and Cap-Haïtien and the capital's main wharf. Since Haiti's constitutions barred foreigners from owning land, German merchants married into prominent Haitian families. In the Lüders affair (6 Dec 1897) two German warships, SMS Charlotte and SMS Stein, gave President Tirésias Simon Sam four hours to pay a $20,000 indemnity, apologise in writing, fire a 21-gun salute to the German flag and raise a white flag over the palace; he yielded. The State Department's own history lists fear of German control among the 1915 motives. In the real record the Germans left in Haiti were imprisoned at Fort National under American guard after Haiti entered the war (1918); most left for good; their property was never returned.
  • The odd detail: a white flag over Haiti's palace, ordered by a German captain, eighteen years before the Marines.
  • People: Emil Lüders — German-Haitian businessman whose arrest started it · Tirésias Simon Sam — the president who yielded (resigned 1902).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Haitians ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%BCders_affair ; https://history.state.gov/milestones/1914-1920/haiti
  • Confidence: High for the 1897 Lüders affair (6 Dec 1897, Charlotte and Stein, $20,000, four hours, 21-gun salute — confirmed); the 200-people / 80% figures are UNVERIFIED — V_M04 (the same sentence appears on the Wikipedia pages German Haitians and Germany–Haiti relations, the second with "citation needed", and on GlobalSecurity; no scholarly source was located); Medium on internment details (Wikipedia; the date of Haiti's declaration of war, 12 July 1918, is from memory — UNVERIFIED).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — strongly. America never enters the Great War, so Haiti never declares war on Germany, the Germans are never interned and their houses are never confiscated. The occupier's stated fear is realised: a victorious Germany's merchant colony sits inside an American occupation for the whole decade.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Germany as the patron of other people's revolutions", at street level — German import houses in Port-au-Prince with Haitian in-laws, American guards at the wharf they own. Who financed the cacos?

The corvée — men seized to build the Marines' roads ⟨fixed: V_M04 — was "roped together"⟩ — 1916–1 October 1918

  • Where: the new road network across Haiti, especially the north and the Central Plateau (Hinche, Mirebalais).
  • What happened: The occupation revived an old Haitian road-labour obligation (the corvée) to build a highway system for military transport, since Haiti's revenue was going to debt service. Johnson (1920) writes that "the Occupation seized men wherever it could find them", forcibly taken "to toil for months", worked under armed guards, herded into compounds at night, and that "those attempting to escape were shot" ⟨fixed: V_M04 — the earlier wording put "roped together" in quotation marks; those words are not in Johnson's text or on the pages cited⟩; families were not told where they had gone. The corvée was abolished on 1 October 1918 but kept in practice in places. Michel-Rolph Trouillot's estimate, cited by Sciences Po's mass-violence network, is about 5,500 deaths in the labour camps. The State Department's history names forced labour, press censorship and racial segregation as the causes of the peasant rising.
  • The odd detail: the escape attempts are why the caco war's second round happened — men fled the road gangs into the hills and joined the rebels, and Charlemagne Péralte himself had been sentenced to labour in convict dress.
  • People: James Weldon Johnson — NAACP investigator, wrote "Self-Determining Haiti" for The Nation, summer 1920.
  • Sources: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/35025/35025-h/35025-h.htm ; https://www.sciencespo.fr/mass-violence-war-massacre-resistance/en/document/massacres-perpetrated-20th-century-haiti.html ; https://occupationundead.scholar.bucknell.edu/2018/10/01/61/
  • Confidence: High on the practice and the abolition date; the death toll is an estimate (Trouillot 1990). The revived law's date (an 1864 rural code is often cited) — UNVERIFIED this session.
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era (the corvée began before the break and ran under the occupation).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Handle carefully: several scholars read the 1930s American "zombie" — a Black labourer worked to death with no will of his own — as the corvée's shadow. The period's horror image and the occupation's forced labour are the same picture; a GM can let players notice that.

Charlemagne Péralte and the photograph on the door — 31 October–1 November 1919

  • Where: Fort Capois, near Sainte-Suzanne (north Haiti); born at Hinche (b. 10 Oct 1885).
  • What happened: A former mayor of Hinche and military commander of Port-de-Paix, Charlemagne Péralte was sentenced to five years' labour in early 1918 after a failed gendarmerie payroll raid, escaped in September 1918, and by mid-1919 led about 5,000 cacos (the wider rising involved up to 40,000). His 6 October 1919 raid on Port-au-Prince was repelled. On the evening of 31 October 1919 Marine Sgt. Herman H. Hanneken and Cpl. William R. Button, disguised in caco clothes with their skin blackened with cork ash, and guided by former police chief Jean-Baptiste Conzé (paid $2,000), walked into his camp; Hanneken shot him twice in the chest with a .45. The Marines tied his body to a door, photographed it and distributed the picture to break the rebellion. It did the opposite: the image read as a crucifixion, and Péralte became the national martyr (later on stamps and coins). Both Marines received the Medal of Honor.
  • The odd detail: the Americans went in wearing the enemy's face — two white Marines blacked up as cacos — and the propaganda photograph they made became Haiti's icon of resistance.
  • People: Charlemagne Péralte — caco leader, "chief of the revolution" · Herman H. Hanneken — the Marine who shot him (later hunted Sandino's men in Nicaragua) · William R. Button · Jean-Baptiste Conzé — the informer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne_P%C3%A9ralte ; https://www.gutenberg.org/files/35025/35025-h/35025-h.htm
  • Confidence: High. His burial is disputed: a Marine sergeant (Julian Kelly) reported in 1937 that he had been buried covertly near Caracol. The later reburial/state funeral (commonly dated 1935) — UNVERIFIED this session.
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A photograph as a relic. Three layers: the Marines' warning notice; the people's crucified saint; and whatever a GM decides happens to a man whose image is copied thousands of times and whose grave the occupier hid. Respect: Péralte is a real national hero — don't make him a monster.

Benoît Batraville — the red badge of Ogou — January–20 May 1920

  • Where: Mirebalais (born there, 1877); the Bel-Air quarter of Port-au-Prince; Barrière Roche near Lascahobas.
  • What happened: A Mirebalais schoolteacher, Benoît Batraville took over the caco rising after Péralte's death. He practised Vodou and wore a red badge for Ogou, the warrior lwa. On 15 January 1920 he led cacos into Bel-Air; over 300 were killed or captured and the Marines rounded up some 3,200 more fighters. In April 1920 his men ambushed a Marine patrol and mortally wounded Lt. Lawrence Muth; imprisoned cacos later testified that Muth's body was mutilated — the testimony is the Marines' record, given by prisoners. Batraville was killed on 20 May 1920 at Barrière Roche after peasants revealed his hiding place, and was buried at Mirebalais.
  • The odd detail: the American record of the Muth ambush is the seed of every later Marine tale about "voodoo rites" in the hills — told by captured men to their captors.
  • People: Benoît Batraville — schoolteacher, caco leader, Vodou practitioner · Lawrence Muth — Marine officer killed.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beno%C3%AEt_Batraville ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caco_Wars
  • Confidence: High on dates; the Muth mutilation claims are prisoner testimony recorded by Marines — treat as the period's account, not fact. Muth's rank (lieutenant vs. "lieutenant colonel" in one summary) — check.
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A resistance leader who served Ogou is a man of his faith, not a sorcerer; the hook is the Marines' file on him — what the occupier believed he could do, and why they needed to believe it.

Thomazeau and the first dive-bombing — 1919

  • Where: two villages near Thomazeau, the plain east of Port-au-Prince; the Marine air squadron in Haiti.
  • What happened: In the summer of 1919 Lt. Lawson H. M. Sanderson of the Marines' 4th Squadron, flying against the cacos, put his aircraft into a 45-degree dive and released his bomb at about 250 feet [UNVERIFIED — V_M04: the Sanderson dive rests on a search summary of a Navy history PDF, not a page read] — an early form of the dive-bombing the Marines later made doctrine. Sciences Po's mass-violence network (citing Roger Gaillard) records that in November 1919 planes bombed and machine-gunned two villages near Thomazeau, killing about half their inhabitants — possibly among the first aerial attacks on civilians.
  • The odd detail: a technique later credited to naval aviation history was worked out over Haitian peasant villages.
  • People: Lawson H. M. Sanderson — Marine aviator.
  • Sources: https://www.sciencespo.fr/mass-violence-war-massacre-resistance/en/document/massacres-perpetrated-20th-century-haiti.html ; Marine Corps aviation history (search summary): https://www.history.navy.mil/content/dam/nhhc/research/histories/naval-aviation/history-of-marine-corps-aviation/pdfs/mca-m3.pdf
  • Confidence: Medium — the dive is well attested (via search summary); the Thomazeau casualty figure rests on Gaillard and is not numbered.
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Aircraft over the Plaine du Cul-de-Sac: what the peasants called the machines, and what a Marine pilot saw from 250 feet that he never put in the report.

"Practically indiscriminate killing of natives" — Barnett's letter — 1919–1920

  • Where: Marine Corps headquarters, Washington → Col. John H. Russell in Haiti; Hinche; the north.
  • What happened: In the autumn of 1919 Marine Commandant George Barnett wrote a "personal and confidential" letter to Col. John H. Russell complaining that "practically indiscriminate killing of natives has been going on for some time", which prompted the first inquiry. The letter was published in October 1920, in the middle of the presidential campaign. Abuses were concentrated in the north under Maj. Clark H. Wells, relieved in 1920 after Barnett found he had falsified reports. Gendarmes testified to prisoner executions; two officers, Freeman Lang and Dorcas Williams, were implicated; Gaillard records 19 caco prisoners executed at Hinche in January 1919 under a Captain Lavoie. The Navy, the Corps and later the Senate each found "only a few" substantiated cases.
  • The odd detail: the Corps' own commandant wrote the sentence the occupation's critics quoted for years.
  • People: George Barnett — Marine Commandant · John H. Russell — brigade commander, later High Commissioner of Haiti (1922–30) · Clark H. Wells — relieved · Freeman Lang, Dorcas Williams — named officers.
  • Sources: search summaries of https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2021/august/never-known-day-peace and https://www.zinnedproject.org/materials/woodrow-wilson-myths/ ; https://www.sciencespo.fr/mass-violence-war-massacre-resistance/en/document/massacres-perpetrated-20th-century-haiti.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caco_Wars
  • Confidence: Medium-High (quotation consistent across sources; page not fetched in full).
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era; but see the next entries — in the setting the 1920 campaign it fed is a different campaign (Harding is a senator; Wood runs in 1920).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A confidential letter as a document the players can find — who in Washington kept a copy, and why the name of one relieved major keeps coming up in a Chicago file.

The "Roosevelt Constitution" — 1917 visit, 1918 plebiscite, 1920 boast, 1934 visit

  • Where: Haiti (Jan 1917); the 1918 plebiscite; the 1920 campaign trail; Cap-Haïtien (5 July 1934 ⟨fixed: V_M04⟩).
  • What happened: Franklin Roosevelt, Assistant Secretary of the Navy, spent less than a week [UNVERIFIED — V_M04: length of stay not confirmed] in Haiti in January 1917, with the Financial Adviser McIlhenny, scouting plantation land. The 1918 constitution, approved by plebiscite 98,225 to 768 after the Haitian assembly had been dissolved by force, removed the old ban on foreign land ownership and ratified every act of the occupation. Campaigning for vice-president in 1920, Roosevelt told reporters he had written Haiti's constitution himself (historians say he did not; the occupation's officers had). On 5 July 1934 ⟨fixed: V_M04 — the joint statement with President Vincent is dated 5 July 1934, Presidential Palace, Cap-Haïtien⟩ he became the first sitting US president to visit Haiti, and issued a joint statement with President Vincent on the withdrawal.
  • The odd detail: the man who in the real record ends the occupation had earlier boasted of writing its constitution.
  • People: Franklin D. Roosevelt — Asst. SecNav 1913–20; president from 1933 in both histories.
  • Sources: https://talkingpointsmemo.com/primary-source/haiti-constitution-fdr ; https://occupationundead.scholar.bucknell.edu/2017/01/20/franklin-d-roosevelt-in-haiti/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_occupation_of_Haiti
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: 1917–18 holds (Wilson's Navy, then Marshall's). BENDS in 1934: the setting rules that Roosevelt's retreat from empire does not happen — no withdrawal from Haiti (RESOLVED_ANSWERS, Audit 151). His July 1934 visit can still happen — as a president inspecting a colony he once claimed to have written the law for.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Roosevelt at Cap-Haïtien, 1934, not signing a withdrawal — what he came for instead, and who he met.

A Chicago senator's inquiry — Senate Select Committee on Haiti and Santo Domingo — 1921–1922

  • Where: Washington, and Port-au-Prince (hearings in late Nov–early Dec 1921).
  • What happened: Under S. Res. 112 the Senate set up a select committee "to inquire into the occupation and administration" of Haiti and the Dominican Republic, chaired by Senator Medill McCormick of Illinois. Hearings ran from 5 August 1921 to June 1922, some held in Port-au-Prince itself. The report (printed June 1922) accepted that there had been abuses but concluded the occupation should continue. (The brief's "1920 inquiry" is the Navy/Marine inquiries of 1919–20 and the Johnson articles; the Senate's own inquiry is 1921–22.) McCormick — grandson of the Chicago Tribune's founder Joseph Medill, a sometime manager of the paper — died in his suite at the Hamilton Hotel, Washington, on 25 February 1925; it was not reported so at the time, but the death was considered a suicide.
  • The odd detail: the man who took testimony about the occupation in Port-au-Prince was a Chicago newspaper heir whose own death was quietly misreported.
  • People: Medill McCormick — US Senator (R-Ill.) 1919–25 · Ruth Hanna McCormick — his wife, later a congresswoman.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medill_McCormick ; https://archive.org/details/inquiryintooccu00domigoog ; https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/001972855 (search summaries for hearing dates and chair)
  • Confidence: High (committee, chair, dates via catalog records); the suicide is Wikipedia's wording.
  • Divergence check: holds in shape — a senator's inquiry fits any American government; BENDS in its politics (in the real record it was a Republican Senate probing a Democratic occupation after Harding's 1920 attacks; here the 1921 government is Wood's).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A bridge to the Chicago book: the Tribune family's senator heard Haitian witnesses in 1921 and was dead in a Washington hotel in 1925. What he took home in his luggage is a lead a GM can seed.

Marchaterre — "À bas la misère" — 6 December 1929

  • Where: Marchaterre, near Les Cayes, southern Haiti.
  • What happened: It began on 31 October 1929 as a strike at the agricultural school at Damien over cuts to scholarships for Haitians, and widened into strikes against censorship, customs, taxes and hardship. On 6 December 1929 about 1,500 peasants marched, chanting "À bas la misère" ("down with misery") and protesting the arrest of three leaders. Marines with machine guns blocked the road; after stones were thrown, they fired. 12 to 22 Haitians were killed and 21 to 51 wounded (sources vary). Hoover ordered an inquiry the next day; the Forbes Commission arrived on 28 February 1930, called the occupation a failure, and set in motion the 1930 elections that made Sténio Vincent president and the withdrawal agreement of 7 August 1933.
  • The odd detail: a students' scholarship strike at an agricultural school is the thread that pulls down the occupation — in the real record.
  • People: Herbert Hoover — president 1929–33 in both histories · W. Cameron Forbes — commission chairman · Louis Borno — client president, resigned 1930 · Sténio Vincent — president from Nov 1930.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Cayes_massacre ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_occupation_of_Haiti
  • Confidence: High on date and place; casualty range as given.
  • Divergence check: the massacre holds — occupation-era. BENDS after it: the Forbes Commission can still sit, but its "withdraw" conclusion is shelved; Vincent can still be elected in 1930 — as a president under an occupation that never ends.

Part B — Vodou, the law, and the American "zombie" (1864–1945)

Framing for this part. Vodou is a religion; the "zombie" of 1929–37 American print and film is an American commercial product built on a Haitian belief, during an American occupation. Entries below keep three things apart: what Haitians believe (the zonbi as a soul or body stolen and enslaved — a horror of being made a slave, not of being eaten); what Haitian law says (a poisoning clause); and what Americans sold (Seabrook, Hollywood). The sensational words are the sources' words.

Vodou as a living religion — the basics a GM must get right

  • Where: all Haiti, town and country; alongside Catholicism, not instead of it.
  • What happened: Vodou centres on Bondye, a distant creator, and the lwa (spirits) who act in the world — more than a thousand of them, grouped in "nations" (nanchon): the cool, righteous Rada, the hot, morally ambiguous Petwo, and the Gede, the family of death and the dead. Priests (oungan) and priestesses (manbo) lead rites in the ounfò (temple), whose ceremonial room, the peristil, has a centre post (poto mitan) through which the lwa arrive. Lwa are called with vèvè drawn in cornmeal or ash, and speak through possession. Most Haitians practised Vodou and Catholicism together, with lwa matched to saints (Papa Legba ↔ St Peter, keeper of keys). The Haitian psychiatrist Louis P. Mars (1945) described it plainly for foreign readers: spirits are intermediaries, "God is too far away" to attend to daily life, every peasant keeps an altar, and it is "a very serious matter" on which people sometimes spend more than on necessities.
  • The odd detail: in Haitian belief as Mars recorded it, zombi also means the soul of someone never possessed by a lwa, which cannot go to heaven — and "a farmer who is successful" is suspected of having zombis working for him. The zombi is an accusation about who profits from whose labour.
  • People: Louis P. Mars, M.D. — Haitian psychiatrist, government medical officer, later of the Bureau/Institute of Ethnology.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Vodou ; https://sites.duke.edu/haitilab/files/2014/11/Mars-1945-The-story-of-zombie-in-haiti.pdf (read in full)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — the religion is older than the divergence and untouched by it.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): ALT.HIST §29.3 already rules "never 'voodoo jungle magic'" and "cult versus population". The right Umbra use is an oungan or manbo as an expert witness and ally — a professional of the unseen whose knowledge the IHS lacks and the Marines criminalise.

Article 246 — the Code Pénal's "zombification" clause — 1835 [UNVERIFIED — V_M04: Gizmodo gives only the 1864 amendment] / amended 1864

  • Where: the Haitian Code Pénal (as in force in the 1920s–30s; editions of 1883 are often cited).
  • What happened: Haiti's criminal code defined poisoning as attempted murder. Under President Fabre Nicolas Geffrard (in power from 1859), who set out to eradicate "old superstitions", an 1864 amendment added the famous paragraphs: using against a person substances that, without causing death, produce a more or less prolonged lethargic state, is attempted murder by poisoning — and if the person was buried as a result, it is murder. Seabrook printed it in 1929 (as "Article 249", in English, and in French in his appendix) as evidence that zombies were real; the modern numbering is 246.
  • The odd detail: the clause never uses the word zombi. The "zombie law" is a poisoning statute that the whole American zombie myth was made to stand on — including by the author who got its number wrong.
  • People: Fabre Nicolas Geffrard — president 1859–67 · William Seabrook — who put it before American readers.
  • Sources: https://gizmodo.com/the-curious-history-of-haitis-anti-zombie-laws-1654986154 ; Seabrook, The Magic Island (1929), text read at https://archive.org/details/magicislandbywbs00seab (ch. "Dead Men Working", appendix)
  • Confidence: High on the text and Seabrook's citation (read in the book itself); Medium on 1835/1864 dating (one secondary source).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A statute that assumes the thing exists. An occupation court in the setting's never-ending Haiti enforces it — and a Section 13 agent has to decide whether an Article 246 case is a poisoning, a sorcery, or both.

The law against "sortilèges" — what the Marines' courts could enforce

  • Where: the Code Pénal's articles against sorcery (usually cited as Arts. 405–407; Seabrook cites "Article 409").
  • What happened: Seabrook's appendix (1929) quotes the French text: all "faiseurs de ouangas, caprelatas, vaudoux, dompèdres, macandals et autres sortilèges" are punished with three to six months in prison by the police court, and on a repeat offence six months to two years; and "all dances and other practices" that keep up "fetishism and superstition" in the population are to be treated as sorcery and punished the same. Under the occupation Vodou was suppressed as an obstacle to "modernisation" (Wikipedia notes the specifics are thinly documented). After the Marines, Sténio Vincent's 1935 law again banned "superstitious practices", and the Catholic Church's campagne anti-superstitieuse (peaking c. 1939–42, under President Élie Lescot from 1941) destroyed ritual objects and sacred spaces and made practitioners swear the rejeté — a public renunciation oath.
  • The odd detail: the list names "macandals" — the law still carries, as a word for sorcery, the name of François Makandal, the maroon leader burned at Cap-Français in 1758.
  • People: Sténio Vincent — president 1930–41 · Élie Lescot — president 1941–46.
  • Sources: Seabrook, The Magic Island, appendix (read: https://archive.org/details/magicislandbywbs00seab) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Vodou
  • Confidence: High for the quoted text (as Seabrook printed it); Medium for the 1935 law's exact date and terms. Makandal's 1758 execution is well known but not re-checked this session.
  • Divergence check: the law is pre-1916. The 1935 and 1939–42 campaigns BEND: in the setting the Marines never leave, so Vincent's decree and Lescot's campaign would be passed under the occupation — or by it.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The IHS and the Catholic hierarchy in Haiti are not the same thing, and the 1939–42 campaign is the place to show it — a burning of drums and altars that a Umbra IHS priest knows is destroying the only people who could hold something back.

William Seabrook's The Magic Island — 3 January 1929

  • Where: New York (Harcourt, Brace); Haiti, where Seabrook lived c. 1927–28 (dates not confirmed this session).
  • What happened: A travel book by the American journalist William Buehler Seabrook, illustrated by Alexander King, published 3 January 1929 and made the Literary Guild's book of the month. Its chapter "Dead Men Working in the Cane Fields" gave American English the zombie, defined as "a soulless human corpse, still dead, but taken from the grave and endowed by sorcery with a mechanical semblance of life". Seabrook claims initiation by a priestess he calls Maman Célie, including a blood rite, and writes up Faustin Wirkus, the Marine "king" of La Gonâve. It inspired Kenneth Webb's Broadway play Zombie (1932) and White Zombie (1932). It entered the US public domain on 1 January 2025.
  • The odd detail: the chapter headings put a Wall Street–style company name at the centre of the horror — Seabrook calls "Hasco" "a chunk of Hoboken" in Port-au-Prince's eastern suburbs.
  • People: William B. Seabrook (1884–1945) — journalist, occultist, later author of Asylum (1935) · Maman Célie — priestess (as Seabrook names her) · Alexander King — illustrator.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magic_Island ; https://archive.org/details/magicislandbywbs00seab
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era (Seabrook's Haiti is the occupation's Haiti, and the book is an American bestseller in both histories).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Seabrook is a real American occult celebrity (he knew Aleister Crowley — UNVERIFIED this session) who made money telling Americans what Haitians believed. The period's layer, personified.

The Hasco zombies — spring 1918 (as told to Seabrook)

  • Where: the Haitian-American Sugar Company (HASCO) mill and cane fields, eastern Port-au-Prince and the Cul-de-Sac plain; Morne-au-Diable, "a roadless mountain district near the Dominican border"; Croix-des-Bouquets.
  • What happened: In Seabrook's book the Haitian farmer Constant Polynice tells this as true. In the big cane season of spring 1918, HASCO (paying "twenty or thirty cents a day") offered a bonus for new workers. The headman Ti Joseph of Colombier registered a gang who stared "vacant-eyed like cattle" and gave no names — Joseph said they were mountain people who did not understand the plains' creole. They were zombies he and his wife Croyance had taken from their graves, fed only unsalted food. At Fête-Dieu Croyance, pitying them, took them to see the procession and bought them tablettes pistaches — peanut sweets; the baker had salted the nuts. Tasting salt, they knew they were dead, walked back to Morne-au-Diable, clawed at their own graves and fell dead. Their families took Ti Joseph's name and a sweat-stained shirt to a bokor beyond Trou Caïman for a ouanga; Seabrook's teller says Joseph was later killed.
  • The odd detail: salt breaks the spell — Mars (1945) confirms the belief independently: "The Zombis are supposed to eat no salt."
  • People: Constant Polynice — Seabrook's informant · Ti Joseph and Croyance — the story's (unverifiable) sorcerer couple.
  • Sources: Seabrook, The Magic Island, ch. "Dead Men Working in the Cane Fields" (read: https://archive.org/details/magicislandbywbs00seab) ; Mars 1945 (above)
  • Confidence: High that Seabrook printed it; folklore/hearsay as to events — label it "as Seabrook's informant told it".
  • Divergence check: holds — 1918 is occupation-era.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The setting's single best Haiti seed: an American sugar company's 1918 payroll with a gang of workers who never gave names. The ledger exists; the question is who signed for them.

The White King of La Gonâve — Faustin Wirkus crowned 18 July 1926

  • Where: La Gonâve, the large island in the Gulf of Gonâve west of Port-au-Prince.
  • What happened: Faustin E. Wirkus, a Pennsylvania coal-sorter turned Marine (enlisted 1915), gunnery sergeant and a lieutenant in the Garde d'Haïti, commanded the post on La Gonâve. After he helped the island's Queen Ti Memenne, and because he shared a name with the emperor Faustin Soulouque, islanders proclaimed him "Faustin II" in a Vodou ceremony on 18 July 1926; he "co-ruled" with the queen for three years. Seabrook made him famous; Wirkus wrote The White King of La Gonave (1931, with Taney Dudley, Seabrook's introduction) and told his story in a 1933 short film, Voodoo. Transferred stateside in 1929, left the Corps in 1931, re-enlisted 1939, died 8 October 1945 at Brooklyn Naval Hospital; buried at Arlington.
  • The odd detail: his birthplace is disputed — his official biography says Rypin, Poland; ship and census records point to Pittston, Pennsylvania.
  • People: Faustin Wirkus — Marine, "king" · Ti Memenne — queen of La Gonâve (as the sources name her) · Taney Dudley.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faustin_Wirkus ; Seabrook, The Magic Island
  • Confidence: High for dates; the "coronation" is as reported by Wirkus and Seabrook — the Haitian side's meaning of the ceremony is not recorded in these sources.
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era; in the setting nobody transfers him home when the occupation ends, because it doesn't.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A Marine who was given a title in a rite he did not understand, on an island the occupation barely reached. What did the island think it was crowning — and what did it expect of him?

Felicia Felix-Mentor, the "zombie" of Ennery — 24 October 1936, photographed 1937

  • Where: Ennery, in the hills north of Gonaïves ("near Cap-Haïtien" in Mars's words); the government hospital at Gonaïves; the Central Hospital, Port-au-Prince.
  • What happened: Early on 24 October 1936 a ragged, emaciated woman, her eyes diseased and covered with a dark rag, appeared in Ennery's streets; crowds gathered. The Mentor family took her for Felicia Felix-Mentor, who had died and been buried in 1907 (at 29, per Wikipedia) — she was lame, as Felicia had been from a broken left leg. She was moved to a government hospital. Zora Neale Hurston visited and photographed her in the hospital yard — "the first known depiction" of a person her neighbours knew as a zombi (Charles King) — and Life printed a photograph on 13 December 1937. Hurston wrote in Tell My Horse (1938): "I know that there are Zombies in Haiti." The government psychiatrist Louis P. Mars, sent to study the case weeks later, X-rayed both legs at the Central Hospital: no fracture — the limp was undernourishment and went after two months' diet. His verdict (1945): "evidently a case of schizophrenia", and "mass hysteria".
  • The odd detail: per Charles King's account, her return threatened a settled family: the brother had become a prosperous farmer, the ex-husband a government official with a new family — a zombie who comes home is also a claimant.
  • People: "Felicia Felix-Mentor" — the unidentified woman · Zora Neale Hurston — novelist-anthropologist · Louis P. Mars — psychiatrist.
  • Sources: https://sites.duke.edu/haitilab/files/2014/11/Mars-1945-The-story-of-zombie-in-haiti.pdf (read) ; https://www.delanceyplace.com/view-archives.php?p=4338 (excerpt of Charles King, Gods of the Upper Air, 2019) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felicia_Felix-Mentor ; Life date via search summary.
  • Confidence: High on the 1936 date, Ennery and the X-ray (Mars, primary); Medium on the Life issue date.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — in the setting the Garde and the hospital are still under American officers in 1936; Mars's government report would be read by an occupation.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A true three layers case, already documented: the village's zombi, the psychiatrist's schizophrenic, and Hurston's "I know". An Umbra GM decides the fourth — and the X-ray plate is a clue object sitting in a Port-au-Prince hospital file. Respect: she was a real, suffering woman; don't make her a monster.

Part C — Haiti: places, Marines' books, and the Haitian answer (1805–1936)

The Citadelle Laferrière and Sans-Souci — built 1805–1820; ruined 1842

  • Where: the Bonnet à l'Évêque peak above Milot, south of Cap-Haïtien, at about 910 m (3,000 ft); the palace of Sans-Souci at Milot below it.
  • What happened: Commissioned by Henri Christophe in 1805 after independence and finished in 1820, the Citadelle was built by tens of thousands of former slaves against a French return that never came; the construction is said to have cost about 20,000 lives. Its walls rise 40 m from the rock; it held 365 cannon of assorted sizes, and its cannonballs still lie in pyramidal stacks at the foot of the walls. Christophe, king of the north from 1811, suffered a stroke in 1820; his troops mutinied and — legend says — he shot himself with a silver bullet; his followers covered his body in quicklime and walled it into one of the Citadelle's courtyards. Sans-Souci palace (built c. 1810–13) was wrecked in the 1842 earthquake. Both are a UNESCO site (1982).
  • The odd detail: a king buried in quicklime inside his own fortress — the setting's "consecrated ground" idea (ALT.HIST §29.3) in stone, on a mountain.
  • People: Henri Christophe (Henry I) — revolutionary general, king of northern Haiti 1811–20.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citadelle_Laferri%C3%A8re
  • Confidence: High on dates and dimensions; the death-toll figure is traditional; silver bullet and quicklime are legend, labelled so by the source. Occupation-era Marine visits to the Citadelle are well known but not sourced this session.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. In the setting the fortress sits in an American-occupied north for the whole 1930s.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Orson Welles set his 1936 Macbeth at Christophe's court (next entry). A Citadelle module: the occupation wants the cannon for scrap or the peak for a radio mast; the quicklime courtyard is the thing nobody should open.

Jean Price-Mars, Ainsi parla l'oncle — 1928

  • Where: Port-au-Prince; born Grande-Rivière-du-Nord (15 Oct 1876), d. Pétion-Ville (1 Mar 1969).

  • The odd detail: the first serious Haitian defence of Vodou as a religion appeared the year before Seabrook sold America the zombie.

Part D — The Dominican Republic: the occupation and the gavilleros (1916–1924)

The landing and the military government — 13 May–29 November 1916

  • Where: Santo Domingo; Puerto Plata and Monte Cristi (1 June); Las Trencheras (27 June); Guayacanes; Santiago (3 July).
  • What happened: With President Juan Isidro Jimenes resigning rather than accept American control, Rear Admiral William B. Caperton — the same admiral who landed in Haiti ten months earlier — forced the war minister Desiderio Arias out of Santo Domingo on 13 May 1916 by threatening to bombard the city. Marines landed on 15 May, took Puerto Plata and Monte Cristi on 1 June, fought at Las Trencheras (27 June; five Marines killed) and Guayacanes (27 Dominicans killed; one Marine, Cpl. George Fravee), and entered Santiago; Arias surrendered and left. On 29 November 1916 Capt. Harry Shepard Knapp proclaimed a military government — the Dominican Republic governed directly by US naval officers (Knapp, then Rear Adm. Edwin Anderson Jr. and successors). The Marines built a Guardia Nacional (1917). The customs had already been in American hands since the 1905 receivership (ALT.HIST §29.4's "held by the ledger"); a 27 December 1924 treaty kept them so until 1941.
  • The odd detail: Haiti kept a Haitian president as a front; the Dominican Republic did not even get that — its government for eight years was an American naval officer's proclamation.
  • People: William B. Caperton — admiral of both landings · Desiderio Arias — war minister, rebel · Juan Isidro Jimenes — president, resigned · Harry S. Knapp — first military governor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_occupation_of_the_Dominican_Republic_(1916%E2%80%931924)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: the landing is pre-/at the break (May 1916), happened. The 1922 Hughes–Peynado plan, the 15 March 1924 election of Horacio Vásquez and the 18 September 1924 withdrawal BEND — they never happen. In the setting the military government, or something like it, runs through the 1930s.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A colony without a native head of state is the cleanest "empire" the setting has. Who is the military governor of Santo Domingo in 1936 — and what did the previous one see that got him recalled?

Gregorio Urbano Gilbert fires on the landing — San Pedro de Macorís, 10 January 1917

  • Where: the port of San Pedro de Macorís, the sugar coast east of Santo Domingo.
  • What happened: As Marines came ashore at San Pedro de Macorís on 10 January 1917, Gregorio Urbano Gilbert — eighteen, a linotypist and store clerk born in Puerto Plata (25 May 1898) to a Dominican mother and a Bahamian father — loaded a revolver and fired on them, killing an officer, C. H. Burton. Captured and sentenced to death, commuted to life, he was released on 22 October 1922, went to Cuba, Curaçao and Nicaragua, and fought with Sandino against the Marines there (from 1928). He came home in 1929, lived to fight the second American landing in 1965, and died on 29 November 1970; in 2019 he was declared a National Hero — "hero of two peoples".
  • The odd detail: one teenage clerk's revolver links the Dominican occupation to Sandino's war — the same Marines, the same man, two countries.
  • People: Gregorio Urbano Gilbert — resistance fighter · C. H. Burton — Marine officer killed.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorio_Urbano_Gilbert ; search summary of https://www.elcaribe.com.do/gente/cultura/con-apenas-17-anos-de-edad-gregorio-urbano-gilbert-se-enfrento-los-yanquis/

  • Divergence check: holds — 10 Jan 1917 is occupation-era; BENDS after 1922: in the setting Nicaragua's Marines never leave either, so Gilbert's Nicaraguan war never ends.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The man who carries a grievance from island to island — the setting's "empire as a transport network" (ALT.HIST §29.3) works for the resistance too.

Cayo Báez and the photograph of his scars — c. 1920–1923

  • Where: near Salcedo, in the Cibao; the magazine Las Letras, Santo Domingo.

Papá Liborio — the healer-prophet of San Juan, killed 27 June 1922

  • Where: San Juan de la Maguana and the southwestern valleys near the Haitian border; killed on a hill near the frontier; body shown in San Juan's Parque Central.
  • What happened: Olivorio Mateo (b. 1876, San Juan de la Maguana, to Afro-Dominican farmers), a labourer, vanished during a sudden hurricane in 1908 and came back seven days later as a divine messenger — "Papá Liborio", "the Master": he healed "with a shot of rum", urged peace, promoted the cult of the Holy Trinity and prophesied. His following alarmed the Dominican state and then the Marines; between 1916 and 1922 he faced occupation forces 16 times (at La Peñita, 1920, dozens were killed). On 27 June 1922 he was shot dead with one of his sons. His body was carried by truck to San Juan and displayed in the central park by American officers, so that people would know he was not a god. His devotion survived: a shrine and spring ("La Agüita de Papá Liborio") are still visited, and in December 1962 a Liborista commune at Palma Sola was destroyed by the Dominican military (hundreds killed) — outside this window.
  • The odd detail: the Marines displayed a dead messiah's body to prove he could die — exactly the logic of the Péralte door, three years later and one border away — and his followers still say he did not stay dead.
  • People: Olivorio Mateo, "Papá Liborio" — healer, prophet, rebel · his followers, the Liboristas.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivorio_Mateo ; search summaries of https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palma_Sola_massacre and https://juanbutten.com/the-life-and-death-of-papa-liborio-a-legacy-in-collective-memory/
  • Confidence: High on dates; the display of the body is from search summaries of two sources (consistent). Liborismo is a living popular devotion — report it as such.
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era (1922). In the setting the 1962 massacre's context (post-Trujillo) does not arise; the Liboristas persist under an American military government.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers, ready-made: the Marines' "bandit"; the people's healer who came back from the storm; and what an Umbra GM decides happened during the seven days. Respect: a living devotion — the prophet is not the monster; the thing in the storm may be.

Part E — Trujillo's rise, and what the setting does to it (1918–1938)

Rafael Trujillo joins the Marines' Guardia — 1918–1928

  • Where: San Cristóbal (born 24 October 1891); the Guardia Nacional (later Policía Nacional Dominicana).
  • What happened: A former telegraph operator with a record of petty crime (cattle rustling, cheque forgery and postal robbery, per Wikipedia) and a gang called "the 42", Rafael Leónidas Trujillo joined the American-built Guardia Nacional in 1918, sponsored by a US officer, Maj. James J. McLean. Trained by the Marines, he rose: lieutenant 1919, captain 1922, major 1923, commander of the National Police 1924, brigadier general 1928.
  • The odd detail: the dictator's career is an American personnel file — every step from second lieutenant to general was taken inside the force the Marines created.
  • People: Rafael L. Trujillo · James J. McLean — his US Marine sponsor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafael_Trujillo ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_occupation_of_the_Dominican_Republic_(1916%E2%80%931924)
  • Confidence: High on the promotions; the early-crime claims are as Wikipedia reports them.
  • Divergence check: 1918–24 holds — occupation-era. After 1924 BENDS: with no withdrawal there is no Vásquez government to command; Trujillo's rise stops at whatever rank the Americans let a Dominican hold — probably chief of the native constabulary under American officers.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The setting's version of Trujillo is more dangerous for being blocked: a Marine-trained police chief with a gang called "the 42", a ceiling above him, and every reason to look for a patron who can lift it — the Golden Circle, or Germany.

The 1930 coup and the impossible election — February–16 August 1930

  • Where: Santiago and Santo Domingo.
  • What happened: In February 1930 a revolt from Santiago under Rafael Estrella Ureña toppled the ageing President Horacio Vásquez, while Trujillo, commanding the army, kept it in barracks by secret agreement. Estrella became acting president on 3 March 1930; Trujillo ran for president, his opponents were terrorised, and he was inaugurated on 16 August 1930 with a claimed 99 per cent — the US minister reported more votes than there were voters.
  • The odd detail: the US legation's own count — more votes than voters — is the epitaph of the Guardia it built.
  • People: Horacio Vásquez — president 1924–30 · Rafael Estrella Ureña — the front man · Trujillo.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafael_Trujillo
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — does not happen as recorded. There is no sovereign Dominican president to overthrow. The setting's equivalent is a question for the owner: does Trujillo seize power from the Americans (a mutiny of the Guardia), for them (a native strongman installed to cut garrison costs), or never?
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Leave it open as a live 1930s possibility — a coup rumour in a colony's constabulary is a better campaign engine than a settled dictatorship.

Hurricane San Zenón — 3 September 1930

  • Where: Santo Domingo; landfall near the city.
  • What happened: A small, violent Category 4 hurricane (peak winds about 155 mph, 933 mbar, a radius of maximum winds of only 8 miles, a destruction path about 2 miles wide at landfall) struck the capital on 3 September 1930 — San Zenón's day — about two weeks after Trujillo's inauguration. Deaths are estimated at 2,000 to 8,000; the Red Cross counted 2,000 dead and 8,000 injured in the city. About half the city was levelled, three districts almost entirely. Trujillo put the whole army on the streets within 24 hours, took emergency powers, and built his reputation on the rebuilding. The Red Cross sent $15,000 the next day; the British HMS Danae and RFA Serbol brought help. The ship Coamo spent seven hours in the storm, passing through the eye.
  • The odd detail: a hurricane named for the saint of its day, arriving in a new dictator's third week, gave him the ruins to build a personality cult on. (Mass burning of bodies is widely reported but not confirmed this session.)
  • People: Trujillo · the crew of the Coamo.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1930_Dominican_Republic_hurricane ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafael_Trujillo
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: the storm holds (weather ignores treaties); its politics BEND — in the setting the relief is run by the American military government, and whoever commands the Guardia gets the credit or the blame.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A city half-flattened in an afternoon: which old walls, crypts and colonial foundations the wind opened in the first city of the Americas, and what walked out before the Marines arrived.

"Dios y Trujillo" — Ciudad Trujillo, 1936

  • Where: Santo Domingo, renamed Ciudad Trujillo in 1936; the island's highest peak, renamed Pico Trujillo.
  • What happened: Trujillo renamed the oldest European city in the Americas after himself in 1936, and the highest mountain too. An electric sign read "Dios y Trujillo"; churches were required to post "God in Heaven, Trujillo on Earth"; he took the title "Benefactor of the Nation". Historian Robin (Lauren) Derby's The Dictator's Seduction (2009) studies how the regime ruled through ritual, gossip, gift exchange — and witchcraft.
  • The odd detail: a state that made its churches post the dictator beside God.
  • People: Trujillo · Robin Derby — historian (UCLA).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafael_Trujillo ; https://www.international.ucla.edu/lai/article/202890
  • Confidence: High on the renamings and the slogans.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — needs Trujillo in sovereign power. In the setting the capital stays Santo Domingo unless the owner rules otherwise.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "God in Heaven, [X] on Earth" is a cult formula. If the setting's Trujillo never gets a country, a GM can give the slogan to someone who does get a following.

The Columbus Lighthouse competition — 1929–1931

  • Where: Santo Domingo (the site); the competition's final judging in Rio de Janeiro, 1931.
  • What happened: The idea of a monument to Columbus in Santo Domingo went back to the historian Antonio del Monte y Tejada (1852); the Fifth Pan-American Conference (Santiago de Chile, 1923) resolved that the governments of the Americas should build it. An international design competition drew 455 entrants from 48 countries; the jury, meeting in 1931, included Frank Lloyd Wright, Eliel Saarinen and Uruguay's Horacio Acosta y Lara, and chose the young Scottish architect Joseph Lea Gleave's cross-shaped design. Foundations were dug only in 1948; it was finished in 1992. It is meant to hold the remains the Dominican Republic says are Columbus's (found in the cathedral in 1877); Seville says DNA shows its own are his.
  • The odd detail: Frank Lloyd Wright judged a monument to Columbus for the first city of the Americas in the year after San Zenón flattened it.
  • People: Joseph Lea Gleave — winning architect · Frank Lloyd Wright · Eliel Saarinen — jurors.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbus_Lighthouse
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: the Pan-American machinery BENDS in a hemisphere where America owns half the delegates' countries; the competition can still run.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Two claimants to one skeleton — Seville and Santo Domingo — is a relic dispute the IHS would follow closely. Pair with the fukú (Part F).

The Parsley Massacre — el corte, kout kouto-a — 2–8 October 1937

Respect. This is the killing of some twenty thousand real people — Haitian migrants and Dominicans of Haitian descent, many born in the borderlands — whose families still live on both sides of the river. Record it as what it was; do not use it as scenery. If the owner wants an occult layer here, it should sit with the killers and their patrons, never with the victims or their religion.

  • Where: the northwestern frontier and the Cibao — Dajabón, Montecristi (bodies thrown into the sea), Santiago and the border villages; the Dajabón (Massacre) River; killings resumed in the south early in 1938.
  • What happened: On 2 October 1937, at a dance in his honour in Dajabón Province, Trujillo announced that "three hundred Haitians are now dead in Bánica" and that this "remedy" would continue. Over the next week Dominican army units brought in from across the country killed Haitians and Dominicans of Haitian descent — mostly with machetes, bayonets and even shovels, so that the killing would look like peasant violence; US investigators found Krag rifle bullets, a weapon only the army had. Soldiers were reported to get drunk before the killings. Estimates run from 14,000 to 40,000; most scholars accept about 20,000 — perhaps a quarter to a half or more of the Haitian population in the country — and 2,419 injured were recorded. Haitians call it kout kouto-a ("the stabbing") or masak nan pèsil; Dominicans, el corte ("the cutting").
  • The odd detail: the army chose machetes as a disguise — the state dressed a military operation as a peasant uprising, and it was a bullet from an obsolete American-pattern rifle that gave it away.
  • People: Rafael Trujillo — ordered it · the victims — Haitian and Dominican-Haitian borderland families, mostly unnamed in the record.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsley_massacre ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafael_Trujillo ; https://www.sciencespo.fr/mass-violence-war-massacre-resistance/en/document/massacres-perpetrated-20th-century-haiti.html (17,000–20,000; "up to 15,000 in the northeast"; renewed killings early 1938; Turits, Castor, Roorda).
  • Confidence: High on dates, places, method; the toll is an estimate range.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — heavily. In the real record this is one sovereign state's army killing another state's nationals across a border. In the setting both countries are American-occupied, so the border is an internal line between two American possessions and the Guardia is American-officered. Either (a) it never happens, (b) it happens and the occupier is directly responsible, or (c) a Trujillo who seized the Guardia does it anyway. Owner's call — flagged in Part G.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): If it happens in the setting, it is the Golden Circle's programme at its most naked (a slaveholders' map drawn on a racial line). The investigators' role is to document and to find who gave the order — not to discover that the dead were anything but people.

Parsley — the shibboleth and the myth — 1937 and after

The price of the dead — Vincent's silence and the $525,000 — 15 October 1937–31 January 1938

  • Where: Port-au-Prince; Washington, D.C.
  • What happened: Haiti's president Sténio Vincent, who had financial ties to Trujillo, first forbade discussion of the killings and on 15 October 1937 said relations "have not suffered any damage". Trujillo then "Dominicanised" the border with barracks, schools, churches and roads, and quotas on Haitian cane workers.

  • People: Sténio Vincent · Franklin D. Roosevelt · Trujillo.

  • Divergence check: BENDS — with both halves of the island occupied, the "reparations" would be one American administration paying another, or no one paying at all.

Évian and Sosúa — Trujillo's offer of 100,000 refugee visas — July 1938–1940

  • Where: Évian-les-Bains (the conference, July 1938); Sosúa, on the north coast near Puerto Plata.
  • What happened: At the Évian Conference on Jewish refugees (July 1938), the Dominican Republic was the only country to offer to take large numbers — up to 100,000.   • People: Trujillo · the Sosúa settlers.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafael_Trujillo ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sos%C3%BAa

Part F — Folklore of Hispaniola (label: folklore, with first attestations)

The ciguapa — first written 1866

  • Where: the mountains of the Cibao, caves, grottoes and riverbanks; Dominican folklore.
  • What happened: The ciguapa is a small, naked woman of the mountain forests with knee-length hair and, in later versions, feet that point backwards — so her tracks lead the wrong way. Nocturnal and impossibly agile, she lures men who wander the woods at night. The first known written account is Francisco Javier Angulo Guridi's "La Ciguapa" (1866), where she is a beautiful Ciguayo (Taíno-region) woman about three feet tall with golden skin; the backwards feet and blue or dark skin came later. Scholars debate whether it preserves Taíno or maroon tradition or is 19th-century "fakelore"; the Trujillo regime used it for its mestizo (anti-African) ideology.
  • The odd detail: the thing that makes the ciguapa uncatchable — backwards feet — is not in her first attestation.
  • People: Francisco Javier Angulo Guridi — 19th-century Dominican writer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciguapa
  • Confidence: High for the 1866 attestation. Folklore.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (folklore), holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Tracks that lead away from where she went — a mountain-patrol encounter for a Marine or Guardia squad that files it as "bandits".

The bacá — a pact spirit that makes its owner rich

  • Where: all Hispaniola; strongest in the Dominican south; baka in Haitian Kreyòl.
  • What happened: In Dominican folklore the bacá is a spirit made by a pact with the devil: it takes the form of a large black dog or bull with burning red eyes (also cat, pig, chicken), brings its owner wealth and protection, and demands souls — a firstborn, any child, the owner's own, or whoever crosses its land after midnight. It cannot go out in daylight; it passes to the owner's eldest son on his death; removing it takes a priest, by day. Historian Robin Derby (UCLA) notes that in Haiti bakas are "sent spirits" of sorcery, and that in both countries they are about money — bacá stories encode who gets rich and how; she links them to Hispaniola's old cattle frontier and has recorded borderland memories of 1937.
  • The odd detail: according to one account (via search summary), the only animals a bacá becomes are the ones Columbus brought — dogs, pigs, cattle, horses.
  • People: Robin Derby — historian of the bacá.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bac%C3%A1_(mythological_creature) ; https://www.international.ucla.edu/lai/article/202890
  • Confidence: Folklore — no first-attestation date found this session (see Leads).
  • Divergence check: holds (folklore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The bacá is the Golden Circle's economy in a dog's shape — a pact for wealth paid in other people's lives. Best used for a planter or a colonial official, not for a Haitian cane-cutter.

Fukú and "zafa" — the curse of Columbus

  • Where: Dominican Republic (and diaspora).
  • What happened: A fukú (fucú) is a curse or bad omen; in Dominican belief Columbus's name carries one, and whoever says or hears it should cross their index fingers and whisper "¡Zafa!" to break it. The belief attaches itself to the Columbus Lighthouse — one account says a boulder fell on the officials' car at the 1948 ground-breaking (no one hurt). Junot Díaz made the word internationally known in 2007.
  • The odd detail: the "discoverer's" own name is the island's curse word.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://www.colonialzone-dr.com/important_stuff-superstitions.html ; search summaries (Book-It Repertory Theatre, DR1 forums) for the Columbus association and the 1948 boulder.
  • Confidence: Low on attestation — the belief is real and well known, but no pre-1940 written attestation was found this session; the 1948 anecdote is second-hand.

The galipote — a shapeshifter of San Juan de la Maguana

  • Where: San Juan de la Maguana and the southwest (the same valley as Papá Liborio).
  • What happened: In Dominican folklore a galipote is a person who, by a pact with the devil, can become an animal or even a tree or a stone, has terrible strength and is immune to firearms, and was said to drink children's blood at night. It can only be killed with a stake of palo de cruz cut on Good Friday. Variants: the lugarú (from French loup-garou), which becomes only a dog; the zancú, which becomes invisible. The legend probably dates to the 17th–18th centuries; travellers in remote areas still carry amulets or say a prayer.

Part G — Divergence questions for the owner (don't settle — note)

  1. Haiti after 1934. Already ruled: no withdrawal (Audit 151). Open: does Haiti keep a client president (Vincent from 1930, as recorded) under a permanent High Commissioner, or is it governed directly? Péralte's 1934/35 state funeral and the "second independence" do not happen either way.
  2. The Dominican Republic after 1924. The 1922 Hughes–Peynado plan, Vásquez's 1924 election and the September 1924 withdrawal never happen. Is the DR still under a naval military government in the 1930s (the real 1916–24 form), or a client regime?
  3. Trujillo. Three shapes: (a) a Guardia chief who never gets higher; (b) a strongman the Americans install to cut costs (closest to the real outcome, arrived at differently); (c) a mutineer who takes the Guardia from them. Each changes 1930 (the coup), 1936 (Ciudad Trujillo) and 1937.
  4. The October 1937 massacre. With both halves of the island American, the border is internal. Never happens / happens under American responsibility / happens under a Trujillo who has seized the Guardia. This is the lane's heaviest call and deserves the owner's own word rather than a default.
  5. The German colony in Haiti. Real history interned it (1918) because America went to war with Germany; in the setting it is never interned. Does a victorious Germany protect its merchants in Port-au-Prince (a Lüders affair in reverse), or is this where "Germany contests the hemisphere" (ALT.HIST §8.6) becomes concrete?
  6. The 1920 election and the Senate inquiry. Real: Harding campaigned against the occupation and the Republican Senate probed it (1921–22). Setting: Harding is a senator, Wood wins in 1920. Does Senator Harding still attack Haiti — and does a Wood administration (Wood governed Havana) hold the inquiry at all?
