M03 — Cuba, 1898–1940: Havana at the centre of the Golden Circle's map

How to read the divergence lines. Before 1917 everything happened as recorded. The setting's hinge for Cuba is the February–March 1917 Marine landing during the Chambelona revolt: in the record the Marines stayed in Oriente and Camagüey until 1922; in the setting they never leave. So every entry from 1917 on says HOLDS (the event still makes sense under a permanent occupation), BENDS (it happens, but differently) or CANNOT HAPPEN AS RECORDED (it depends on Cuba being sovereign, on the Great War, on the USSR/Comintern, or on a president the setting does not have). Note also that the setting's presidents are Marshall 1916–21 → Wood 1921–27 → Dawes 1927–29 → Hoover 1929–33 → FDR 1933–: Wood, the man who governed Havana 1899–1902, is president through the whole of the Crowder–Zayas–early-Machado period.

Overview (51 entries). The real record already has the setting's shape. Leonard Wood governed the island 1899–1902, cleaned Havana street by street (the fever gone by October 1901), licensed its first gambling hall over his own lawyer's objection, and wrote to Roosevelt that Platt left Cuba "little or no independence"; the setting only has to let the Marines of February 1917 stay. The Platt Amendment, Guantánamo ($2,000 a year in gold) and the Isle of Pines (an American colony waiting 21 years for a Senate vote — in the setting, Wood's Senate) are the legal bones. The Republic's violence is the lane's heaviest material and needs the most care: the 1904 "niña Zoila" brujería case, the 1912 massacre of the Partido Independiente de Color (3,000–6,000 Afro-Cubans killed while Marines guarded the mills), and the 1919 Matanzas/Regla lynchings — the period's "witch" panic, marked here as the period's. The Dance of the Millions (sugar 22.5¢ → under 3¢, twenty banks gone, "Pote" hanged) is the Depression's rehearsal as ALTERNATE_HISTORY §16.3 already says — and in a German-won war its crash could be a German decision. Machado's decade supplies a cellular secret society (the ABC, seven-man lettered cells) that tunnelled under the Colón Cemetery with 200 lb of dynamite, a death squad (the Porra) hunted through the streets in August 1933, a sergeant (Batista) raised by a coup, and a hotel built on a legendary cave shelled with 300 officers inside. The religion and folklore entries (Ocha, Palo, Abakuá, Ortiz, the güije, the Madre de Aguas, La Milagrosa) are written to keep living religions the population, never the cult. The biggest single find: Cuba's patroness, Our Lady of Charity, was declared in May 1916 and crowned in Santiago on 20 December 1936 — the week of the alpha's solstice and eclipse, three days after the San Lázaro pilgrimage. The biggest divergence finds: Coolidge's 1928 Havana visit cannot happen (it would be President Dawes); Cuba's 7 April 1917 war on Germany never happens (already ruled, §30.7), so the occupation loses its record-cover-story; a Japanese warship stood off Havana in September 1933; and the Comintern suspects in Mella's murder do not exist.

Part A — 1898 and the first occupation (happened as recorded)

The Maine explodes in Havana harbour — 15 February 1898, 9:40 p.m.

  • Where: Havana harbour, moored off the city; the wreck lay in the harbour for thirteen years.
  • What happened: The armoured cruiser USS Maine, sent to Havana in January 1898 to "protect American interests" during the Cuban war of independence, blew up at 21:40; more than five tons of powder in her forward magazines detonated and destroyed the forward third of the ship. 261 of the 355 aboard died. The US Navy's Sampson Board ruled a submarine mine set off the magazines; Spain's own inquiry (Del Peral and De Salas) said spontaneous combustion in a coal bunker beside the magazines. "Remember the Maine" carried the country into war that April. In December 1910 the Army Corps of Engineers built a cofferdam round the wreck and pumped it dry; by June 1911 the main deck was exposed. 66 sets of remains were recovered during the salvage and only one was identified. On 16 March 1912 the refloated after-section was towed out and scuttled in 3,600 feet of water in the Florida Straits. In 1976 Admiral Rickover's study concluded the bunker fire was the likelier cause.
  • The odd detail: for thirteen years the city lived with the wreck's upperworks standing out of its harbour; then the Americans built a wall of steel round it, drained the water away, walked on the bottom among the dead, and took the ship out to sea to sink her a second time, with flowers.
  • People: Capt. Charles Sigsbee — the Maine's captain, survived; Adm. William T. Sampson — presided over the US inquiry; Adm. Hyman Rickover — the 1976 re-examination.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Maine_(1889)
  • Confidence: High (death toll, times, dates, salvage figures from the page; the "with flowers" is period colour — UNVERIFIED in what I read, treat as a flourish).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): three layers exactly as the record already has them — the American board says mine, the Spanish board says coal, and the files say something in the harbour. The Golden Circle wanted a war that opened Havana; it got one on a single night. The 65 unidentified dead raised in 1911 are a gift: whose were they, really?

San Juan Hill and Kettle Hill — 1 July 1898

  • Where: the San Juan Heights east of Santiago de Cuba, Oriente province.
  • What happened: The American assault on the heights guarding Santiago. Col. Leonard Wood commanded the 2nd Brigade of the Cavalry Division, which included the 1st Volunteer Cavalry — the Rough Riders — whom Theodore Roosevelt led up Kettle Hill. The 10th Cavalry (Buffalo Soldiers) went up the same slope alongside them; Lt. John J. Pershing, with the 10th, wrote that "white regiments, black regiments, regulars and Rough Riders" fought shoulder to shoulder. Troops waiting under fire at the San Juan River named their positions "Hell's Pocket" and "Bloody Ford". Lt. John Parker's four Gatling guns fired about 18,000 rounds. The Spanish commander, Gen. Arsenio Linares, held the heights with only 760 regulars while keeping some 10,000 in reserve in Santiago. US losses: 144 killed, 1,024 wounded, 72 missing; Spanish: 58 killed, 366 wounded.
  • The odd detail: a Signal Corps observation balloon was towed forward over the column and drew the Spanish fire straight down onto the men beneath it.
  • People: Leonard Wood — brigade commander, later governor of Cuba and (in the setting) president; Theodore Roosevelt — the Rough Riders' lieutenant-colonel; John J. Pershing — lieutenant, 10th Cavalry (the setting's Mexico general); Arsenio Linares — Spanish commander.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_San_Juan_Hill
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. It is the founding day for three of the setting's men at once: Roosevelt (Hunter Guild founder), Wood (president 1921–27) and Pershing (the Southern War).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Hunter Guild's prehistory — Roosevelt and Wood first saw the Caribbean's "other side" in the fever camps after Santiago, not in the Amazon. A Rough Rider veteran in 1930s Chicago who won't talk about what the 10th Cavalry men saw in the brush at Hell's Pocket.

Columbus's bones leave Havana Cathedral — 26 September 1898 to 20 January 1899

  • Where: Havana Cathedral (Catedral de la Purísima Concepción, Plaza de la Catedral, Old Havana) → Seville.
  • What happened: The remains believed to be Christopher Columbus's had been brought from Santo Domingo in December 1795 when Spain ceded that half of Hispaniola to France, and lay in Havana's coral-stone Baroque cathedral (built by the Jesuits 1748–77, consecrated 1782) from 1796. When Spain lost Cuba, the box was taken out of its niche on 26 September 1898, loaded aboard the cruiser Conde de Venadito on 13 December 1898 [both 1898 dates UNVERIFIED — V_M03: not on the Wikipedia pages read, which give the 20 December 1795 exhumation at Santo Domingo, the 1795–96 move to Havana and "1899" for Seville], and reached Seville in January 1899; the Seville tomb (from 1902) says, in effect, that when Cuba "emancipated from Mother Spain" Seville received the deposit of Columbus's remains. Santo Domingo, for its part, had announced in 1877 the discovery of a lead box inscribed with Columbus's name and has always claimed the real bones never left. In October 2024 University of Granada researchers said DNA confirms the Seville bones are his.
  • The odd detail: the empire left Havana the way a family leaves a house it has sold — taking its dead with it — three months before the Americans formally took over on 1 January 1899.
  • People: Christopher Columbus — the disputed dead; Archbishop Fernando Portillo y Torres — arranged the 1795 removal from Santo Domingo.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Christopher_Columbus ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havana_Cathedral ; image: Library of Congress, "The cathedral, Havana (W.) from which Columbus' remains were removed to Spain 1898", https://loc.gov/pictures/item/2019635263/ (LOC; check rights line before use).
  • Confidence: High for the dates of the transfer (26 Sep / 13 Dec 1898 via search summary of the latinamericanstudies.org page); the 1877 Santo Domingo claim is standard but I did not read its detail this session — Medium.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): two sets of Columbus's bones in the Caribbean, and an empty niche in Havana's cathedral since 1898 — the IHS would want to know what the Spanish took so carefully out of the centre of the Golden Circle's map on the eve of the American flag, and what, if anything, they left in its place.

Leonard Wood, military governor of Cuba — 23 December 1899 – 20 May 1902

  • Where: Havana (Palacio de los Capitanes Generales, Plaza de Armas); earlier Santiago de Cuba (military governor from 1898).
  • What happened: After Santiago, Wood governed the city and province, then the whole island from 23 December 1899 to 20 May 1902, when the flag came down and Tomás Estrada Palma took office. An Army surgeon by training, he drove the sanitation of Havana, rebuilt schools, courts and prisons, and suggested the strategy that became the Platt Amendment. In a 1900 report he wrote that Cuban stability meant conditions where "money can be borrowed at a reasonable rate of interest and when capital is willing to invest in the island". In a confidential letter to Roosevelt he wrote: "There is, of course, little or no independence left Cuba under the Platt Amendment", and that the consistent thing now was to seek annexation. On 15 May 1902, five days before handing over, he issued an order excluding Chinese immigrants.
  • The odd detail: the man who in the setting becomes president (1921–27) had already written, in his own hand, that Cuba's independence was a fiction and annexation the logical end — the setting's permanent occupation is simply Wood's 1901 letter come true.
  • People: Leonard Wood — governor, later (setting) president; Tomás Estrada Palma — first president of Cuba, 1902; Elihu Root — Secretary of War, Wood's superior.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Wood ; the Wood-to-Roosevelt quotation via search summary of https://www.theodorerooseveltcenter.org/encyclopedia/foreign-affairs/platt-amendment/ and https://read.dukeupress.edu/hahr/article/53/1/133/151583/Leonard-Wood-and-Cuban-Independence-1898-1902 (Healy, HAHR 1973).
  • Confidence: High for dates and the 1900 report; the letter quotation is well known — High; its exact date (usually given as October 1901) is UNVERIFIED here ⟨V_M03: the quotation is confirmed on peacehistory-usfp.org (reproduced there as "little or no independence left in Cuba under the Platt Amendment" — wording varies between reproductions); the Theodore Roosevelt Center page named above does NOT contain the letter; addressee Roosevelt is from a search summary; the date was not found⟩.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. In the setting this is the résumé line that makes Wood president; PEOPLE_OF_THE_TIMELINE.md already carries it.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): President Wood (1921–27) is the one man in Washington who knows Havana's ground personally — which streets, which cellars, which cemetery vaults. A Section 13 or Secret Service file on what Governor Wood found under the city during the 1901 cleanup and ordered sealed.

The Yellow Fever Commission and Camp Lazear — 1900–1901

  • Where: Camp Columbia at Quemados (Marianao, west of Havana); "Camp Lazear" nearby; Las Animas Hospital, Havana.
  • What happened: Major Walter Reed's Army board — James Carroll, Jesse Lazear and the Cuban-born Aristides Agramonte — tested the theory of the Havana physician Carlos Finlay that a mosquito carried yellow fever. Lazear, the only mosquito expert on the team, worked with larvae from Finlay's own laboratory. He wrote to his wife on 8 September 1900, "I rather think I am on the track of the real germ", and died of yellow fever on 25 September 1900, aged 34. It was given out that he had been bitten by accident; in 1947 Philip Hench found in Lazear's own notebook that he had infected himself deliberately — the cover-up at the time may have concerned family insurance. The camp was named for him in November 1900. Volunteers — soldiers, and the investigators themselves — let infected mosquitoes bite them, and others slept in bedding soiled by the dying, proving the disease was not carried on clothes and linen.
  • The odd detail: a small house in the camp where men slept for nights on the sheets and pyjamas of yellow-fever corpses, on purpose, to prove the corpses' linen was harmless.
  • People: Walter Reed — head of the board; Jesse Lazear — died of his own experiment; James Carroll — survived a deliberate infection; Aristides Agramonte — Cuban-born member; Carlos Finlay — Havana physician whose theory it was.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesse_William_Lazear ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Reed
  • Confidence: High (Lazear dates, notebook finding); the "infected-bedding house" is standard in the record but only summarised on the Reed page I read — Medium on its details.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a notebook that told the truth forty-seven years late. What else is in the Commission's papers — a man who volunteered, was bitten, did not sicken, and was sent home with his $100?

Clara Maass — dies of the experiment, Havana, 24 August 1901

  • Where: Las Animas Hospital, Havana; buried first in the Colón Cemetery.
  • What happened: Maass, a 25-year-old contract nurse from New Jersey, volunteered for the continuing yellow-fever inoculation experiments in Havana (run after Reed's board by the Cuban physician Juan Guiteras, not named on the page I read — UNVERIFIED). Volunteers were paid $100, and another $100 if they fell ill. She was bitten by infected mosquitoes repeatedly between March and August 1901 and died on 24 August 1901. Her mother called it "little short of murder." Her death ended human yellow-fever experiments. She was buried in the Colón Cemetery with military honours and moved to Fairmount Cemetery, Newark, on 20 February 1902.
  • The odd detail: the price of a human trial in American Havana was a hundred dollars, doubled if the fever took.
  • People: Clara Maass — nurse, the only American woman to die in the programme; Juan Guiteras — directed the 1901 inoculations (UNVERIFIED on the page read).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clara_Maass
  • Confidence: High (dates, payment, burials).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): her empty first grave in the Colón Cemetery; a nurse's ghost at Las Animas ("the souls") hospital — the name alone does the work. Keep her a victim, not a monster.

The cleanup of Havana: yellow fever driven out — 1901

  • Where: Havana, divided into 20 sanitary districts.
  • What happened: Acting on the Commission's result, Major William C. Gorgas, Havana's chief sanitary officer under Wood, formed a "Stegomyia brigade" and an "Anopheles brigade". Inspectors went house to house hunting stagnant water; householders were fined when larvae were found on their premises [UNVERIFIED — V_M03: fines and the two "brigades" are not on the EBSCO or Lind pages read]; water was treated, cisterns screened, sick houses fumigated and quarantined. October 1901 was the first October in Havana's recorded history without a case [UNVERIFIED — V_M03 as worded; consistent with the 28 September last death, but no page read says it]. Gorgas took the method to the Panama Canal.
  • The odd detail: an occupying army with the right to enter every house in the city and search it — for water. The first great American intrusion into Havana's private rooms was a mosquito hunt.
  • People: William C. Gorgas — sanitary officer, later Surgeon General; Leonard Wood — the governor who backed him.
  • Sources: via search: https://www.jameslindlibrary.org/articles/dramatic-effects-of-control-measures-on-deaths-from-yellow-fever-in-havana-cuba-in-the-early-1900s/ ; https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/gorgas-develops-effective-methods-mosquito-control
  • Confidence: High (figures consistent across sources; via search summaries).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Wood's 1901 house-by-house inspection registers are the finest map of 1901 Havana's cellars, cisterns and back rooms ever made — exactly the document an investigator (or the Golden Circle) would want in the 1930s.

The Platt Amendment and the Guantánamo lease — 1901–1903

  • Where: Washington; Havana (Constitutional Convention); Guantánamo Bay, Oriente.
  • What happened: Written into the Army Appropriations Act and signed by McKinley on 2 March 1901 (Senate 43–20, House 161–136), the amendment set eight conditions for withdrawing US troops. Article III gave the US the right to intervene for "the preservation of Cuban independence" and "the protection of life, property, and individual liberty"; Article VI left the Isle of Pines out of Cuba's boundaries pending a treaty; Article VII obliged Cuba to sell or lease land for coaling or naval stations. Cuba's Constitutional Assembly accepted it on 12 June 1901 by 16 to 11 (four abstaining), and it went into the 1901 Constitution. The lease of Guantánamo Bay (about 45 square miles of land and water) followed on 23 February 1903, "complete jurisdiction and control" to the US and "ultimate sovereignty" to Cuba; a July 1903 agreement set the rent at $2,000 a year in US gold coin. Invoked for the occupations of 1906–09, 1912 and 1917–22. Abrogated 29 May 1934 under FDR — except Guantánamo, which the 1934 treaty made effectively perpetual.
  • The odd detail: the rent — two thousand dollars in gold coin, a year, for a naval base; the kind of sum a Chicago alderman spent on a wedding.
  • People: Orville Platt — the senator; Elihu Root — its drafter in fact; Tomás Estrada Palma — signed the lease.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platt_Amendment ; via search: https://blog.gitmomemory.org/2012/02/24/this-week-in-guantanamo-1903-and-2012/
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 happened. BENDS after 1916: the 1934 abrogation is FDR's Good Neighbor act; in a Cuba where the Marines never left, the Platt Amendment is either never repealed or repealed on paper only. Owner's word needed — ALTERNATE_HISTORY §8.2 cites Platt as 1901–1934, which is the real span.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Article III" as a phrase every Cuban in the setting knows the way a Chicagoan knows "the Volstead Act". Guantánamo as the Navy's own secret ground inside the occupation — a place the Army's garrison cannot enter.

The Isle of Pines — "America's forgotten colony", 1901–1925

  • Where: Isla de Pinos (now Isla de la Juventud), south of Havana province; towns Nueva Gerona, Santa Fe, Columbia, the McKinley colony.
  • What happened: Because Article VI left the island's status open, American land companies sold it to settlers as future US territory. At the peak in the mid-1910s some 2,000 Americans — citrus growers, businessmen, teachers, missionaries — lived there, built churches and English-language schools and shipped grapefruit and oranges to New Orleans; Columbia was the first American town, and the McKinley colony was sold on the false claim that McKinley had meant to annex the island. The Hay–Quesada Treaty recognising Cuban sovereignty was signed 2 March 1904 and ratified by the US Senate only on 13 March 1925; by 1924 about 700 of the island's 4,250 residents were US citizens and owned about 90% of the land. Settlers told President Coolidge that ceding the island would be "a blot on American history". Hurricanes and business failures thinned them; fewer than 300 remained by the 1930s. Cubans called the island Isla de Tesoros — legend says pirates buried treasure in its pine forests.
  • The odd detail: an American small town — Methodist church, schoolhouse, grapefruit groves — living for twenty-one years on the island that also held the old pirate-treasure legends, waiting for a Senate vote that finally said no.
  • People: Sen. Boies Penrose — argued settlers had been promised US sovereignty by the military governor (Wood); Gonzalo de Quesada — Cuban minister who signed.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hay–Quesada_Treaty ; https://www.historynewsnetwork.org/article/americas-forgotten-cuban-colony (Neagle)
  • Confidence: High for dates and numbers; the treasure legend is folklore (via search; first attestation not established).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 happened. BENDS: the 1925 ratification happens under President Wood in the setting (Coolidge was never president). Owner's word needed: does Wood — whose own military government is said to have promised the settlers sovereignty — let the treaty through, or does the Isle of Pines stay American? Either is a hook.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Isle as the occupation's quiet back room: American settlers, pirate-treasure rumours, and (from 1926) Machado's panopticon prison (see Part D). A settler's daughter who went into the pines in 1917 and came back with Spanish gold and no memory.

The Frontón Jai Alai, "the Palace of Screams" — opened 7 May 1901

  • Where: corner of Concordia and Lucena, Centro Habana.
  • What happened: The first jai-alai fronton in the New World opened on 7 May 1901, built by the Basque Mazzatini brothers. The US Army's legal adviser, Col. Edward S. Dudley, turned down their application three times and complained to Washington; the fronton operated without a licence with the support of Gen. Wood, and a ten-year contract was only signed in 1904. The concession became a stick opponents used against Wood. The building was known across the island as "El Palacio de los Gritos" — the Palace of Screams — for the betting crowd, and to the players as "El Matadero", the slaughterhouse. By 1920 frontons ran island-wide; Hemingway and Babe Ruth are said to have bet there.
  • The odd detail: the Americans came to clean Havana up, and the military governor's personal intervention licensed its first great gambling hall.
  • People: the Mazzatini brothers — Basque promoters; Col. Edward S. Dudley — Army legal adviser who objected; Leonard Wood — who overrode him.
  • Sources: https://abandonedfl.com/fronton-jai-alai/
  • Confidence: Medium (a local-history site; the Dudley complaint and Wood's backing are corroborated in the search results pointing to Hitchman and Pruitt's Wood studies, not read in full).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a gambling palace whose licence exists only as Wood's say-so — in a setting where Wood is president, that favour is a debt someone can still call. The "Palace of Screams" is a ready-made haunted venue name.

The Second Occupation — Taft, then Magoon — 29 September 1906 – February 1909

  • Where: Havana; posts across the island.
  • What happened: Estrada Palma's party rigged the 1905 election against the Liberal José Miguel Gómez; the Liberals rose in the "Little August War" of 1906 and both sides asked for American intervention. Roosevelt sent William H. Taft and Robert Bacon (arrived 19 September); Estrada Palma resigned on 28 September and the next day Taft proclaimed himself provisional governor, to rule "only long enough to restore order". Charles E. Magoon took over on 13 October 1906. The Army of Cuban Pacification — about 425 officers and 6,196 men — built 92 km of roads and manned thirty posts. Gómez won the supervised election of 14 November 1908 and took office on 28 January 1909; the last US troops left by 6 February 1909. Cuban memory made Magoon a byword for graft and the botella (the sinecure "bottle" handed to a political client) — a charge American historians have disputed (UNVERIFIED on the page read).
  • The odd detail: a sovereign republic's president resigns and a US cabinet secretary, standing in Havana, simply names himself governor the next morning — using the treaty clause the Cubans had voted in by 16 to 11.
  • People: William H. Taft — Secretary of War, provisional governor; Charles E. Magoon — governor 1906–09; Tomás Estrada Palma — resigned; José Miguel Gómez — Liberal, president 1909–13.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Occupation_of_Cuba
  • Confidence: High (the Magoon-corruption reputation is Medium).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened — the setting's own precedent for "an occupation that outlives its justification" (§8.2).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the 1906 model is the one the setting's permanent occupation keeps pretending to be — "only long enough to restore order". Every 1930s occupation proclamation quotes Taft.

Part B — Race, fear and the Republic's violence (1904–1919)

Respect note for this Part. The victims here are Afro-Cubans killed by the state and by mobs; the "witches" of the period's newspapers were elderly men of Lucumí and Kongo cabildos (mutual-aid societies) and practitioners of living religions. The phrases "negros brujos", "brujería", "race war" and "ferocious savagery" below are the period's own words, marked as such. The "niña Zoila" case — the first great brujería panic — November 1904

  • Where: a farm (the Finca Zúñiga) near Güira de Melena, Havana province; the Congo Real cabildo of the nearby village of Gabriel.
  • What happened: On 11 November 1904 a white child of about twenty-two months, Zoila Díaz, disappeared from her parents' farm. Her body was later found; the press reported her heart had been removed. Rumour at once blamed "witches" of the Congo Real cabildo; on confidential information to Güira's mayor and weak evidence, police arrested the elderly Lucumí man Domingo Bocourt and two labourers of about seventy, Julián Amaro and José Cárdenas. The prosecution's story — carried at length by La Discusión and La Lucha (UNVERIFIED on a page read) — was that Bocourt needed "a white child's blood" to cure a woman, Juana Tabares, who had lost six of nine children in slavery [UNVERIFIED — V_M03: the Aisthesis 46 article says instead that Bocourt was accused of ordering the killing to cure two Black girls, Adela and Juana; "Juana Tabares" and the "six of nine children" were not found]. The Partido Independiente de Color and the Morúa Law — 1908–1910

  • Where: Havana; the party's strength was in Oriente and among veterans of the independence war.
  • What happened: In August 1908 veterans of the Liberation Army led by Evaristo Estenoz and Pedro Ivonnet founded the Partido Independiente de Color (PIC), the first Black political party in the hemisphere, demanding Afro-Cubans' "rightful share" of the republic they had fought for (Afro-Cubans had been a large part of the insurgent army). In 1910 the Senate passed the Morúa Amendment — sponsored by the Afro-Cuban senator Martín Morúa Delgado — banning political parties organised by race; President José Miguel Gómez used it to outlaw the PIC, and its leaders were arrested in 1910.
  • The odd detail: the law that outlawed the Black party was written by a Black senator — the Republic's line was that a race party was itself the racism.
  • People: Evaristo Estenoz — PIC founder; Pedro Ivonnet — co-leader, a veteran officer; Martín Morúa Delgado — senator; José Miguel Gómez — president 1909–13.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negro_Rebellion (redirects to "War of 1912"); via search: https://www.afrocubaweb.com/history/eldoce.htm
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a veteran's organisation with its own oaths, outlawed and driven underground — in 1930s Havana, surviving members are a population with every reason to keep records the occupation doesn't have.

The massacre of 1912 ("the Race War", La Guerrita de 1912) — 20 May – July 1912

  • Where: Oriente province — La Maya, Guantánamo, the mountains of the east; Marines at Guantánamo, Daiquirí and the sugar and mining properties.
  • What happened: On 20 May 1912 — the tenth anniversary of the Republic — the PIC rose in armed protest to force the repeal of the Morúa Law. President Gómez asked Washington for help on 23 May; the first Marine reinforcements (32 officers, 777 men) arrived on 28 May, and 1,292 more landed at Guantánamo on 7 June [the 1,292 figure UNVERIFIED — V_M03; Wikipedia says only "most of the regiment" reached Guantánamo on 7 June and one battalion landed at Havana on 10 June], guarding American sugar estates, mines and railways and freeing the Cuban army to hunt. Gómez's government spoke of "civilization" against "ferocious savagery"; one newspaper proposed that Cuba adopt Jim Crow laws. The army, rural guard and white volunteers killed between 3,000 and 6,000 Afro-Cubans, combatants and non-combatants; the historian Aline Helg records that "entire families were machine-gunned in their bohíos". Estenoz was shot at point-blank range on 27 June; Ivonnet surrendered on 18 July and was killed the same day "while trying to escape". The last Marines left on 2 August. The party was destroyed.
  • The odd detail: the rising's date — 20 May, the Republic's own birthday — and the phrase "shot while trying to escape" (la ley de fuga), which becomes the standard Cuban euphemism for a police killing through the Machado years.
  • People: Evaristo Estenoz — killed 27 June; Pedro Ivonnet — killed 18 July; José Miguel Gómez — president; Gen. José de Jesús Monteagudo — commanded the army's campaign (named in the scholarship; not on the pages read — UNVERIFIED); Aline Helg — historian, Our Rightful Share (1995).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negro_Rebellion ; via search: https://www.afrocubaweb.com/history/eldoce.htm ; http://www.historyofcuba.com/history/race/RaceWar1.htm
  • Confidence: High for dates and the range of the death toll; the "machine-gunned families" quotation via search summary of Helg.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. It is the second use of the Platt Amendment by Marines.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Oriente is where the setting's never-leaving Marines sit after 1917 (next Part) — on ground where, five years earlier, Marines guarded the mills while thousands of Black Cubans were hunted in the hills. The dead of 1912 are the region's ghosts, and they are not the monsters: a hook where the haunting is the evidence, and the thing hunting the investigators is whoever wants 1912 to stay unwritten.

The 1919 panic: Cecilia of Matanzas and the Regla lynching — June 1919

  • Where: the city of Matanzas; Regla, across Havana harbour.
  • What happened: In June 1919 a small girl, Cecilia (named in Cuban accounts as Cecilia Dalcourt), disappeared in Matanzas. The press called her white — though she was described as light-skinned and of mixed descent — and her disappearance was at once blamed on Black male "witches" said to have killed her and eaten her entrails. Eight alleged brujos were arrested, held in solitary and tortured; one "confessed" and a body presumed to be hers was dug up [UNVERIFIED — V_M03: the eight arrests, torture and "confession" were not found on any page read]. At the end of June 1919 the violence in Matanzas left seven dead and several wounded; at the same time an alleged attempted abduction of a girl in Regla also ended in a lynching of the accused. It came in a year of "militant Negrophobia", as Haitian and Jamaican cane-cutters arrived in large numbers and the press folded Vodou and Myal into Cuban brujería. The girl's fate remains a mystery.

La Chambelona and the "Sugar Intervention" — February 1917 – 15 February 1922

  • Where: Oriente and Camagüey provinces (Santiago, Guantánamo, the eastern sugar zone); Caicaje (Las Villas), where the rebellion ended.
  • What happened: President Mario García Menocal's disputed re-election in November 1916 set off a Liberal revolt in February 1917 known by its campaign song, "La Chambelona". The gunboat USS Paducah landed men on 12 February 1917; by March the Liberal forces were broken and their leader, ex-president José Miguel Gómez, captured after the fight at Caicaje. Cuba declared war on Germany on 7 April 1917, the day after the United States. Officially it was support for a wartime ally; in fact, protection of American sugar. Cuba produced a record harvest in 1918. The last Marines left on 15 February 1922.

  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_Intervention

On the afternoon of 27 or 28 September 1932 ⟨fixed: V_M03 — the sources split: Wikipedia (EN and ES) and Cuba Memorias give 28 September; OnCuba and Fotos de La Habana date the attack Tuesday 27 September⟩ ABC gunmen machine-gunned the limousine of Senate President Clemente Vázquez Bello, Machado's likely successor; he died on the operating table at the Camp Columbia hospital (Wikipedia; ⟨fixed: V_M03 — Fotos de La Habana says instant death, "no fewer than ten bullets" to the neck⟩). The same day several opposition figures, including members of the Freyre de Andrade family, were murdered — reprisals, apparently on Machado's order.

  • People: Joaquín Martínez Sáenz — ABC's leader; Jorge Mañach, Francisco Ichaso — writers, founders; Clemente Vázquez Bello — Senate president, killed; the Freyre de Andrade brothers — killed in reprisal.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_(Cuba) ; via search: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clemente_Vazquez_Bello ; https://oncubanews.com/cuba/sociedad-cuba/historia/el-atentado-a-vazquez-bello/

And the tunnel under the Colón Cemetery: what else did the diggers break into? See the Colón Cemetery entry (Part F).

The fall of Machado and the hunt for the porristas — 27 July – 12 August 1933

  • Where: Havana — General Machado Airport; the Presidential Palace; the Prado; the homes of Machado's men.
  • What happened: A bus drivers' strike that began on 27 July 1933 grew into a general strike; Sumner Welles pressed Machado to go and the army turned. On 12 August Machado flew out at 3:32 p.m. from the airport named after him, in a chartered amphibian with four aides, landed overnight on Andros and went on to Nassau (reportedly with a £500,000 deposit at the Bank of England); his wife Elvira escaped by gunboat to Key West. Machado died in Miami Beach in 1939.

The Sergeants' Revolt — Batista takes the army — 4–5 September 1933

  • Where: Camp Columbia, Marianao (the same ground as Reed's 1900 yellow-fever camp at Quemados).
  • What happened: NCOs meeting at Columbia from late August over pay and promotions turned, on the night of 4 September 1933, into a coup led by a 32-year-old stenographer-sergeant, Fulgencio Batista: "From this moment forward, do not obey anyone's orders but mine." Céspedes fell; a five-man Pentarchy lasted five days; then Ramón Grau San Martín led the "Hundred Days" government, which Washington never recognised. Roosevelt sent some thirty warships into Cuban waters (another count: 23 vessels including eight destroyers); American — and Japanese — warships stood by but did not intervene. In January 1934 Grau fell under pressure from Batista and Welles's successor, Jefferson Caffery; Batista ruled through presidents (Mendieta and others) until he was elected himself in 1940.
  • The odd detail: the coup that made Batista was plotted on the ground where, thirty-three years earlier, American volunteers let mosquitoes bite them for $100.
  • People: Fulgencio Batista — sergeant, then colonel and chief of staff; Ramón Grau San Martín — president Sept 1933 – Jan 1934; Sumner Welles; Jefferson Caffery — Welles's successor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Revolution_of_1933 ; via search: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_1933 ; https://uscs.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/DS27-U.S.-Warships-in-Cuban-Waters.pdf
  • Confidence: High for the dates and roles; warship counts vary by source (23 vs 30).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the setting's biggest Cuban question. A sergeants' coup inside a country with a permanent US garrison either (a) happens because the Americans allow it (Batista as the occupation's chosen strongman — historically, that is how Welles and Caffery came to see him), or (b) cannot happen at all. The Japanese warship in the record is striking in a setting where Japan leans German (§30.6): a Japanese cruiser in Havana harbour in 1933 is a German-bloc presence at the centre of the map.   • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Batista as a character who rises because the occupation wants a Cuban face — "no one is clean". The Japanese warship as the one foreign flag the occupation cannot order out.

The siege of the Hotel Nacional — 2 October 1933

  • Where: Hotel Nacional, on the Taganana hill above the Malecón, Vedado (opened 30 December 1930; McKim, Mead & White; built in fourteen months by Purdy & Henderson on the site of the old Santa Clara battery).

On 2 October 1933 Batista's soldiers attacked with rifles, machine guns and artillery; the officers surrendered after the shelling, and about 94 combatants were dead (about 80 soldiers and 14 officers), with the building scarred by shell and bullet holes. Some officers were murdered after surrendering (the Wikipedia battle article says so ⟨fixed: V_M03⟩). The result was that the old officer corps ceased to exist and Batista's sergeants became its colonels.

Atarés: the last stand of Blas Hernández — 8–9 November 1933

  • Where: Castillo de Atarés, a hexagonal hilltop fort at the head of Havana harbour (built 1763–67 by the engineer Agustín Crame).
  • What happened: In November 1933 the ABC and army dissidents rose against Grau and Batista. Part E — Afro-Cuban religion, and how the period saw it

Respect note. Regla de Ocha (Santería/Lucumí), Palo Monte and Abakuá are living religions and institutions with millions of adherents and long histories of persecution. The words "brujería", "ñañiguismo" and "negros brujos" are the period's hostile categories (and its criminal-code language), not descriptions. In the setting these traditions are not the villains; the brief's principle holds — "none of them 'evil magic' as a whole; finding out what you are dealing with is the investigation" (ALTERNATE_HISTORY §29.3).

Regla de Ocha / Lucumí ("Santería") — the orishas behind the saints

  • Where: Havana, Regla, Matanzas and across the island; worship in the casa-templo (ilé), the house of a santero or santera.
  • What happened: Formed in the nineteenth century from Yoruba (Lucumí) religion, Catholicism and Spiritism, carried by the Lucumí cabildos de nación. Its deities, the orishas, correspond to Catholic saints: Changó ↔ Santa Bárbara (red; lightning), Yemayá ↔ the Virgin of Regla (the sea, motherhood), Ochún ↔ Our Lady of Charity (rivers, love), Babalú Ayé ↔ Saint Lazarus (disease and healing), Elegguá ↔ the Holy Child of Atocha (the crossroads). Initiation is the asiento (kariocha); the babalawo reads Ifá divination. Despite the 1901 Constitution's religious freedom, the Euro-Cuban and Catholic establishment called it brujería. The name "Santería" came from 1930s scholarly writers before practitioners took it up (some find it pejorative); Rómulo Lachatañeré documented the tradition in ¡Oh mío Yemayá! (1938), and in 1936–37 Fernando Ortiz arranged an early public concert of sacred batá drums.
  • The odd detail: the religion's own name is a 1930s outsiders' coinage — in the decade the setting plays in, it was being named, drummed in public and prosecuted at the same time.
  • People: Rómulo Lachatañeré — Afro-Cuban ethnographer; Fernando Ortiz — anthropologist.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santería
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a santera as an ally who knows what the orishas' names are being borrowed for — the setting's cult hides behind saints, and she can tell the difference. The IHS and a babalawo comparing notes is a scene.

Palo Monte (Regla de Palo) — the nganga and the dead

  • Where: island-wide; munanso congo ("Kongo houses") led by a tata or yayi.
  • What happened: Palo draws on the Kongo religion of Central Africa (roughly 700,000 to a million enslaved Africans were brought to Cuba), with Catholic and Spiritist elements; its main branches are Mayombe, Monte, Briyumba and Kimbisa. Its centre is the nganga (prenda, "treasure") — an iron cauldron, clay pot or gourd holding sticks (palos) from particular trees, earth from particular places, water, stones and human remains, the nfumbe, whose spirit is believed to live in the vessel and work for the palero. An anthropologist quoted by Wikipedia: the nganga "is not an intermediary of the divine, it is the divine itself". The establishment treated Palo as brujería, and the period ranked it "darker" and more aggressive than Ocha.
  • The odd detail: the dead as a co-worker: the nfumbe agrees (the tradition holds) to serve — which is exactly why the period's newspapers, finding bones, cried murder.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palo_(religion)
  • Confidence: High (as a description of the tradition; I have not assigned period dates beyond "nineteenth–twentieth century").
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the natural ghost-and-necromancy expert of the setting's Havana — careful: the hook is a palero who knows which cemetery the stolen bones came from (Colón, Espada) and who stole them for something that is not Palo.

Abakuá — the ñáñigo society and the íremes — from 1836

  • Where: Regla (first lodge, 1836), then Havana, Matanzas and Cárdenas only.
  • What happened: Abakuá descends from the Ékpè leopard society of the Cross River region (Efik and Ejagham peoples), a men's fraternity that crossed ethnic lines for trade. Enslaved Efik men founded the first Cuban lodge (potencia or juego), Efik Butón, in Regla in 1836. It is men-only, bound by oaths of secrecy, with a complex hierarchy; members are abanékues (ecobios). By 1881 Havana alone had 77 lodges in nine districts. On 6 January (Three Kings' Day) its masked dancers, the íremes — checkerboard cloth suits and conical hoods, the "little devils" (diablitos) of Spanish onlookers — represent "the return of the dead to Earth". Spanish and republican law made ñañiguismo a crime category (the colonial deportations of ñáñigos to Fernando Póo and Ceuta are in the scholarship — UNVERIFIED on the page read).
  • The odd detail: a secret society with dock-workers' muscle in the port of Havana — the city's real sworn brotherhood, a century older than the ABC.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abakuá
  • Confidence: High for 1836, Regla, 1881's 77 lodges and the íremes.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): cult versus population in its purest form: a real secret society, sworn and male and old, which the period treats as criminal — and which, in the setting, may be the only organisation in Havana that has been watching the harbour's other secret society for a hundred years. Allies, not suspects.

Fernando Ortiz, from Los negros brujos (1906) to transculturation (1940)

  • Where: Havana — the university, the Sociedad Económica, the House of Representatives (Ortiz sat 1917–22 as a Liberal).
  • What happened: In 1906 the young lawyer Fernando Ortiz published Los negros brujos in the framework of criminal anthropology — the brujo as a "social type" to be studied and reformed rather than executed (Encyclopedia.com's summary). He later rejected that framing and spent his life documenting Afro-Cuban culture: the Sociedad de Folklore Cubano (1923 or 1928 — sources differ), the Sociedad de Estudios Afrocubanos (1937) with its journal Estudios Afrocubanos, the 1936–37 batá concert, and Contrapunteo cubano del tabaco y el azúcar (1940), which coined "transculturation". He began in Machado's Liberal Party and broke with it.
  • The odd detail: the man whose first book is the period's hostile framing wrote the book that made the framing obsolete — the same author on both sides of the setting's "three layers".
  • People: Fernando Ortiz — anthropologist, lawyer, politician.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernando_Ortiz_Fernández ; https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/negros-brujos
  • Confidence: High (the folklore society's founding year is inconsistent across sources).
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Ortiz as the ideal NPC expert for an investigator in 1930s Havana — and his 1906 police-file photographs of "brujos" (the scholarship discusses a visual archive) as the record a cult could use to find the old practitioners who know it.

The comparsas banned, and back on the Prado — c. 1913–1916 to 1937

  • Where: Havana's carnival, the Paseo del Prado.
  • What happened: The Afro-Cuban street comparsas (costumed carnival troupes with conga drums, historically tied to the colonial cabildos and Día de Reyes) were restricted and banned in the 1910s (1913–1916 in different accounts) as "Afro-Cuban manifestations". They were re-authorised in 1937 for the Prado celebrations — partly for the tourists — under a new organisation. The 1937 troupes had names a GM could use tomorrow: El Alacrán (the scorpion), Los Marqueses de Atarés, Los Componedores de Bateas, Los Chinos Buenos, Los Dandies, Las Boyeras, Los Guaracheros de Regla.
  • The odd detail: a troupe named for the "Marquises of Atarés" — the fort where men were shot after surrender four years before — dancing down the Prado.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://islandsofhistory.org/volume-2/the-comparsa-in-cuba/ ; via search: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_carnival
  • Confidence: Medium for the ban dates (1913/1914/1916 vary); High for the 1937 return and the names.
  • Divergence check: holds; in the setting the 1937 revival is also a gift to the occupation's tourists.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a 1937 carnival case: a masked dancer in El Alacrán's troupe who isn't in the troupe. The population is the crowd; the cult is one costume.

Our Lady of Charity of El Cobre — patroness in May 1916, crowned in December 1936

  • Where: the Sanctuary of El Cobre, in the copper-mining village west of Santiago de Cuba; Santiago's Eucharistic Congress (1936).
  • What happened: Legend (the account dates to c. 1612) says two Indigenous brothers and an enslaved African boy — "the three Juans" — found a small wooden statue floating in the Bay of Nipe on a board inscribed "Yo soy la Virgen de la Caridad". She became the Virgin of the enslaved copper miners of El Cobre (freed by Charles IV's decree of 1801). Veterans of the independence war petitioned Rome, and Benedict XV declared her patroness of Cuba on 10 May 1916. Pius XI granted her canonical coronation on 20 December 1936, during the Eucharistic Congress in Santiago. In Regla de Ocha she is Ochún. Part F — Folklore, the dead, and the old stones of Havana

Cuban Folk-Lore by an American in occupied Havana — 1908

  • Where: printed in Havana by the Avisador Comercial press.
  • What happened: L. Roy Terwilliger published Cuban Folk-Lore in 1908, during the Second Occupation — an American's catalogue of island beliefs. He writes that even educated whites "have a wholesome fear of the Brujo or witch doctor"; records the lodestone (piedra imán) in love and harm magic; a sickness "wrapped carefully in red cloth" and left in the public highway to pass to whoever picks it up; a "Mabruja or Evil Spirit" said to possess dancers and need driving out; and the belief that if no mass is said for a soul, the newly dead will come at night and pull the feet or carry off the blanket of the nearest relation.
  • The odd detail: the red-cloth bundle in the road — the perfect found object to begin a scenario.
  • People: L. Roy Terwilliger — American author (otherwise unknown to me).
  • Sources: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/51040/51040-h/51040-h.htm
  • Confidence: High that the book says this; it is period framing — an outsider's list, not an ethnography.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a ready period "handbook" an American investigator in the setting would actually carry — and get wrong. Good for the "three layers": Terwilliger is layer one.

The güije (jigüe) — the river imp — attested 1836; a 1934 poem

  • Where: rivers and lagoons island-wide; famous local ones such as the güije of the Yayabo (Sancti Spíritus) and sightings in Camagüey.
  • What happened: Folklore. The güije is a small, hairy, dark-skinned being of rivers and pools, "mischievous and playful" or malicious, blamed for drownings and for stolen children. The lexicographer Esteban Pichardo listed it in 1836 (earliest attestation found, via search). Ways to summon one: offer cigars; walk round a ceiba tree twelve times at midnight; or gather seven men named Juan. Some tellings make the güijes the spirit-helpers of the Taíno cemíes. The folklorist Samuel Feijóo called it "the most robust, constant and complete" of Cuban legends. The poet Nicolás Guillén put it in "Balada del Güije", in West Indies Ltd. (1934) — a mother warning her child away from the river.
  • The odd detail: "seven men named Juan" — the same trick of names as the three Juans who found the Virgin of El Cobre.
  • People: Esteban Pichardo — lexicographer (1836); Samuel Feijóo — folklorist (Mitología cubana, 1986); Nicolás Guillén — poet (1934).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Güijes ; via search: https://www.radiohc.cu/de-interes/caleidoscopio/237521-el-guije-del-rio-yayabo
  • Confidence: High as folklore; the 1836 attestation via search summary (Medium).
  • Divergence check: holds (folklore).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the güije's own racial caricature is part of the record (a small black imp) — handle it the way Guillén did, as the river's warning, not as a monster to shoot. A drowned child in an Oriente mill-river, a garrison that blames "the natives", and a thing in the water that is neither.

The Madre de Aguas — the horned serpent of the springs — "from the 1600s"

  • Where: rivers, lagoons and springs across Cuba.
  • What happened: Folklore. A giant boa "the thickness of a palm tree", with two horn-like knobs on its head and reversed, bullet-proof scales, which lives in a pool or river that never dries while it lives there; whoever tries to kill or capture it dies; it swallows calves. Stories are said to go back to the 1600s (the first recorder is not named in the article; Cuban scholarship by Jesús Guanche is cited). It is kin to the wider Afro-Atlantic water-mother figures (Mami Wata, Watramama, Brazil's mãe-d'água).
  • The odd detail: a monster that is good for the water supply — kill it and the spring dies. A mill's engineers draining a lagoon is a plot.
  • People: Jesús Guanche Pérez — Cuban ethnologist.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madre_de_aguas
  • Confidence: Medium (folklore; "1600s" first attestation unconfirmed).
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): American engineers of a sugar central (Hershey-style) drain a lagoon for irrigation in 1930s Oriente; the spring dies, the cattle vanish, and the workers know why. Good "occupier doesn't listen" story; the creature is the local ecology, not the villain.

La Milagrosa — Amelia Goyri in the Colón Cemetery — died 3 May 1901

  • Where: Colón Cemetery, Havana.
  • What happened: Amelia Goyri de la Hoz died in childbirth on 3 May 1901, aged about 24; her child died too. Her husband and cousin José Vicente Adot y Rahel visited the grave several times a day for years, knocking with one of the vault's four iron rings and walking away backwards so he could see her as long as possible. Legend says that when the vault was opened in 1914 (for the burial of Adot's father), Amelia's body was incorrupt, and the baby, buried at her feet, was found in her arms. She became La Milagrosa, the miracle-worker: petitioners still knock with the iron ring, pray, and leave backwards; mothers and the childless bring flowers and thanks.
  • The odd detail: the ritual is the widower's: every pilgrim who knocks and walks backwards is repeating one grieving man's private habit.
  • People: Amelia Goyri de la Hoz — "La Milagrosa"; José Vicente Adot y Rahel — her husband.
  • Sources: via search: https://www.thehistoryreader.com/cultural-history/la-milagrosa-havanas-miraculous-one/ ; https://oncubanews.com/en/chronicles/legend-miraculous-love/ ; https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/21536/amelia_goyri-de_la_hoz
  • Confidence: High for the death date and names; the 1914 exhumation "miracle" is legend.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (death and legend), happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): three layers in a single tomb: the Church's reading (incorruption, a saint in waiting), the folk practice (knock, pray, back away), and whatever is actually in the vault. The IHS would want the 1914 exhumation's witnesses.

The Colón Cemetery — Havana's city of the dead — from 1876

  • Where: Vedado, Havana; ~50 hectares laid out like the city's own grid.
  • What happened: Built from 1871 (founded) / 1876 (opened — sources give both) to replace the old Espada Cemetery, designed by the Galician architect Calixto Arellano de Loira, who became its first occupant, dying before it was finished. Some 800,000 graves and over a million interments; a marble main gate (Faith, Hope and Charity) by José Vilalta Saavedra. The Monument to the Firefighters (sculptor Agustín Querol, architect Julio M. Clara Maass (1901) and, legend has it, many of the Maine's dead lay here before removal; La Milagrosa is here; and in September 1932 the ABC tunnelled under its central avenue beside the Firefighters' Monument and laid dynamite (Part C).

  • People: Calixto de Loira — architect, first burial; Agustín Querol — sculptor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colon_Cemetery,_Havana

El Templete and the ceiba — three times round on 16 November

  • Where: Plaza de Armas, Old Havana, beside the Castillo de la Real Fuerza.
  • What happened: A small Greco-Roman temple inaugurated on 19 March 1828 marks where, by tradition, Havana's first mass and first town council were held under a ceiba tree on 16 November 1519; inside are Jean-Baptiste Vermay's paintings of the scene. Each 16 November (the eve, by some accounts) crowds walk three times round the ceiba in silence, touching it and making a wish; the ceiba is sacred in Afro-Cuban religion as well. (The tree in the window was one of several successors; the last was removed in 2016.)
  • The odd detail: a Catholic foundation rite and an orisha's tree, performed as one act by the same crowd — the city's origin as a ritual circle.
  • People: Jean-Baptiste Vermay — painter.
  • Sources: via search: https://oncubanews.com/en/made-in-cuba/three-times-around-ceiba/ ; https://havanatimes.org/features/havana-founding-landmark-el-templete/
  • Confidence: High for 1828/1519 and the practice (via search summaries).
  • Divergence check: holds.

El Morro, La Cabaña and the nine o'clock gun
