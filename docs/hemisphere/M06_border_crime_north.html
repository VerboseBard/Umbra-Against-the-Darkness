M06 — The border and the crime of the Mexican north, 1910–1940

  • Lane: M06 — border towns and their vice economies under Prohibition (Tijuana / Agua Caliente, Mexicali and its Chinese quarter, Ciudad Juárez, Nogales, Nuevo Laredo, Matamoros), smuggling (rum north, guns and people, drugs), the Border Patrol, the Texas Rangers' violence of 1915–19, the Mexican Repatriation, notable murders and disappearances (Bierce, Aimee Semple McPherson, Orozco, Blanco, Benton), the Chinese of the north, outlaw legends and folk saints, and the period press's framing.
  • Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (research agent) · Brief: BRIEF.md in this folder + the Chicago sweep's BRIEF.md (entry format, sourcing rules).
  • Setting sources read first: ALTERNATE_HISTORY.md §0, §3, §7.4, §8; M01_mexico_history_1910_1940.md (skimmed so as not to repeat: Torreón, Bierce, Plan de San Diego/Porvenir, the Yaqui, Villa's death, the Escobar rising and Naco bombing, and the 1931 Sonora expulsion all appear there in short form — entries here that overlap say (also in M01) and go deeper or wider).

How to read the divergence lines. Every entry says where it falls against the setting's treaty line. The setting cedes Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila and Nuevo León (its "mineral north"), holds Tampico outright and Veracruz's customs house by receivership. Two things the setting has not ruled and this lane keeps hitting: (1) the Baja California / Tijuana / Mexicali question — Baja is not on the ceded list, but ALTERNATE_HISTORY §7.4 lists Mexicali's Colorado-delta cotton (Colorado River Land Co.) among the northern prizes; and (2) the Tamaulipas border towns (Nuevo Laredo, Matamoros, Reynosa) — Tamaulipas is not ceded except the Tampico enclave, so in the setting the only stretch of the old Rio Grande line that still divides America from Mexico may be the Tamaulipas reach. Sonora, Chihuahua and Coahuila are American, so Nogales, Agua Prieta, Naco, Ciudad Juárez, Piedras Negras/Ciudad Acuña are, in the setting, American cities on both sides of a former line. Before March 1916 everything happened as recorded.

Overview (40 entries). The border of 1910–40 was not one line but a string of different economies, and most of its famous crimes and legends sit on the setting's ceded side. The best material: a German agent, Felix Sommerfeld, who ran Madero's secret service, armed Villa and proposed in May 1915 to "provoke a war between the U.S. and Mexico" (the setting's Germany-as-patron spine, ready-made); Ambrose Bierce's five rival graves; Aimee Semple McPherson walking out of the Sonoran desert at Agua Prieta in 1926; the Baja question (Cantú's licensed-vice "republic," Chandler's Colorado River Land Co., Rodríguez's Agua Caliente) that the setting has not ruled; Niño Fidencio curing the president of Mexico at Espinazo, Nuevo León (ceded ground, living religion — handle carefully); and the Repatriation, where the same Chicago-area steel towns the Chicago book uses sent trainloads of Americans "home" to a Mexico that the setting half-owns. The Rangers' violence (the Plan of San Diego, La Matanza, Porvenir, the 1919 Canales hearing) and the Chinese of the north (Torreón 1911, the Sonoran expulsion 1931) are recorded respectfully and without an occult overlay. Also included: two unresolved "tell-tale" numbers a GM can hand a player (Juárez's ledger of drunks and prostitutes; the Border Patrol's 23 dead in ten years) and a run of period names and legends (the Tijuana Bibles, the salad, Tayopa's lost silver). Two structural findings: the Tamaulipas reach (Nuevo Laredo, Matamoros) is the only stretch of the old line still international in the setting, so Prohibition-era border drama has to live there or in Baja; and almost every Sonoran, Chihuahuan and Nuevo Leonese hero or villain here (Calles, de la Huerta, Cárdenas at Agua Prieta, Villa, Orozco, Fidencio) belongs to ground the treaty gives away.

Part A — Tijuana, Baja California and the Prohibition resorts

The Agua Caliente resort — opened 22 June 1928, closed 1935

  • Where: Tijuana, Baja California, a few miles south of the San Diego line (now the site of the Escuela Preparatoria Federal Lázaro Cárdenas).
  • What happened: Three American businessmen — the "Border Barons" Wirt G. Bowman (who already ran Tijuana's Foreign Club), Baron Long (California hotelier and nightclub owner) and James N. Crofton (racing man) — built a resort for roughly $10 million, on land owned by Gen. Abelardo L. Rodríguez, then military commander and governor of Baja California (later president of Mexico, 1932–34, and himself an investor). It opened 22 June 1928: a 500-room hotel, a casino, a spa with Turkish steam baths and a minaret tower, designed by a 19-year-old architect, Wayne McAllister. A second stage (December 1929) added an Olympic-size pool, an 18-hole golf course, horse and greyhound tracks, a tropical aviary, bungalows, a private radio station and an airstrip. The casino's showpiece was the Gold Room, with gold chips and a rumoured $500 minimum wager. Guests included Clark Gable, Jean Harlow, the Marx Brothers, Marlene Dietrich, Bing Crosby, Charlie Chaplin and Jack Dempsey; Rita Hayworth was discovered dancing there. President Cárdenas outlawed gambling in 1935 and the resort died; the buildings reopened as a school (a junior high, 1939, by one account) and the blue-tiled minaret, the spa shell and the bungalows survive.
  • The odd detail: the resort had its own private airstrip and radio station — Hollywood flew in for the weekend and the casino's takings were driven north to San Diego banks in a Cadillac (see the next entry).
  • People: Wirt G. Bowman — promoter, Foreign Club owner; Baron Long — hotelier; James N. Crofton — racing manager; Abelardo L. Rodríguez — governor and land-owner, future president; Wayne McAllister — teenage architect.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agua_Caliente_Casino_and_Hotel · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tijuana
  • Confidence: High on dates, investors, facilities and the 1935 ban (Wikipedia, cross-checked against a second search summary); the $500 minimum is "rumoured" in the source.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — and the setting has not said whether Baja California is ceded. Baja is not on the ceded list, but ALTERNATE_HISTORY §7.4 puts Mexicali's Colorado-delta cotton among the northern prizes. If Baja stays Mexican (recommended), the resort holds: it is the point of the border — American vice beyond American law, run by a Sonoran governor (Rodríguez was born in Guaymas, ceded Sonora). If Baja is ceded, Tijuana is American soil and a casino there is illegal, so Agua Caliente would need to be a licensed concession. Prohibition itself holds (repeal Dec 1933, domestic calendar), so the 1928–33 vice economy is intact either way.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a resort for the rich and famous on the occupier's doorstep, financed by three Americans and a Sonoran general, with a private airstrip — a natural Golden Circle meeting-house, a place where Hollywood, Chicago gunmen and Mexican officials sit in one room and nobody records who. Cárdenas's 1935 ban is the day the room closes.

The Agua Caliente money-car robbery and the first machine-gun crime in San Diego — 20 May 1929

  • Where: the National City dike on old Highway 101, San Diego County; the hideout at Villa Terrace near Balboa Park; a black Ford abandoned at Edgemont and B streets.
  • What happened: On Monday 20 May 1929, a Cadillac coupe carrying the casino's Sunday revenue north to San Diego banks was stopped by a windshield-less black Ford that shot its tires. Its two Mexican guards, Nemesio Monroy and José Borrego, fought back and were killed; the bandits took the bags and got away with about $85,000–86,000 — but all but $5,800 was in checks they could not cash. The county sheriff said a Thompson gun had lately been bought by a Tijuana resident; police called it the work of "Chicago gangsters". In fact the killers were Martin "Marty" Colson (27) and Robert Lee Cochran (24), career burglars, with a third man, Jerry Kearney (28, small-time bootlegger). Kearney and Cochran burned the checks in the backyard; Kearney tried to dig a bullet out of the wounded Colson's shoulder with a pocket knife, nicked an artery, and his wife called a doctor. Colson was captured within hours; the others two days later in Los Angeles. The machine gun, it turned out, was never fired: the guards were shot with .38 pistols, and the gun went into the sea off San Pedro. Both men pleaded guilty and got life (6 Aug 1929); Colson had begged the judge for the gallows.
  • The odd detail: Colson, in Folsom, tried to swim a prison moat with homemade diving gear; in July 1933 his brother Emil tried to smuggle guns in kegs of nails; in 1934 Colson shot himself in the warden's office with a pistol made in the prison shop.
  • People: Nemesio Monroy and José Borrego — the two guards killed; Martin Colson and Robert Lee Cochran — the robbers; Jerry Kearney and his wife Agnes — accessory and nurse; Judge Charles N. Andrews — sentencing judge.
  • Sources: http://www.sandiegoyesterday.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/05/Agua-Caliente-money-car-robbery.pdf (a San Diego Union-Tribune feature of 13 Dec 2009 reprinting the 21 May 1929 report; read in full) · https://voiceofsandiego.org/2010/08/20/larceny-luxury-and-liquor-he-wrote/ (title only)
  • Confidence: High on the facts above (single well-sourced feature drawn from the 1929 San Diego papers); the "first machine-gun robbery" claim is disputed by the feature itself (the gun was carried, not fired).
  • Divergence check: the killings are on the American side (National City, California) and involve no Mexican sovereignty; holds as domestic crime. Only the source of the money bends (see the resort's entry). The period press's first reflex — "Chicago mob" — is the point: in the setting Chicago's gangland is already the year's great story.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the press blamed Chicago gangsters for a job done by three local losers, and the real motive was worthless paper. An Agua Caliente man who wanted the checks (which name gamblers) burned is a different reading; the two guards who "fought back" are the only ones who ever told the truth about that day and they are dead.

Phar Lap wins at Agua Caliente and dies sixteen days later — 20 March / 5 April 1932

  • Where: Agua Caliente racetrack, Tijuana; then a horse farm at Menlo Park, near San Francisco.
  • What happened: The Australian gelding Phar Lap won the Agua Caliente Handicap on 20 March 1932 (ridden by Billy Elliot, by two lengths) — the richest purse in the world at the time. On the morning of 5 April 1932 he collapsed at a farm outside San Francisco and died in the arms of his strapper Tommy Woodcock. American veterinary experts' official autopsy concluded colic or a bacterial infection, inconclusively. Australia has argued about poison for ninety years; in 2008 a synchrotron analysis at the Advanced Photon Source near Chicago of hairs from his mane was reported to show that he had swallowed a massive dose of arsenic in the 40 hours before he died. (The claim that gangsters threatened him at Tijuana is a popular story I could not confirm — see Leads not chased.)
  • The odd detail: the analysis of the horse's hair was done in a synchrotron near Chicago — seventy-six years later — and it still does not say who fed him arsenic, or by accident.
  • People: Phar Lap — the horse; Tommy Woodcock — his strapper; Billy Elliot — jockey; Harry Telford — trainer/owner (UNVERIFIED this session).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agua_Caliente_Casino_and_Hotel · https://phys.org/news/2010-05-hair-analysis-legendary-racehorse-phar.html (via search) · https://www.nma.gov.au/defining-moments/resources/phar-lap (via search)
  • Confidence: High on the dates and the win; the arsenic finding is a scientific claim (Medium — the ingestion was reported, the intent is unknown); the poisoning legend is folklore.
  • Divergence check: the horse's death is in California (holds); the win is in Baja California — subject to the resort's Baja ruling. 1932 is Hoover's year in the setting (domestic calendar holds).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the world's richest race, run at an American-owned resort in Mexico, and the winner poisoned within sixteen days: a classic "who was the bookmaker" case, and the arsenic detail is already a real forensic lead a Section 13 lab would love.

The Magonista invasion of Baja California and the first Battle of Tijuana — January – June 1911

  • Where: Mexicali (taken 29 January 1911), Tecate and Tijuana (taken 9 May 1911), Baja California; the San Diego and Los Angeles waterfronts that fed it.
  • What happened: Ricardo Flores Magón's anarchist Partido Liberal Mexicano (PLM) raised a rebellion on the Baja border while Madero was fighting in Chihuahua. Its "Foreign Legion" of American radicals — many IWW members, including (per some accounts) Joe Hill, who left San Pedro on 30 April 1911 — and adventurers seized Mexicali, then Tijuana. The Welsh soldier of fortune Caryl ap Rhys Pryce led ~200 rebels at the Battle of Tijuana on 9 May 1911 and held the town. The Díaz government called them "filibusters" (armed foreigners seeking to seize Mexican land), and the American press ran stories of a plot to annex Baja; promoter Dick Ferris encouraged separatist talk, but a modern historian finds no evidence of real millionaire backing. Pryce left in late May; Jack Mosby (a Marine deserter and IWW man) was elected commander on 3 June and tried to raise money with a "Wild West" Sunday spectacle for tourists. The Magonistas were routed on 22 June 1911. Tourists and gamblers were already arriving at Tijuana's casinos and curio shops during the occupation.
  • The odd detail: the revolutionaries financed themselves partly by taxing Tijuana's casinos and curio shops and by collecting customs duties ($400–$1,000 a month) — the vice economy of the border was funding the anarchists as early as 1911.
  • People: Ricardo Flores Magón — anarchist leader (see his own entry: he died in Leavenworth); Caryl ap Rhys Pryce — Welsh mercenary, Foreign Legion commander; Jack Mosby — IWW commander; Stanley Williams — Legion commander, killed 8 April; Col. Celso Vega — federal commander at Tijuana; Joe Hill — IWW songwriter (participation "likely", not certain).
  • Sources: https://sandiegohistory.org/journal/1999/january/magonista/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magonista_rebellion_of_1911 (via search) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caryl_Ap_Rhys_Pryce (via search)
  • Confidence: High on the campaign and battle; Medium on Joe Hill (sources say "likely").
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Baja's later status is the setting's open question — but 1911's dream of a foreign "Baja republic" is already a national-security nightmare for a US that would, in the setting, turn south a few years later.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the "Foreign Legion" of Wobblies and mercenaries under a red flag, financed by casino taxes. In the setting, "Germany as the patron of other people's revolutions" has an obvious precedent: the Magonista press was suspected of foreign money too. The Golden Circle's map (centred on Havana, 1,200-mile radius) includes the Pacific coast of Mexico; a Baja separatist "republic" is exactly the kind of buffer the Circle would want.

Part B — Mexicali and the Chinese of the north

La Chinesca — Mexicali's Chinese majority, its underground and the tunnel legend — 1900s–1930s

  • Where: Mexicali, Baja California — the old Chinese quarter, "La Chinesca", beside the Calexico line.
  • What happened: Chinese labourers came around 1900 to dig and farm for the Colorado River Land Company (Harry Chandler / Otis of the Los Angeles Times). When the irrigation works were done many stayed. By 1920 the Chinese outnumbered Mexicans in Mexicali by about 10,000 to 700 (a figure widely repeated; treat as approximate). They organised: the Asociación Chung Shan de Baja California (1915) and the Asociación China de Mexicali (1919). Around 40 basements are said to have been linked by tunnels; the Chinese are said to have run gambling and vice, and the Prohibition-era casinos, brothels, opium dens and bootleggers used them. Some accounts add tunnels running under the border to Calexico — which no source verifies and which some in the community regard as legend. In the 1920s–30s tong wars and anti-Chinese violence swept the north; Mexicali was spared the worst of the 1931 expulsions elsewhere because the land was still owned by the Company and Cárdenas's 1937 land reform came later. The last basement residents left after floods in the late 1970s.
  • The odd detail: the basements were originally where labourers slept out the 105-degree summers — the "underground city" of the legend began as air conditioning.
  • People: Harry Chandler — Colorado River Land Co. investor; the Asociación China de Mexicali — the community's own governing body.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Chinesca · https://www.vice.com/en/article/inside-the-basement-tunnels-where-mexicalis-chinese-immigrants-once-lived/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexicali
  • Confidence: High on the Chinese majority, the associations and the basements; the tunnels to the U.S. are legend (say so); the "control of organised crime" claim is a period and tourist-brochure framing (Medium).
  • Divergence check: BENDS depending on Baja. ALTERNATE_HISTORY §7.4 lists Mexicali's Colorado-delta cotton (Colorado River Land Co.) among the American prizes — if Mexicali is ceded, the Chinese quarter is American, under the Chinese Exclusion regime that ran to 1943 — a majority of the town that American law says cannot exist. Compare Sonora's expulsions (Part G).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Chinese-majority border town with an underground quarter and a rumour of tunnels to an American town. Respect note: this is a real community with living temples and associations — the occult layer belongs to what the tunnels were dug to hide from, not to the people who lived in them.

Part C — The Chihuahua–Texas line: Juárez and El Paso

The bath riots at the Santa Fe Street Bridge — 28–30 January 1917

  • Where: the Santa Fe Street Bridge between Ciudad Juárez and El Paso; the El Paso disinfection plant.
  • What happened: In January 1917 U.S. health and immigration officials, pressed by El Paso's mayor, required every Mexican crossing the bridge to be stripped, bathed in a kerosene/gasoline mixture, vaccinated, their clothes steamed or gassed (cyanide for shoes), their hair clipped or doused with vinegar and kerosene — a typhus precaution. Fear was fed by a 1916 El Paso jail fire in which 27–28 prisoners had died in a gasoline bath, and by rumours that women were photographed nude. On 28 January Carmelita Torres, a 17-year-old maid, refused to get off her trolley; within an hour more than 200 women blocked the bridge and by noon "several thousand" were there (Texas press), blocking the bridge; the protest ran 28–30 January. The disinfection went on for decades — by the 1920s–30s cyanide gas and Zyklon B were added, and DDT only later, in the 1940s ⟨fixed: V_M06 — the chapter and some web sources put DDT in the 1920s–30s, which is impossible: its insecticidal properties were discovered in 1939 and it went into military use in 1942–43 (Wikipedia, DDT)⟩ — and it continued into the Bracero era.
  • The odd detail: the border's fumigation plant put cyanide gas through Mexican workers' clothing and shoes, and by the 1930s the pesticide Zyklon B was in use there — the same product later used in Europe for a very different purpose (a fact that, in a setting with no Nazi Germany, needs an owner's decision before it is used at all).
  • People: Carmelita Torres — 17-year-old maid who began the protest; Tom Lea — El Paso mayor (Wikipedia's summary says "Jr."; the mayor of 1915–17 is usually given as Tom Lea Sr., father of the artist — UNVERIFIED which); historian David Dorado Romo — recovered the story in Ringside Seat to a Revolution (2006).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1917_Bath_riots · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carmelita_Torres (via search)
  • Confidence: High on date, cause, the women's refusal; Medium on the cyanide/Zyklon detail (Wikipedia summary, not checked against the Public Health Service papers).
  • Divergence check: BENDS. January 1917 is the setting's Southern War, won by 1917. Ciudad Juárez is in Chihuahua — ceded. In the setting there is no international bridge at Santa Fe Street: both banks are American. The bath house could still exist as a health cordon between an occupied Mexican population and the American city — a domestic cordon, not a border, which is more chilling. Note there is no Nazi Germany in the setting, so the Zyklon B fact is a plain pesticide fact and should be flagged to the owner before it is used.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "a health check at the bridge" is a perfect cover for the Golden Circle or for Section 13 to inspect who crosses — a bath, a needle, a clothing gas. Real precedent: a border where a Public Health Service stripped, sprayed and fumigated a people in the name of typhus.

Part D — The Sonora–Arizona line: Nogales, Agua Prieta, Naco

The Battle of Ambos Nogales — 27 August 1918

  • Where: Nogales, Arizona and Nogales, Sonora — International Street and the customs house.
  • What happened: A Mexican carpenter, Zeferino Gil Lamadrid, tried to cross into Arizona without inspection; a U.S. Customs inspector (Arthur G. Barber) shouted for him to halt while Mexican customs officers told him to stay put. Private William Klint raised his rifle and a shot was fired (a warning shot, it may be). Within minutes the two towns were in a gunfight: Lt. Col. Frederick Herman rushed three troops of the 10th Cavalry (Black cavalry) and three companies of the 35th Infantry to the line. Felix B. Peñaloza, mayor of Nogales, Sonora, 53, was mortally wounded by a shot from the Arizona side while walking toward the line under a white flag tied to his cane. US dead: 4–5 (Klint, Lt. Loftus, Cpls. Lots and Whitworth, Capt. Hungerford, per the article); Mexican: an official 15, but the U.S. counted "129 new graves". A three-year-old, Julia Medina, was among the dead. The trigger for the ceasefire at 7:45 p.m. was a white flag over the Mexican customs house. Rumours that German agents had led the attack (an anonymous letter to Herman; "two German agents" among the dead) were searched for in local, state and national archives and not found. Gov. Plutarco Elías Calles and Gen. DeRosey Cabell agreed on the first permanent border fence, two miles down International Street.
  • The odd detail: the first permanent fence on the US–Mexico border was built because of a fight that began when a carpenter would not be inspected — and the wartime rumour that German agents were behind it left no trace in any archive.
  • People: Zeferino Gil Lamadrid — the carpenter; Pvt. William Klint — the soldier whose shot began it; Felix B. Peñaloza — mayor, killed under a white flag; Lt. Col. Frederick J. Herman — U.S. commander; Gen. DeRosey Cabell; Plutarco Elías Calles — governor of Sonora, later president.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ambos_Nogales · http://roadstothegreatwar-ww1.blogspot.com/2024/05/27-august-1918-us-armys-battle-of-ambos.html (via search) · https://www.nogalesinternational.com/news/battle-of-ambos-nogales-signaled-birth-of-border-fence/article_eaa99f46-a735-11e8-acac-8b4566af6c8d.html (via search)
  • Confidence: High on the outline; casualty counts differ by source (15 vs. 125+ Mexican dead).
  • Divergence check: BENDS hard — CEDED NORTH. In the setting Sonora is American by 1917 and the year 1918 is not a Great War year for the U.S., so the German-agent rumour is not about the U.S. war effort but about Germany as patron of other people's revolutions. There is no international line at Nogales — the two towns are one American city and the "fence" of 1918 is at best a military cordon. Notably the source of the fence (the Sonoran governor agreeing) would have to be an American military governor.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the first fence on the border went up after a fight in which a mayor was shot under a white flag and the Army blamed "German agents" — in a setting where Germany really did win and really does back revolutions, the rumour that the archive cannot confirm is precisely the kind a Section 13 file would keep alive.

Part E — The Repatriation

Mexican Repatriation — 1929–1939, peak 1931–33

  • Where: the whole United States; Los Angeles (the model), Detroit, the Midwest steel towns, Texas; the Mexican border.
  • What happened: During the Depression, some 300,000 to 2 million people (estimates vary widely; Wikipedia says most scholarly estimates run 500,000 to 1 million ⟨fixed: V_M06 — was "a common figure is 400,000+"⟩) were deported or pressured to "repatriate" to Mexico — an estimated 40–60% of the voluntary returnees were U.S. citizens, mostly American-born children. Only about 82,000 were formally deported; the rest left under threat, welfare pressure and raids. President Hoover's Secretary of Labor William N. Doak pushed for more agents; the peak year was 1931, and rates fell under Roosevelt. Los Angeles, with the largest Mexican population outside Mexico, staged raids by federal, sheriff's and city police, and put people on trains. Mexico itself paid for the return of over 90,000 between July 1930 and June 1931 and tried to found farm colonies for returnees. By 1934 roughly one-third of the Mexicans in the U.S. had gone. California formally apologised in 2005; Los Angeles County in 2012, with a memorial.
  • The odd detail: roughly half the "Mexicans" sent to Mexico were American citizens who had never seen it — and some were sent from the Midwest in special trains funded by the steel companies that had employed them (see next entry).
  • People: Herbert Hoover — president 1929–33; William N. Doak — Secretary of Labor; the returnees.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Repatriation
  • Confidence: High on the outline; the totals are contested by historians — quote the range, not a single figure.
  • Divergence check: holds in the domestic calendar (Depression, Hoover, Doak), but "where do they go" BENDS. The setting's Mexico north of the treaty line is American, so a person "repatriated" to Chihuahua or Sonora goes to American territory — is a "Mexican" of the ceded north an alien, a subject or a citizen? (Chicago-sweep note: Mexicans in 1930 Chicago were a community of ~25,000.) This is the setting's built-in category problem, and it lands on exactly the 1930s American who the core book uses.

The Repatriation in Chicago's steel towns — Indiana Harbor and Gary, 1931–32

  • Where: Indiana Harbor (East Chicago), the "Block and Pennsy" district called "Little Mexico"; Gary, Indiana; the Mexican consulate in Chicago.
  • What happened: By 1929 Mexicans had surpassed the older European groups as Gary's largest immigrant steelworker group. Part F — Murders, vanishings, legends of the border

Ambrose Bierce walks into Villa's Mexico — last letter 26 December 1913 (also in M01)

The disappearance of Aimee Semple McPherson — 18 May – 23 June 1926

  • Where: Ocean Park / Venice Beach, California; Carmel-by-the-Sea; Agua Prieta, Sonora, and Douglas, Arizona.

McIntyre" with an unidentified "Miss X" (identified as Elizabeth Tovey, a Seattle nurse).   • People: Aimee Semple McPherson — Angelus Temple evangelist; Kenneth Ormiston — radio engineer at KFSG; Asa Keyes — Los Angeles district attorney; Lorraine Wiseman-Sielaff — unreliable witness; R. R. Gonzales — the Mexican man who found her.

Part G — The Chinese of the north: Torreón to Sonora

The Torreón massacre — 13–15 May 1911 (also in M01)

  • Where: Torreón, Coahuila — the railway city of the Laguna cotton district.
  • What happened: When Maderista forces took Torreón, 308 Asians — 303 Chinese and 5 Japanese — were killed, nearly half of the city's Chinese population, by revolutionary troops (under Emilio Madero) together with mobs from surrounding towns and local residents. Local revolutionary Jesús Flores incited it with speeches claiming the Chinese monopolised the grocery trade and "put Mexican women out of jobs" and demanding their expulsion. Chinese community leaders had circulated warnings on 12 May for people to hide. Property damage came to about $1 million, of which $849,928.69 was Chinese property. The Qing government first demanded 30 million pesos, later reduced to 6 million; a 1912 treaty awarded 3.1 million pesos, which was never paid ⟨fixed: V_M06 — was "never fully paid"; Wikipedia says Mexico never made payment, after Madero's murder and the collapse of 1913⟩. An investigation called it "an unprovoked massacre … conceived in malice and race hatred." President López Obrador apologised in 2021.
  • The odd detail: the massacre was announced in advance by the victims' own leaders, who told everyone to hide — and the killers were the revolution's "democratic" side; the debt outlived the president who signed it.
  • People: Emilio Madero — the president's brother, in command; Jesús Flores — the agitator; the 303 Chinese and 5 Japanese dead.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torre%C3%B3n_massacre
  • Confidence: High on date, toll and diplomatic aftermath; the exact scale of the settlement is Medium (sources differ on "never paid" versus "never fully paid").
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. CEDED NORTH (Coahuila). In the setting, a defaulted foreign claim on ground that becomes American in 1917 is a treaty question (see M01): does the 1917 settlement assume Mexico's debts to China for the north?
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): 308 dead and a treaty debt nobody paid, in a town the setting turns American. The unpaid claim gives a Chinese consular or tong character a legal reason to be in Torreón in the 1930s. Report respectfully; the occult layer belongs to what the killing woke, not to the victims' community.

The anti-Chinese leagues and the expulsion from Sonora — 1916–1932 (also in M01)

  • Where: Cananea, Nogales, Magdalena, Hermosillo, Sonora and Sinaloa; the Nogales, Arizona jail; the Chinese quarters of Mexicali (spared).
  • What happened: The campaign was organised, not spontaneous. José María Arana founded the first anti-Chinese leagues in Cananea in 1916 (with schoolteacher Serapio Dávila, who justified it from the 1917 Constitution). Cananea's mayor Julián S. González set a 1 January 1920 deadline to expel every Chinese; Gov. Adolfo de la Huerta gave "veiled approval." Law followed: 1919 (an 80% Mexican-hiring rule and separate Chinese neighbourhoods), 1921 (most Chinese immigration barred), 1923 (a ban on marriages between Mexican women and Chinese men — the women denounced as "traitors"). The Liga Nacionalista Pro-Raza (Hermosillo, 1925), the Comité Pro-Raza (1930, José Ángel Espinoza, who published El problema chino en México and El ejemplo de Sonora in 1931) and the "green guards" of Sonora stoned shops. In August 1931 Gov. Rodolfo Elías Calles ordered mass deportation: Chinese were trucked to the border, entire families "pushed through gaps in the border fence." At Nogales, 181 were waiting and 46 crossed into the U.S. in one night; Ack Wong, a Chinese vice-consul, was jailed at Cananea and freed only after an American consular agent intervened. More than 3,500 were expelled from Sonora; those who could fled to Sinaloa, Chihuahua and Baja California. The Hoover administration later deported the expelled Chinese to China from San Francisco. The Chinese population of Mexico fell from about 26,000 (1920s) to 618 documented in 1940; Sonora's fell to 92 and Sinaloa's from 2,123 to 165.
  • The odd detail: a schoolteacher wrote the theory, a Governor named Calles signed the trucks, and the United States — which itself excluded the Chinese — paid to ship the survivors home.
  • People: José María Arana — league founder; Serapio Dávila — schoolteacher ideologue; Adolfo de la Huerta and Rodolfo Elías Calles — governors; José Ángel Espinoza — Comité Pro-Raza author; Ack Wong — Chinese vice-consul jailed at Cananea.
  • Sources: https://acta.bibl.u-szeged.hu/56516/2/americana_006_001_005/bloch-ortoll.html (Bloch & Ortoll; read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Mexicans · https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comit%C3%A9_Pro-Raza (via search)
  • Confidence: High on the leagues, laws and the 1931 expulsion (scholarly summary); the numbers vary by source (3,000–3,500 in Sonora; "over 300" killed nationally in 1911 alone).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — CEDED NORTH (Sonora). In the setting the Mexican state that drafted these laws no longer governs Sonora after 1917, so the expulsion cannot be a state act — but the leagues (1916, before the treaty) and the Sonoran-born officials (both Calles and de la Huerta are Sonorans) exist. The setting's Chinese of the north, in American territory, fall under the Exclusion regime until 1943.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Sonoran leagues are the "cult versus population" template: a small organised group with a theory, a uniform (green) and a political patron, hiding behind an aggrieved public. In the setting the Golden Circle would fund such leagues, and the American Border officials who trucked the Chinese to Nogales, AZ, are the people a Section 13 case file would name.

Part H — The Rangers, the Border Patrol and the smugglers

The Plan of San Diego and the border war of 1915 — 6 January 1915 (also in M01)

  • Where: South Texas — Norias Ranch, Tandy's Station, the Lower Rio Grande Valley; the plan itself was signed in a Monterrey jail cell.
  • What happened: A manifesto dated San Diego, Texas, 6 January 1915 (actually signed in a Monterrey jail by Huertista prisoners) called for a "Liberating Army of Races and Peoples" to seize Arizona, New Mexico, California, Colorado and Texas as a republic, and for the execution of all white men over sixteen — with Germans notably exempted from the killing. Signers/leaders named include Basilio Ramos Jr. (captured in Brownsville), Luis de la Rosa (a former Brownsville deputy sheriff) and Aniceto Pizaña (associate of Ricardo Flores Magón). Raids began in July 1915 (five months late), peaking in August–September: 30 raids, 21 Americans killed, the Norias Ranch attack of 8 August (five raiders killed) and a train robbery at Tandy's Station (five killed, six wounded). Carranza's government supplied about half the raiders, then pulled back after Wilson recognised it on 19 October 1915. The reprisals: federal officials estimated 300+ Mexican Americans killed in late 1915–16, and the 1919 Canales inquiry put the total killed by law enforcement in 1914–19 at 300 to 5,000.
  • The odd detail: the plan's authors spared German Americans from the massacre they proposed — which is why Americans at the time suspected German agents, though no evidence was ever found.
  • People: Basilio Ramos Jr., Luis de la Rosa, Aniceto Pizaña — named leaders; Venustiano Carranza — whose government backed the raids; Woodrow Wilson — recognised Carranza on 19 October 1915.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plan_de_San_Diego · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Ranger_Division
  • Confidence: High on the plan's terms and the raid counts; the German-involvement suspicion is explicitly "no evidence found."
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. In the setting, Germany won and does patronise revolutions, so this is the record's own textbook case of the rumour ("Germany behind the border raids") that the setting turns from unproven to plausible. Reprisals then run into the Southern War's first years.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a manifesto that names Germans as the exempt class is the best "Germany as patron of other people's revolutions" artefact the real record offers. Section 13 would keep the original Monterrey document in a drawer.

The Canales investigation and the Rangers' reckoning — 31 January – 13 February 1919

  • Where: the Texas Capitol, Austin; South Texas, the Big Bend and Brownsville.
  • What happened: State Representative José Tomás Canales of Brownsville filed 19 charges against the Texas Rangers, forcing a legislative hearing from 31 January to 13 February 1919. The record documented widespread killing of Mexican-descent Texans (and some Black Texans). The 1919 report and later scholarship count 300 to 5,000 killed by Rangers and others between 1910 and 1919. The legislature cut the force from well over 1,000 men (including thousands of unpaid "Loyalty Rangers" raised during the World War) to 68. No Ranger was prosecuted; earlier, a Brownsville police officer, Toribio Rodríguez, had been shot by Rangers and local lawmen in 1912 (he died days later) and his shooters were still on the force in 1919. Rangers Capt. Henry (Harry) Ransom and Sgt. Frank Hamer (later of the Bonnie-and-Clyde ambush) served in this era.
  • The odd detail: the force was swollen by unpaid "Loyalty Rangers" created during the World War — and the legislature that cut the Rangers to 68 men eliminated them.
  • People: José Tomás Canales — the legislator; Capt. Harry Ransom — Ranger captain; Frank Hamer — Ranger sergeant; Toribio Rodríguez — Brownsville officer killed in 1912.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Ranger_Division
  • Confidence: High on the hearing and the force reduction; the 300–5,000 range is the historians' range (wide on purpose).
  • Divergence check: the hearing is in Texas (1919). In the setting the Loyalty Rangers were wartime (America was not in the Great War); the Southern War itself would be the pretext. BENDS lightly — the terror plausibly moves south with the line and the Rangers are the occupation's first police.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a legislative record of 19 charges that names living men — the paper trail a 1930s Secret Service agent could find in an Austin archive, and the reason a Ranger sergeant (Hamer) has enemies. A dead Ranger's ghost story is not needed: the record is the haunting.

The Porvenir massacre — 28 January 1918 (also in M01)

  • Where: Porvenir, Presidio County, Texas — a farming village in the Big Bend country of the Rio Grande.
  • What happened: After a raid on the Brite Ranch on Christmas Day 1917, Texas Rangers of Company B under Capt. James Monroe Fox, four local ranchers (Buck Pool, John Pool, Tom Snyder, Raymond Fitzgerald) and soldiers of the 8th U.S. Cavalry separated 15 unarmed Mexican-descent men and boys from their families and shot them; the victims included Antonio Castañeda, Longino Flores and Pedro Herrera. Fox's report claimed residents had fired on the Rangers; investigation showed the victims "did not die in a shootout, but were killed while they were unarmed and in Texas Ranger custody." Governor William P. Hobby disbanded Company B in June 1918, fired five Rangers and transferred seven; Fox resigned; no one was prosecuted. The survivors fled to Mexico; in 1926 Mexican attorneys filed twelve claims with the U.S.–Mexico General Claims Commission. The Texas Historical Commission put up a marker in 2018.
  • The odd detail: the massacre led to the disbanding of a Ranger company, which the state treated as the punishment.
  • People: Capt. James Monroe Fox — company commander, falsified report; Buck Pool, John Pool, Tom Snyder, Raymond Fitzgerald — local ranchers named as participants; Antonio Castañeda, Longino Flores, Pedro Herrera — three of the fifteen victims; Gov. W. P. Hobby.

  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: happened as recorded up to Jan 1918, but BENDS in the setting: the setting's Southern War is a won war by 1917 and the Mexican side of that reach of the river is CEDED Chihuahua. The massacre could still occur in Presidio County (Texas) — or move to the treaty line. In either case the survivors "fled to Mexico" that is now American.

Respect: name the victims; leave the perpetrators' lore to the record. A Porvenir survivor's grandchild in 1930s El Paso or Chicago is a character the core book does not yet have.

The tequileros and the liquor war on the Rio Grande — 1919–1933

  • Where: the Rio Grande from Ciudad Guerrero, Tamaulipas, through Zapata and Los Ebanos, Texas; San Diego, Texas (transit hub); Laredo/Nuevo Laredo (the Cadillac Bar); El Paso/Juárez; Tijuana.
  • What happened: Prohibition began on 17 January 1920; rum-running followed within weeks. On the Mexican border the smugglers, the tequileros, were often "three to six farm boys, usually on horses, guiding a few well-packed mules" — many of them former soldiers of the Revolution — working with Tejano intermediaries. One intercepted mule train carried 550 bottles (worth $1,375); a strong mule carried 50 or more. In February 1927, in Jim Hogg County, Texas, mounted customs inspectors and smugglers traded shots; one smuggler was killed, six horses and several hundred bottles seized. The publisher of The Line Riders (a book on this subject) describes it as a long and deadly "liquor war" on the Rio Grande, with late-night shootouts and horseback pursuits. Enforcement was uneven: "local farmer" moonshiners were usually left alone while cross-border tequila traders were prosecuted fiercely. Nuevo Laredo's Cadillac Bar (founded by Achille "Mayo" Mehault) served Laredo's drinkers who crossed "as though they were walking across a street." Smuggling peaked 1919–28 and had "come to a quiet end" in Prohibition's last five years.
  • The odd detail: the Prohibition frontier's front line was mules carrying 50 bottles each, driven by veterans of Villa's and Carranza's armies.
  • People: Achille "Mayo" Mehault — founder of the Cadillac Bar, Nuevo Laredo; the tequileros; Jim Hogg County customs inspectors.
  • Sources: https://mexiconewsdaily.com/news/during-prohibition-mexico-tequileros/ · https://www.statesman.com/story/news/history/2021/07/20/cadillac-bar-nuevo-laredo-remembered-memoir-and-cookbook/7840360002/ (via search) · https://rowman.com/ISBN/9781493055043/The-Line-Riders-The-Border-Patrol-Prohibition-and-the-Liquor-War-on-the-Rio-Grande (via search)
  • Confidence: High on the general picture and the 1927 incident (Mexico News Daily summary); the Cadillac Bar detail is from a search summary.
  • Divergence check: holds in the domestic calendar (Prohibition, repeal Dec 1933). The Tamaulipas reach — Nuevo Laredo, Matamoros, Reynosa, Ciudad Guerrero — is not ceded in the setting, so this stretch of the Rio Grande remains the setting's live international line and the real Prohibition-era frontier; the ceded stretches (Ciudad Juárez, Piedras Negras, Nogales) are American on both banks.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): in a setting where most of the old line disappeared in 1917, the Tamaulipas reach is where smugglers, Border Patrol and former revolutionary soldiers still meet. What is carried in the mule's second pannier is the module.

The Candelaria incursion — 10–23 August 1919

  • Where: Candelaria, Presidio County, Texas, and the Rio Conchos, Chihuahua.
  • What happened: Two U.S. Army Border Air Patrol pilots, Lt. Harold G. Peterson and Paul H. Capt. Leonard F. Matlack negotiated their release with money from local ranchers and the Marfa National Bank (Peterson after the first payment, Davis at the second, escaping an ambush). On 19 August Matlack led cavalry across the border; the 8th Cavalry stormed a blockhouse, killing four men, and aircraft pilots killed one of three horsemen in a canyon, reportedly Rentería.   • People: Lt. Harold G. Peterson and Paul H. Davis — captured pilots; Jesús Rentería — bandit; Capt. Leonard F. Matlack — rescuer.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candelaria_border_incursion_of_1919
  • Confidence: High on outline (Wikipedia summary); the identity of the dead horseman is "reportedly."
  • Divergence check: BENDS — CEDED NORTH. In the setting the Rio Conchos is American (Chihuahua), so the pilots did not "cross a border" at all — the raid is an American aircraft crashing in American territory and being ransomed by bandits, which is the occupation's own problem, not a diplomatic one.

Part I — Murders and vanishings of the Revolution's north (1913–1922)

The murder of William S. Benton — 17 February 1914

  • Where: Ciudad Juárez, Chihuahua — Villa's house, where Benton was killed; the cemetery where he was buried.

Part J — Framing, folk healers and legends of the north

El Paso bans marijuana — 1914/1915, and the period's picture of the "Mexican"

  • Where: El Paso and Ciudad Juárez; then Texas.
  • What happened: El Paso became the first American city to restrict cannabis (dated 1914 in some sources, 1915 in others). The city's stated worry was that the drug caused violent behaviour among Mexicans, "Negroes, prostitutes, pimps and a criminal class of whites." A 1917 report described El Paso as a "hot-bed of marihuana fiends." Texas banned transfers in 1919, possession with intent to sell in 1923, and made possession a felony in 1931, with life sentences possible for small amounts.

  • People: Chief Deputy Stanley Good — the sheriff's officer whose quote circulated.

Part K — The Sonora–Arizona and Chihuahua–Texas wars, and the spies who armed them

The Cananea strike and the Arizona Rangers who crossed the line — 1–6 June 1906 (living memory)

  • Where: Cananea, Sonora, 30 km south of the Arizona line; Bisbee, Douglas and Naco, Arizona.
  • What happened: Col. William Cornell Greene's Cananea Consolidated Copper Company employed about 5,360 Mexican workers and 2,200 Americans in 1906. When Greene announced that American miners would earn $5 a day while Mexicans were capped at $3, the Mexicans walked out on 1 June chanting "cinco pesos, ocho horas." They demanded the firing of unpopular foremen, a 75% Mexican hiring quota and equal advancement. Armed Americans, including company employees, fired on the crowd — about 33 workers died and 22 were wounded — after the lumber-yard manager George Metcalf turned a hose and then a gun on strikers and the miners set the yard ablaze. Capt. Thomas H. Rynning led 275 Arizona Rangers and other volunteers from Bisbee, Douglas and Naco across the border, against the Arizona governor's orders; Col. Emilio Kosterlitsky's Mexican federal troops and Governor Rafael Izábal's rurales reinforced the company. The strike ended on 6 June; three leaders got 15-year sentences (freed in 1911). The Mexican Liberal Party's press made Cananea a symbol of Díaz's dependence on foreign capital.
  • The odd detail: 275 American law-enforcement volunteers crossed an international line, against their own governor's orders, to help put down a strike in a Mexican town — four years before the Revolution began.
  • People: William C. Greene — copper king; George Metcalf — lumber-yard manager who fired first; Capt. Thomas H. Rynning — Arizona Rangers commander; Col. Emilio Kosterlitsky — Mexican federal colonel; Rafael Izábal — Sonoran governor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cananea_strike
  • Confidence: High (single summary; numbers are per the Wikipedia article and vary in sources).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. CEDED NORTH — in the setting Cananea's copper is precisely the "war metal" the Southern War took (ALTERNATE_HISTORY §7.4); the 1906 strike is the founding grievance of that mine, and the Arizona Rangers who crossed in 1906 are its precursors.

Villa is beaten at Agua Prieta by searchlights and Lázaro Cárdenas's guns — 1–2 November 1915

  • Where: Agua Prieta, Sonora, on the line opposite Douglas, Arizona.
  • What happened: In October 1915 Wilson shifted U.S. support from Villa to Carranza.   • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): 1,286 men left in the desert by a company that owns the telegraph; a one-day removal of a town's workforce, with a train, lists and no legal cover. A Section 13 case on a company that can do this once can do it again.

Felix Sommerfeld, Sam Dreben and the El Paso trade in guns and secrets — 1908–1919

  • Where: El Paso; Chihuahua; New York (Sommerfeld's later cover); Fort Oglethorpe, Georgia (internment).
  • What happened: Felix A. Sommerfeld (b. 1879 in Schneidemühl, Prussia, a middle-class Jewish family) arrived in Chihuahua in 1908 as a mining engineer but was in fact a German informant; he became "chief of the secret service" under Madero and helped defeat the 1912 Orozco rising. After Madero's murder he bought arms for Carranza and Villa — about $340,000 of arms funneled to Villa in 1915 — with a network of Mexican Americans, exiles and German agents (including Horst von der Goltz), and in 1915 proposed "creating an incident which would provoke a war between the U.S. and Mexico." In the Great War he worked for the German naval attaché Karl Boy-Ed in New York. Historians have found no proof that he was involved in the Columbus raid. He was interned at Fort Oglethorpe in June 1918 and released in 1919; after the 1930s his whereabouts are unknown (a 1942 address in draft records). His El Paso circle included two notorious soldiers of fortune, Sam Dreben ("el Judío Bélico", the "Fighting Jew" — a machine-gun expert who smuggled arms to Villa and did sabotage jobs for Sommerfeld) and Emil Lewis Holmdahl — the man later jailed for the theft of Villa's head (see M01).
  • The odd detail: the man who examined Bierce's disappearance for Villa (see the Bierce entry) was also the man who suggested manufacturing a war between the United States and Mexico — for Germany.
  • People: Felix A. Sommerfeld — German-born agent, Villa's/Madero's/Carranza's arms buyer; Sam Dreben — machine-gunner and gunrunner; Emil L. Holmdahl — mercenary; Horst von der Goltz — German agent; Karl Boy-Ed — German naval attaché.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_A._Sommerfeld · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Dreben (via search) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Lewis_Holmdahl (via search)
  • Confidence: High on Sommerfeld's biography (Wikipedia summary) and the arms figure; the war-provocation proposal is documented but its status as an intent is an interpretation.
  • Divergence check: the espionage is pre-1916, happened. In the setting Germany won and America never entered the Great War, so Sommerfeld's 1915 proposal to provoke a U.S.–Mexican war is exactly what happened next — the Southern War. His own fate (internment 1918 — for what, if there is no U.S. war with Germany?) BENDS: he would not have been interned.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the setting's best "Germany as patron" spine ready-made. A German agent, embedded first in Madero's government and then in Villa's arms trade, who tried to start the war that the setting says America fought. In the setting Sommerfeld wins: he is not interned, and 1930s Mexico's German-backed movements (Gold Shirts, Cedillo — see M01) have a real predecessor. His unknown fate after the 1930s is an open file.

Jack Johnson walks across the line at Tijuana — 20 July 1920

  • Where: the Tijuana–San Diego crossing; Leavenworth Penitentiary; Chicago (Joliet, Geneva jail).
  • What happened: The former heavyweight champion Jack Johnson, convicted in 1913 under the Mann Act by an all-white jury (a year and a day), had fled the country and spent seven years in Europe, South America and Mexico, fighting where he could. On 20 July 1920 he walked with his wife from Tijuana across the border and presented his passport to a San Diego County deputy sheriff. He was taken to Los Angeles, back to Chicago, held for months at Joliet and the Geneva jail, and entered Leavenworth on 19 September 1920 to a crowd of cheering inmates; he served one year.
  • The odd detail: the most-hunted Black man in America gave himself up by handing his passport to a county deputy sheriff at the line, with his wife beside him.
  • People: Jack Johnson — boxer; his wife; the San Diego County deputy sheriff who took the passport.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Johnson (via search) · https://nmaahc.si.edu/explore/stories/jack-johnson-heavyweight-boxer-defied-america (via search)
  • Confidence: High on the crossing and dates (agreeing search summaries; not read in full).
  • Divergence check: the surrender is at the Tijuana border (Baja status unruled); the Mann Act conviction and the Chicago holding are domestic. Holds (Chicago's story) — he passes through Joliet and Geneva in the Chicago book's own geography.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Black champion in exile in Mexico who came home and went to Leavenworth (where Flores Magón would die two years later). A Chicago link: his Joliet and Geneva months are Chicago-region history (his Chicago café, the Club De Luxe, c. 1912, is from memory — UNVERIFIED; check the Chicago sweep's R14 before use).

Caesar's, Tijuana — and the salad of 4 July 1924

  • Where: Caesar's Restaurant, Avenida Revolución, Tijuana.
  • What happened: During Prohibition Caesar (Cesare) Cardini, an Italian immigrant who had run restaurants in San Diego, moved to Tijuana and opened Caesar's, catering to Americans who wanted drinks and a meal legally. His daughter Rosa told the story that on 4 July 1924, a holiday rush having emptied the kitchen, her father improvised a salad of romaine, garlic-infused oil, coddled egg, lemon, Worcestershire sauce, Parmesan and croutons, tossed at the table for show. (The recipe was published by Rosa in 1986 [UNVERIFIED — V_M06: Wikipedia cites a 1987 interview with Rosa].) His Tijuana restaurant remained a destination for "early foodies" and Hollywood stars; Cardini opened in San Diego in 1936.
  • The odd detail: the signature dish of the Prohibition border is an accident of running out of food on the Fourth of July.
  • People: Caesar (Cesare) Cardini — restaurateur; Rosa Cardini — daughter and the story's source.
  • Sources: https://www.npr.org/2024/07/04/nx-s1-4993520-e1/this-july-4-marks-100-years-since-caesar-salad-was-invented-in-tijuana (via search; page timed out) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesar_Cardini (via search) · https://www.kpbs.org/news/2024/07/03/hail-caesar-salad-born-100-years-ago-in-tijuana (via search)
  • Confidence: Medium (the origin story is from the family; other claimants exist — check before print).
  • Divergence check: Prohibition holds (repeal 1933); Tijuana's Baja status is unruled (see the resort entry). The story is folk history and needs no change unless Baja is ceded.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a small, local, vivid, accidental invention — the kind of "odd tidbit" the owner asked for. A recipe as a cipher; a restaurant at the border where every faction eats.

The "Tijuana Bibles" — 1920s–1960s, printed in the United States

The name plays on the fictitious foreign addresses on them; they were said to come from Tijuana, Cuba, England or Canada but were made in the U.S.

Tayopa, the lost Jesuit silver mine — folklore, attested 1927 and 1939

Dobie later told the story in Apache Gold and Yaqui Silver (1939) (Dobie's 1939 book on Tayopa confirmed on Wikipedia).

  • People: C. B. Ruggles — Tayopa hunter; J. Frank Dobie — Texas folklorist; "Custard" — the man with the map.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tayopa · https://www.mgsrefining.com/blog/the-lost-silver-mines-of-tayopa/ (via search; the 1927 detail is from search summary of a treasure-hunting page — Medium)

  • Divergence check: pre-1916 legend; the location (Sonora/Chihuahua) is CEDED NORTH, so a "Mexican lost mine" is, in the setting, an American mining claim in a war zone.

Part M — The vice economies, in numbers

Ciudad Juárez, "El Paso's bar" — the Prohibition ledger, 1918–1933

  • Where: Ciudad Juárez, Chihuahua — the cantinas around the Santa Fe international bridge; the Kentucky Bar (founded 1920).
  • What happened: Texas went dry in late 1918 (the first state to do so) and the federal Eighteenth Amendment took effect in January 1920; El Paso's 1919 commercial directory lists no cantinas. Juárez filled the gap. By 25 April 1927 municipal records listed 126 registered cantinas, graded first, second or third class by furnishings and whether the staff spoke English. Named bars included the Kentucky Bar, El California, La Mascota, American Dancy, El Puerto de Yokohama, Casa Azul, El Oasis, Central Bar, Jockey Club, Black Cat, El Puerto de Bilbao and La Luz Colorada. The city's take: 119,391.60 pesos in liquor licences in 1921, plus fines (26,012.56 pesos in 1920; 41,981.81 in 1924); one study claims $200,000 a month flowed from El Paso to Juárez's cantinas and gambling houses. On 12 April 1921 the police recorded 391 arrests for public drunkenness (a daily average of 48), and in November–December 1920 50–60% of the drunks arrested were Anglo Americans. Police sorted them into ebrio escandaloso, ebrio tirado, ebrio-golpes, ebrio-insultos, ebrio impertinente. There were 114 registered prostitutes (all Mexican nationals) in 1921 and 153 in 1923. El Paso authorities closed the Santa Fe bridge at 9 p.m. and reopened it at 10 a.m. to keep the drunk from coming home. Recorded incidents: an American, Henry Spencer, stabbed in the face at the Central Bar (30 Nov 1920); C. B. Lindley robbed of $125 and jewellery at a cantina; A. J. Smith shot four times. Folklore, labelled: the Kentucky Bar says Kentucky distillers moved to Chihuahua and that Al Capone once stopped in for a drink (repeated in tourist and press pieces — no evidence read).
  • The odd detail: Juárez police kept a taxonomy of drunks in five kinds, and half the ones they arrested were Americans.
  • People: Henry Spencer, C. B. Lindley, A. J. Smith — Americans in the police blotter; the 114–153 registered sex workers; the municipal officials who taxed both.
  • Sources: https://www.redalyc.org/journal/4217/421744677005/html/ (Spanish-language study of Juárez's police and municipal records, read in full via fetch) · https://kvia.com/news/business-technology/2025/09/22/crossing-the-border-for-a-taste-of-history-at-the-legendary-bar-that-gave-us-the-margarita/ (via search; the Capone and Kentucky-distiller claims are tourist-press folklore)
  • Confidence: High on the municipal numbers (from records cited in a scholarly article); Low on the Capone visit and Kentucky distilleries (tourist folklore). The study itself notes gaps: no comprehensive murder statistics.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — CEDED NORTH. In the setting Ciudad Juárez is American after 1917, so the whole rationale of a foreign bar-town beside a dry Texas disappears: Juárez is a dry American city too (Prohibition holds domestically). Two setting fixes: (a) the cantinas persist as an occupation-era exemption (the occupier keeps its troops' liquor in the "Zone"), or (b) the drinking town moves to the Tamaulipas reach (Nuevo Laredo, Matamoros) — see the tequileros entry. The "Chicago" link (Capone at the Kentucky Bar) is a legend; if the owner likes it, it can be made true.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a police ledger that counts drunks, prostitutes and fines to the peso — a documentary artefact a GM can hand over. The "ebrio tirado" (passed-out drunk) is a perfect cover for anyone lying still in a cantina alley for a reason.

Colonel Esteban Cantú and the "Baja republic" — 1915–1920

  • Where: Mexicali and Tijuana, "Distrito Norte de la Baja California."
  • What happened: Col. Esteban Cantú governed the northern district of Baja California from 1915 to July/August 1920, formally appointed by Villa's and the Convention's side (a 20 January 1915 telegram from Villa is cited [UNVERIFIED — V_M06: not on the pages read; the 1915 Villa appointment is confirmed]) and then nominally loyal to Carranza — but "followed their orders only when it suited him," publishing Mexico City's laws to keep up appearances. When he took over, Tijuana and Mexicali had a "morass of crime and immorality" — prostitution, opium dens, cocaine, morphine and heroin — and Cantú licensed or profited from much of it while using the revenue for roads, government buildings and schools, including a road from Mexicali through Tijuana to Ensenada. He entertained the idea of Baja breaking away from Mexico, with American allies including the San Diego Sun, in what one popular account calls "a U.S. conspiracy, apparently led by Harry Chandler of the Los Angeles Times" (a claim, not proven). Gen. Abelardo L. Rodríguez was sent to oust him in July 1920 — the same Rodríguez who, as governor, would own the land under Agua Caliente.
  • The odd detail: the man who cleaned up Baja by taxing its vice was removed by the general who then built its most famous vice palace on his own land.
  • People: Esteban Cantú — governor and de facto ruler; Harry Chandler — LA Times publisher, alleged conspirator; Abelardo L. Rodríguez — general who removed him.
  • Sources: https://www.bajabound.com/bajaadventures/bajafever/two_leaders (via fetch; thin) · https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/206/monograph/book/79270 (Werne's book on Cantú, title only via search — the scholarly source to read)
  • Confidence: Medium (travel-website and search-summary sources; the "vice revenue for public works" summary is the standard reading; the Chandler conspiracy is a claim). Read Werne's monograph before using in print.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the setting's Baja question again. Cantú's regime (1915–20) spans the divergence: a Baja run as a semi-independent, American-financed vice-state is exactly what an American purchase or protectorate would inherit. If Baja is ceded (Colorado River Land Co.'s cotton is on the ceded-prize list), Cantú is the collaborator who handed it over; if it is not, he is the man who nearly did.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Golden Circle's "map" includes the Pacific coast; a licensed-vice republic on the border with LA money behind it is exactly what the Circle would buy. Chandler (LA Times, Colorado River Land Co.) is a real name with a real stake and a documented Mexicali empire — a natural Circle-adjacent figure (proposal, not fact).

Divergence questions this lane raises (for the owner; nothing settled here)

  1. Is Baja California ceded? It decides whether Agua Caliente, Tijuana's vice economy, Caesar's, Cantú's 1915–20 regime and Mexicali's Chinese are American or Mexican — and whether Prohibition-era border vice has a home in the setting at all. Recommendation: keep Baja Mexican (with Tamaulipas), so the border-vice story survives; make Mexicali's cotton an American-owned concession.
  2. What is the setting's remaining international line? With Sonora, Chihuahua and Coahuila American, the only stretches that still divide America from Mexico are Baja and the Tamaulipas reach (Laredo–Matamoros). The Border Patrol, tequileros, smugglers and deportees all live there.
  3. Who is a "Mexican" in the ceded north? The Repatriation, the bath cordon, the marijuana ordinance and the Chinese Exclusion regime all turn on it. One ruling covers all four.
  4. Does the setting keep the Espionage Act, or write a Southern War security law? Flores Magón's 20-year sentence, Bisbee's "German agents," and Sommerfeld's internment all depend on it.
  5. Sommerfeld's fate. In the record he is interned (1918) and vanishes (1930s). In the setting Germany won — is he free, a Berlin man, or a Circle contact?
  6. Zyklon B at the border. A real 1920s–30s fumigant used on Mexican workers; in a world with no Nazi Germany the name carries no shadow — the owner should decide whether to use it as a plain fact.
