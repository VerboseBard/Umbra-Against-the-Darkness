M09 — Tampico and Veracruz, 1900–1940: the setting's two enclaves, in depth

  • Lane: M09 — the Tampico oil coast (held outright in the setting) and the port of Veracruz (Mexican flag, American customs receivership in the setting): oil, oilmen, unions, the Huasteca and its Tének people, fever, Traven; the 1914 landing in detail, San Juan de Ulúa, the 1922 tenant strike, Afro-Veracruz and son jarocho, Catemaco's brujos.
  • Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent) · Brief: BRIEF.md in this folder + the Chicago sweep's BRIEF.md (entry format, sourcing rules).
  • Setting sources read first: BRIEF.md; ALTERNATE_HISTORY.md §7.4 (the oil is at Tampico) and §29.4 item 4 (RULED 2026-09-22, owner: "Keep Tampico, hold Veracruz's customs house" — "held by the ledger"); SESSION_2026-09-08_AUDITS.md (the ports ruling); M01 of this sweep (skimmed; not repeated — cross-referenced).

How to read the divergence lines. As in M01: TAMPICO ENCLAVE (held outright by America after the 1917 treaty; where its landward line runs — how much of the Golden Lane and the Huasteca it swallows — is not settled, and this file flags it wherever it matters), VERACRUZ (Mexican flag; the customs house collected by Americans, guarded by Marines, applied to the indemnity), UNCEDED MEXICO (everything else). Before March 1916 everything happened as recorded.

Overview (36 entries). Both ports have a real prehistory that fits their setting roles almost too well. Tampico was an American-run place before any treaty: Doheny's 3,000 machete men at El Ébano (1901); the two companies holding ~3 million acres by 1922; wells that ran wild (Potrero del Llano, 1910) and burned for six months after lightning, then were sealed in concrete bunkers; paymaster launches robbed on a jungle canal and four Americans killed at the Prieto terminal for $5,562 with "no apparent cause" (the US consul's own words, FRUS 1918); terminals with their own armed men; a Wobbly port that stopped 15,000 workers in 1917; a pleasure quarter whose best-known dance hall was entered through a giant devil's face; B. Traven's Oso Negro flophouse; and — inland — the Tének, a Mayan people among whom the witch is the one who takes the common wealth for himself. Smedley Butler himself said in 1935 that he made "especially Tampico safe for American oil interests." Veracruz supplies the receivership's precedent to the peso: in 1914 the Americans ran the customs house and carried 2,604,051.20 pesos home in a sealed safe and a wooden box. Under it: the Naval Academy's afternoon (Azueta refusing American doctors), San Juan de Ulúa's cells named Glory, Purgatory, Hell and Limbo, the Mulata de Córdoba who sailed a charcoal ship through her cell wall (legend), a rent strike started by prostitutes burning their mattresses and ended by a massacre (4 dead officially, 150 by the union's count, some "thrown into the sea"), the carnival that hunts and tries Mal Humor, the Inquisition's 1766 ban on the chuchumbé, Yanga's 1932 renaming, the Rockefeller mosquito war (and Noguchi's wrong germ), 1923's rebel capital, and 1931 as the port's bad year (the cathedral shooting, the Catemaco church sacked, the Independence Day hurricane). Catemaco's "witch town" is mostly post-1970 (the brujo congress dates from 1970); no pre-1940 attestation of its witch reputation was found — labelled. Biggest open question for the owner: where the Tampico enclave's landward line runs — El Ébano, Potrero del Llano, Cerro Azul, Tamuín, Castillo de Teayo and Poza Rica all sit on one side or the other, and several entries turn on it. Second: who holds San Juan de Ulúa under the receivership.

Part A — Tampico: the oil coast (1900–1938)

Doheny at El Ébano — the first Mexican wells — 1900 – 1901

  • Where: El Ébano, San Luis Potosí — about 35 miles west of Tampico, on the rail line; the Pánuco and Tamesí rivers carried the country out to the port.
  • What happened: The Californian Edward L. Doheny (with his partner Charles Canfield) bought into the jungle country west of Tampico in 1900 and drilled Mexico's first commercial oil wells at El Ébano in 1901 under his Mexican Petroleum Company. The historian Jonathan C. Brown counts three thousand Mexican labourers clearing jungle at El Ébano and a 400-foot railroad siding in place by 1902; Doheny fired an American foreman for "impatience" toward the Mexican crews. In 1905 he bought land near Tuxpan and formed the Huasteca Petroleum Company to hold it; in September 1910 his men brought in Juan Casiano No. 7 in the Golden Lane, the Huasteca company's mainstay for nine years. Everything later — Cerro Azul No. 4 (M01), Pan American Petroleum (sold to Standard Oil of Indiana, April 1925), Teapot Dome — grows from this siding.
  • The odd detail: the Mexican oil age began not at the port but thirty-five miles inland in the bush, where Doheny needed a small army of machete men before he could drill a single hole.
  • People: Edward L. Doheny — founder, Mexican Petroleum / Huasteca (PEOPLE §6); Charles A. Canfield — partner; Herbert Wylie — Doheny's driller (Casiano, Cerro Azul).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_L._Doheny · https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/view?docId=ft3q2nb28s&brand=ucpress (Jonathan C. Brown, Oil and Revolution in Mexico, UC Press, ch. 1 "Drilling to the Source") · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_American_Petroleum_and_Transport_Company (via search)
  • Confidence: High (1901, El Ébano, Huasteca 1905, Casiano Sept 1910); the 3,000 labourers are Brown's figure, read this session.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. El Ébano is in San Luis Potosí, inland: whether it falls inside the TAMPICO ENCLAVE depends on the enclave's unsettled landward line — flag it. Doheny's later career BENDS (Teapot Dome needs Harding and Fall's Interior Department — see Part A entry on Greystone).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): three thousand men cutting jungle for a Californian who had been told there was oil under the tar seeps — what else did they cut into? The Huastec name for the seeps (chapopote) and what the villages kept away from.

Potrero del Llano No. 4 — the well that ran wild, then burned — 27 December 1910; fire August 1914

  • Where: Potrero del Llano, near Tuxpan, northern Veracruz — the southern Golden Lane, some 300 km north-west of the port of Veracruz.
  • What happened: Pearson's Mexican Eagle (El Águila) company brought in Potrero del Llano No. 4 on 27 December 1910; it threw oil 250 feet into the air and ran wild for roughly two to three months at an estimated 100,000–110,000 barrels a day, the oil caught in earthen pits dug around it. Capping began in late January 1911. Tamed, it gave up to 45,000 barrels a day and more than 25 million barrels in its first three years — the most productive well in the Western Hemisphere until Cerro Azul. In August 1914 an electrical storm ignited the seepages around the well and the fire burned for six months; afterwards a concrete bunker was built around the wellhead, both against lightning and "to help defend it from revolutionists." El Águila's young geologist on the ground was Everette Lee DeGolyer.
  • The odd detail: a lightning strike set the ground itself on fire around the well, and the company answered by entombing the wellhead in concrete — a blockhouse against the sky and the rebels at once.
  • People: Weetman Pearson (Lord Cowdray) — owner, El Águila; Everette Lee DeGolyer — El Águila geologist 1910–14, later a founder of American geophysics.
  • Sources: https://petroleumhistoryblog.com/2016/06/16/potrero-del-llano-no-4-oil-gusher/ · https://digitalcollections.smu.edu/digital/collection/mex/id/1511/ (SMU DeGolyer Library photograph "Potrero del Llano No. 4 fire" — title seen via search) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everette_Lee_DeGolyer (via search)
  • Confidence: High on date, height, flow and the 1914 fire/bunker; the depth (1,911 ft) is from a search summary only ⟨fixed: V_M09 — oilystuff.com gives 1,932 ft; sources differ, treat depth as UNVERIFIED⟩. The 1914 concrete bunker (foot-thick walls, on the 1911 concrete foundation, "to protect the well head from further lightening strikes and to help defend it from revolutionists") is confirmed at https://www.oilystuff.com/single-post/mexico-viii.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Tuxpan district, northern Veracruz state — inside or outside the TAMPICO ENCLAVE depending on how far south the owner draws the line (the Golden Lane runs Tampico → Tuxpan). Note: SMU's DeGolyer Library holds period photographs of the gusher and the fire (Image: https://digitalcollections.smu.edu/digital/collection/mex/id/1458/ — rights per SMU; not downloaded).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the concrete bunker on the wellhead is a perfect set piece: a sealed room in the middle of an oil field, built after the ground burned for six months — what was it really built to keep in?

The paymaster launches of the Chijol Canal — 29 September and 27 December 1917

  • Where: the Chijol Canal — the government-built waterway (begun 1901) running about 120 km south from Tampico through the oil fields toward Tuxpan.
  • What happened: The oil camps were paid in cash carried down the canal by company launches. On 29 September 1917 four bandits stepped out of the bush and boarded El Águila's launch Afridi, taking $23,406 in Mexican gold from Paymaster J. Hess. On 27 December 1917 El Águila's launch Basuto was fired on "as she came in sight"; Paymaster A. E. Schmidt lost $40,710.66 and the engineer Manuel Viano was wounded. On 21 February 1918 a party of "about 40 men, all well armed" struck the paymasters of several companies on the same canal: the Texas Company's paymaster Edgar House — whose shotgun the bandits spotted and demanded — was "afraid to surrender the gun and prepared to defend himself" and "was killed in the fusillade" ⟨fixed: V_M09 — FRUS does not say he fired first⟩; Paymaster Minnett (wounded in three places), Dr. Brisbane (seriously), Prather and a Mexican launch boy were wounded; about 14,000 pesos were taken. The US consul at Tampico, Claude I. Dawson, catalogued it all for Washington in August 1918.
  • The odd detail: the launches carried British-Indian frontier names — Afridi, Basuto — Pearson's company naming its pay-boats after the empire's warrior peoples, and being robbed on a jungle canal regardless.
  • People: J. Hess, A. E. Schmidt — El Águila paymasters; Edgar House — Texas Co. paymaster, killed; Manuel Viano — wounded engineer; Claude I. Dawson — US consul at Tampico.
  • Sources: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1918/d615 (FRUS 1918, File 312.115/341, Dawson to Secretary of State, 5 Aug 1918) · https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1918/d616 (Ambassador Fletcher's list: "Edgar House: Murdered Tampico, February 21, 1918")
  • Confidence: High — a primary diplomatic document read this session.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — TAMPICO ENCLAVE. In the setting 1917 is the war's end and the treaty year: these robberies fall in the transition from war to occupation. Bandits on the canal become the first guerrillas of the second era; the paymaster launches sail under Navy escort, and "consul" Dawson is instead the enclave's first civil administrator (a role the setting has not named).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a pay-launch found drifting on the Chijol Canal with the strongbox full and the crew gone — the one robbery on the consul's list where nothing was taken.

The Prieto terminal killings — 29 June 1918

  • Where: the Prieto terminal of the Mexican Gulf Oil Company, on the north bank of the Pánuco about 4 miles above Tampico's fiscal wharf.
  • What happened: Five armed Mexicans robbed the terminal of 9,427.81 pesos ($5,562.40) and killed four Americans — the machinist Alfred Esparcia, assistant cashier L. R. Millard, warehouse clerk L. A. Dunn and commissary clerk R. M. Cooper — and the Mexican foreman Natividad Flores. Consul Dawson stressed that "no resistance was made by any of the men at the terminal." Mexico's Foreign Office replied on 17 July 1918 that it had directed the authorities to arrest and punish the guilty. Dawson's covering report carries a line a GM can use verbatim ⟨fixed: V_M09 — it is in the report's opening section, not its first sentence⟩: banditry "has become so rife all through this territory that it has largely ceased to be the subject of comment and is accepted as a part of each day's experiences, even within the limits of the city of Tampico."
  • The odd detail: five men dead for five and a half thousand dollars, with no one resisting — the consul himself could find "no apparent cause" for the killing.
  • People: Alfred Esparcia, L. R. Millard, L. A. Dunn, R. M. Cooper — Americans killed; Natividad Flores — Mexican foreman killed; Claude I. Dawson — consul.
  • Sources: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1918/d615 · https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1918/d616
  • Confidence: High (names and sums from FRUS). (Search summaries give Millard's initials as "L. H." — the FRUS page read this session says L. R.)
  • Divergence check: BENDS — TAMPICO ENCLAVE, 1918: in the setting this is inside an American-held port a year into the occupation — the kind of killing that sets the enclave's curfew and its fences.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "no apparent cause" is the classic three-layers opening — the period reads robbery, the river folk read something else, and the terminal's ledger shows the money was the least of what went missing.

The night attack on the Eureka and Corona terminals — 20 August 1918

  • Where: oil terminals three miles from Tampico — the Eureka Coast Oil Co. (American) and the Corona company (Royal Dutch Shell).
  • What happened: The consul at Tampico telegraphed at 6 p.m. on 21 August 1918 that "banditti last night attacked terminals of Eureka Coast Oil Co., American, and Corona Co., Dutch Shell," assaulted the wife of a British employee of the Dutch company, and "were finally beaten off by armed employees of Eureka Coast Co. Several Mexicans killed or wounded."
  • The odd detail: by 1918 the terminals had their own armed employees able to fight a night battle — the companies were already their own garrisons.
  • People: Claude I. Dawson — consul (sender).
  • Sources: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1918/d617 (FRUS 1918, File 812.00/22178)
  • Confidence: High (telegram text); the victims are unnamed in the source.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — TAMPICO ENCLAVE. Shell's presence is itself a bend question: in the setting a defeated, bankrupt Britain (ALTERNATE_HISTORY §30.8) may have sold El Águila and Corona cheap — to Americans, or to the Dutch, whose companies the setting has America buying distressed (§29.3). Flag for the owner.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): armed company men who beat off a night attack and then refused to say what they had been shooting at — a Hunter Guild recruiting ground.

Peláez's country and the oilmen's "white guards" — 1915 – 1920 (cross-ref M01)

  • Where: the Huasteca between Tampico and Tuxpan — the Golden Lane.
  • What happened: M01 has the core entry (Gen. Manuel Peláez taxing the companies to keep Carranza out). Added here: by 1922 the two giants held staggering acreage — Huasteca Petroleum owned or leased 1,223,780 acres and El Águila 1,890,286 acres (⟨fixed: V_M09 — the figures are in Wikipedia's Petroleum industry in Mexico, "Development before 1938", ref. 17; the libcom IWW article does not contain them⟩). Search summaries of Peláez's article say his men "intimidated, killed, stole, and set fire to Carrancista garrisons and oil company properties at will" — i.e. the companies paid protection to the same army that burned what it chose.
  • The odd detail: three million acres of Mexico in two companies' hands, guarded by a native general the companies paid and could not control.
  • People: Manuel Peláez — the oilmen's general; William F. Buckley Sr. — the lawyer who backed him (next entry).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum_industry_in_Mexico (acreage; added by V_M09) · https://libcom.org/article/re-remembering-mexican-iww · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Pel%C3%A1ez (via search)
  • Confidence: Medium (acreage from a secondary article; the "at will" line from a search summary).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — TAMPICO ENCLAVE. In the setting Peláez is the obvious native collaborator (M01). After 1917 his white guards become either the enclave's constabulary or its first insurgents.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Peláez's guards as the Golden Circle's native auxiliary (M01's proposal) — the setting's "cult versus population": a few hundred armed men, a whole region of ordinary villagers paying for them.

William F. Buckley Sr., the Tampico lawyer thrown out of Mexico — 1908 – 1921

  • Where: Tampico, Tamaulipas (Buckley & Buckley's office from 1912); the Pantepec Oil Company, Tampico (founded 1913).
  • What happened: The Texan lawyer William F. Buckley Sr. moved to Mexico in 1908, founded Buckley & Buckley with his brother Claude to represent American and European oil companies, opened a Tampico office with his brother Edmund in 1912 and founded the Pantepec Oil Company in 1913. He backed Peláez's revolt and lobbied (through the American Association of Mexico) to amend Article 27 of the 1917 Constitution, which vested subsoil oil in the nation. In 1921 the Obregón government expelled him: he was allowed to gather his personal belongings and a few law books in Tampico, put on a ship, and his Mexican assets were seized. (His son William F. Buckley Jr., born 1925, spoke Spanish as his first language.)
  • The odd detail: the founder of an American conservative dynasty was deported from Tampico with only "a few law books."
  • People: William F. Buckley Sr. — oil lawyer, Pantepec founder; Claude and Edmund Buckley — brothers and partners; Álvaro Obregón — the president who expelled him.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_F._Buckley_Sr. (via search) · https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/buckley-william-frank (via search)
  • Confidence: Medium–High (consistent across two search summaries; not read in full).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — TAMPICO ENCLAVE. In the setting Obregón (a Sonoran from the ceded north — M01's key finding) cannot expel anyone from an American enclave. Buckley's type becomes the enclave's own: the lawyer who drafts its land titles and sits on its governing council.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the enclave's land-claims lawyer, whose office holds every Huastec village's colonial title deed — and knows which ones the companies bought, and which ones they could not.

Greystone: the oil money comes home to a guest bedroom — November 1921; 16 February 1929

  • Where: the Wardman Park Hotel, Washington (1921); Greystone Mansion, Beverly Hills (1929).
  • What happened: The Tampico fortune built Greystone — 55 rooms, 46,000 sq ft, over $3.1 million, finished in 1928 for Doheny's son Edward Jr. ("Ned"). In November 1921 Ned and his secretary and boyhood friend Hugh Plunkett had carried $100,000 in cash to Interior Secretary Albert B. Fall at the Wardman Park Hotel — the loan at the heart of Teapot Dome (the Elk Hills naval reserve lease). Both were due to testify when, on 16 February 1929, they were found shot dead in a Greystone guest bedroom. The official story: Plunkett, "nervous," killed Ned and himself. But the gun was Ned's, and Ned was not buried in the family's Catholic cemetery; the two men lie at Forest Lawn, Glendale, a few hundred yards apart. Doheny Sr. was twice acquitted of bribery; Fall was convicted of taking it. Doheny died 8 September 1935; his widow destroyed his papers.
  • The odd detail: a Catholic oil king's son is buried outside consecrated ground — the family's own verdict, whatever the coroner said.
  • People: Edward L. Doheny Sr. — the Tampico oilman; Ned Doheny — son; Hugh Plunkett — secretary; Albert B. Fall — Interior Secretary (who chaired the Senate's Mexican-affairs inquiry, 1919–20 — UNVERIFIED this session).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greystone_Mansion · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_L._Doheny
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — domestic. Teapot Dome needs President Harding and his Interior Secretary Fall; Harding is never president in the setting (Wood 1921–27, Dawes 1927–29). The $100,000 needs a different recipient — Leonard Wood's own Interior man, or a naval officer at the Tampico enclave (the enclave's naval oil reserve is a natural substitute for Elk Hills). Greystone and the 1929 deaths can stand as dated.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): in the setting the black-bag money goes to buy the Tampico naval reserve, not Elk Hills — and the Greystone deaths are the Secret Service's cold case: the two men who carried the cash die in the week before they testify.

The Wobblies of Tampico — April 1916 – July 1920

  • Where: Tampico and the oil terminals; the port's docks and lighters.
  • What happened: Tampico was the Mexican stronghold of the Industrial Workers of the World and the anarcho-syndicalist Casa del Obrero Mundial (COM) — the idea likely arriving with sailors of the IWW's Marine Transport Workers' Industrial Union. In April 1916 protests over living conditions became a strike that shut most oil companies and public services. In April 1917 an IWW-led strike against El Águila spread to at least six other oil companies, the longshoremen and boatmen — a general strike of 15,000 workers that halted all oil production and was violently repressed. Another large strike followed in November 1917; in July 1920 the IWW and COM led a general strike of 10,000 oil workers. Named organisers include Pedro Coria (from Arizona), Ramón Parreño, Francisco Gamallo, Rafael Zamudio, Víctor Martínez and José Zapata. B. Traven, arriving 1924, absorbed this world; his first Mexican novel was published in Germany as Der Wobbly.
  • The odd detail: the world's second-busiest oil port [UNVERIFIED — V_M09: no source read gives Tampico this rank] was stopped cold, twice in a year (April and November 1917), by a union whose idea came in on the ships.
  • People: Pedro Coria — IWW organiser from Arizona; Ramón Parreño, Francisco Gamallo, Rafael Zamudio, Víctor Martínez, José Zapata — Tampico organisers.
  • Sources: https://libcom.org/article/re-remembering-mexican-iww · https://files.libcom.org/files/Re-remembering%20the%20Mexican%20IWW.pdf (via search)
  • Confidence: High for dates and numbers as reported by the article; names Medium (spellings from one source).
  • Divergence check: April 1916 happened (pre-war). BENDS — TAMPICO ENCLAVE from 1917: the April 1917 general strike lands in the war's last months in the setting — a strike in an enclave under American guns is a military matter. Note the setting's record: no Soviet Union, no Red Scare — so the IWW is an American labour problem, not a "Bolshevik" one; the period's word for them in the setting is "anarchist" or "Wobbly," not "Red."
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Wobbly sailors carry news and contraband between Tampico, Galveston, New Orleans and Havana — the Golden Circle's own map, run by its enemies. A union hall that knows which tankers carry more than oil.

The Barrio de la Unión — Tampico's pleasure district — c. 1914 – 1959

  • Where: the Barrio de la Unión, central Tampico (later displaced to "El Triángulo" near the markets).
  • What happened: A local-history feature in Milenio (Tampico) says no place in Mexico was "so famous for its prostitution and good atmosphere in its bars, cantinas, dance halls and gambling houses as Tampico," with the boom from 1914 and the decline after 1925. Named houses include the Montparnasse and the Tívoli — the latter with "una enorme cara de diablo" (an enormous devil's face) as its façade — and establishments run by women (María de la O; Casa Lola). Foreign women came with the oil money; Agustín Lara played and sang there. The interim governor Emilio Portes Gil wrote to President Calles about the violence. Relocation was first discussed in 1938; the last houses were closed in February 1959.
  • The odd detail: the Tívoli's door was a giant devil's mouth — you entered the oil port's best-known dance hall through the Devil's face.
  • People: Agustín Lara — composer, played the district; Emilio Portes Gil — Tamaulipas governor, later president (1928–30); María de la O — named proprietor.
  • Sources: https://www.milenio.com/cultura/tampico-barrio-union-cuna-prostitucion-lujo
  • Confidence: Medium (one local-history article, read; names and dates as it gives them).
  • Divergence check: BENDS — TAMPICO ENCLAVE. An American-held oil port with a Mexican pleasure quarter is the Canal Zone's or Havana's pattern: Navy shore patrols, a VD clinic, a "tolerated zone" drawn on an American map. Portes Gil (a Tamaulipas man, from outside the enclave) still exists but no longer governs Tampico.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Tívoli's devil's-face door is ready-made for the occult layer — a dance hall whose entrance is a mouth. Vampires in high society have a low-society feeding ground, and the shore patrol's missing-sailor reports are the lead.

B. Traven's Tampico and the Hotel Oso Negro — 1924 – 1927

  • Where: Tampico and the oil fields; an agricultural settlement north of the city (search summaries name it Columbus, today Cuauhtémoc, Altamira — UNVERIFIED).
  • What happened: The mystery author "B. Traven" arrived in Mexico in the summer of 1924 and lived in and around Tampico in the 1920s, working as the heroes of his books do — cotton picker, oilfield roustabout, cattle driver, baker, waiter (the five jobs of The Cotton-Pickers, 1926; first published in Germany as Der Wobbly). The Death Ship (1926) and The Treasure of the Sierra Madre (German 1927; English 1935) followed; Treasure opens in Tampico, where Dobbs and Curtin scrounge for oil-field work and sleep at the Hotel Oso Negro ("Black Bear"), a vermin-ridden flophouse where they hear old Howard's gold stories. Traven was very probably Ret Marut, a German actor and anarchist editor (Der Ziegelbrenner) who fled the suppression of the Bavarian Soviet Republic in 1919; in 1926 he was the photographer of a UNAM expedition to Chiapas. John Huston's 1948 film won three Oscars; Traven dealt with it as "Hal Croves."
  • The odd detail: the greatest American treasure-curse story of the century was written in German by an anonymous anarchist fugitive who had worked the Tampico oil fields.
  • People: B. Traven / Ret Marut / Traven Torsvan / Hal Croves — author; Dobbs, Curtin, Howard — his characters.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._Traven · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Treasure_of_the_Sierra_Madre (via search) · https://literalmagazine.com/timothy-heyman-on-the-incomparable-legacy-of-the-german-mexican-novelist-b-traven/
  • Confidence: High on the books and dates; Medium on the Columbus farm (search summary only); the Marut identification is the scholarly consensus, not proven.
  • Divergence check: BENDS. The Naval Academy's afternoon — 21 April 1914

  • Where: the Heroica Escuela Naval Militar, Veracruz waterfront; the corner of Landero y Cos and Esteban Morales streets; the rail line to Tejería.
  • What happened: Around noon Commodore Manuel Azueta came to the academy and urged its roughly 90 cadets and instructors to fight; the director, Capt. Rafael Carrión, organised the defence. When Admiral Fletcher sent American doctors to the wounded Azueta, he threw them out — "let those dogs get out, I don't want to see them." He died on 10 May 1914. San Juan de Ulúa — the fortress-prison of "the jars" — 1500s – 2 July 1915

  • Where: San Juan de Ulúa, the island fortress in Veracruz harbour.
  • What happened: Spain's harbour fortress became Mexico's most feared prison. Its dungeons were called las tinajas ("the jars") for their damp, and named Gloria, Purgatorio, Infierno and Limbo. Benito Juárez was held there ten days incommunicado in 1853, in flooded cells, before Santa Anna deported him. Under Díaz the political prisoners of the 1906 risings (Acayucan) went there: César E. Canales (May 1909) described cells "narrow, unventilated, dark, damp, pestilent and full of vermin"; Enrique Novoa, after three years in El Infierno, told of asphyxiating heat, rats against his body at night and a liver swollen from hunger; Cipriano Medina described floods to the knee and inmates like "walking skeletons," 20–30 to a cell, fed on scraps from the city's restaurants. Legend puts the bandit Chucho el Roto there (his death date is disputed — 1885 or 1894 — UNVERIFIED). Carranza closed the prison by decree on 2 July 1915 (search summary) and used the fortress as a presidential residence.
  • The odd detail: the cells were named for the stations of the Catholic afterlife — a prisoner was sent to Purgatory or Hell by address.
  • People: Benito Juárez — prisoner 1853; César E. Canales, Enrique Novoa, Cipriano Medina — 1906-rising prisoners who testified; Chucho el Roto (Jesús Arriaga) — legendary gentleman-bandit.
  • Sources: https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/san-juan-de-ulua-testimonios-de-los-presos.html · https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=104279 (via search; 403 on fetch)

The Veracruz carnival reborn — Saturday 21 February 1925

  • Where: the central city of Veracruz, from outside the railway workers' union hall.
  • What happened: After decades of official discouragement (since the 1880s, as a "vestige of Spanish colonialism and a threat to social order"), Veracruz revived public Carnival in 1925. The chuchumbé — the dance the Inquisition banned — denounced 20 August 1766

  • Where: Veracruz — the root of son jarocho.
  • What happened: The first record of the chuchumbé's verses is a denunciation made in Veracruz on 20 August 1766 by the Mercedarian friar Nicolás Montero (the accounts disagree on whether a friar was also the offender). An Inquisition edict of 31 October 1766 banned it as "scandalous, obscene and offensive"; the ban was renewed until about 1802. The dance and its satirical, sexual verses came from the Afro-descendant population of Cuba and Mexico; son jarocho (the music of the Sotavento — jarana, requinto, the wooden tarima platform the dancers stamp on) grew from it and its cousins. The word jarocho was a colonial caste term for people of African and Indigenous descent.
  • The odd detail: the ancestor of "La Bamba" was a banned song, first known to history from the Inquisition's own denunciation file.
  • People: Fray Nicolás Montero — the denouncer.
  • Sources: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuchumb%C3%A9 (via search) · https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/opinion/mochilazo-en-el-tiempo/los-bailes-prohibidos-por-la-santa-inquisicion/ (via search) · https://bajio.quadratin.com.mx/agn-recuerda-prohibicion-historica-del-chuchumbe/ (via search)

The Rockefeller mosquito war — Veracruz, 1920 – 1923

  • Where: the port of Veracruz and the Gulf coast (Tampico included in the Gulf campaign — extent UNVERIFIED).

Catemaco and the brujos of the Tuxtlas — folklore; the witch congress is 1970

  • Where: Catemaco, on Lake Catemaco in Los Tuxtlas, southern Veracruz; the Cerro del Mono Blanco; Nanciyaga.
  • What happened: Catemaco is called Mexico's witchcraft capital: brujos and curanderos (white and black, in the tourist distinction) offering cures, love magic and limpias. Label carefully: the famous annual Congress of Brujos (first Friday of March) was founded in 1970 by the "brujo mayor" Gonzalo Aguirre, and the town's witch tourism dates from the 1970s; the railway reached Catemaco only in 1912 and roads in the 1950s.
