M01 — Mexico, 1910–1940: the real record under the Southern War

  • Lane: M01 — the Mexican Revolution to 1916, the border war that seeds the setting's Southern War, Tampico and Veracruz, the Yaqui, Zimmermann, then the real 1920s–30s (Obregón, Calles, the Cristero War and the destruction of churches, Obregón's murder, Garrido Canabal's Red Shirts, Cárdenas and the 1938 oil expropriation), with murders, disasters and the American press's framing.
  • Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent) · Brief: BRIEF.md in this folder + the Chicago sweep's BRIEF.md (entry format, sourcing rules).
  • Setting sources read first: UMBRA_RULEBOOK.md §31 (the hemisphere row, the presidents) and Appendix 9–11; ALTERNATE_HISTORY.md §0, §3, §7.3–7.4, §8, §9, §28, §29, §30.8–30.9; PEOPLE_OF_THE_TIMELINE.md §3, §6, §8.

How to read the divergence lines. Every entry says where it falls against the setting's treaty line: CEDED NORTH (Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila, Nuevo León/Monterrey — American after the 1917 treaty), TAMPICO ENCLAVE (held outright), VERACRUZ (Mexican flag, American customs receivership), or UNCEDED MEXICO (everything else — Mexico City, Jalisco, Michoacán, Guanajuato, Tabasco, Yucatán…). Before March 1916 everything happened as recorded; from the Columbus raid onward the setting's Southern War takes over the record.

Overview (44 entries). The real record under the Southern War is richer than the setting needs. The Revolution began with a Spiritist (Madero took dictation, he believed, from his dead brother and Benito Juárez) and its first president was murdered in a deal hosted by the American embassy. The Southern War's seed is exact: Santa Isabel (Jan 1916), Columbus (9 March 1916), Pershing's column, Patton's bodies on the car hoods, and Carrizal (21 June 1916) — the hinge where the record stops (Wilson refused to march on Chihuahua) and the setting's war begins. Tampico (1914 affair, the 1908 Dos Bocas fire, Cerro Azul's 598-ft gusher, Peláez's oilmen-paid "white guards") and Veracruz (the 1914 customs house, the vultures, the 1931 cathedral shooting) both have a real prehistory for their setting roles. The owner's "destruction of old churches" is abundant and exactly dated: the Calles Law (priests 4,500 → 334), Christ the King dynamited (or bombed) on the hill traditionally called the geographic centre of Mexico (30 Jan 1928) ⟨fixed: V_M01⟩, Fr. Pro's photographed execution, a church turned into a cockpit at Sahuayo, Garrido Canabal's saint-burnings and demolished Villahermosa cathedral, the Coyoacán shootings and the champagne [UNVERIFIED — V_M01]. The biggest finding for the setting: almost every major Mexican figure of 1920–36 (Obregón, Calles, de la Huerta) is a Sonoran — from the ceded north — and several real events cannot happen as recorded because they sit on the American side of the line (Maldonado 1937, the Yaqui campaign, the 1931 Chinese expulsion, the 1929 Escobar rising, Villa's death at Parral, the 1938 expropriation at Tampico). Part E lists the ten divergence questions this raises. Germany as patron recurs on its own: the Dresden at Tampico (1914), Zimmermann (1917), the Gold Shirts' embassy money (1935), the Cedillo accusation (1938).

Part A — The Revolution's arc to 1916 (happened as recorded)

The Ten Tragic Days and the murder of Madero — 9–22 February 1913

  • Where: Mexico City — the Ciudadela arsenal, the Zócalo and National Palace, the US Embassy, and the ground behind Lecumberri penitentiary.
  • What happened: On 9 February 1913 military cadets freed Gen. Bernardo Reyes, who rode on the National Palace and was shot from his horse; some 400 died in the first clash. Félix Díaz (Porfirio's nephew) held the Ciudadela and shelled downtown for ten days. Gen. Victoriano Huerta, commanding the government's defence, changed sides; the "Pact of the Embassy" was brokered at the US Embassy by Ambassador Henry Lane Wilson on the night of 18–19 February (Spanish sources say 18 Feb, English 19 Feb) ⟨fixed: V_M01⟩, making Huerta provisional president. On the night of 22 February President Francisco Madero and Vice President José María Pino Suárez were shot near Lecumberri, officially "during a rescue attempt" (the ley fuga). Madero's brother Gustavo was seized at a restaurant, killed and thrown into a hole.
  • The odd detail: the dead in the streets piled up so fast that the authorities burned the bodies in heaps rather than bury them — the capital's centre smelled of it for days.
  • People: Francisco I. Madero — the revolution's first president, a spiritist (see next entry); Victoriano Huerta — the general who betrayed him; Henry Lane Wilson — the American ambassador who hosted the deal; Félix Díaz — nephew of the old dictator.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Tragic_Days
  • Confidence: High (the body-burning and Wilson's role are standard; exact casualty totals vary by source).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. UNCEDED MEXICO (the capital). Note for the setting: the American Wilson in the Embassy is Henry Lane, not Woodrow — Woodrow was inaugurated 4 March 1913, ten days later, and recalled him.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): an American embassy that brokers a murder in its own drawing room is the Golden Circle's style before the Circle has a map to follow. Three layers: the period says coup; the folklore says Madero's spirits warned him; the files say who was in the room.

Madero the spiritist — 1900s–1913

  • Where: Coahuila (San Pedro de las Colonias, his family's estates) and Mexico City.
  • What happened: Francisco Madero, the wealthy Coahuila landowner whose Plan of San Luis Potosí called the Revolution for 6 p.m. on 20 November 1910, was a devoted Spiritist. As a student in Paris he made a pilgrimage to the tomb of Allan Kardec, Spiritism's founder. From 1901 he believed the spirit of his brother Raúl, dead at age four (Mexico News Daily says three) ⟨fixed: V_M01⟩, was communicating with him, urging charity and self-discipline — and, after political violence in 1903, urging him to "aspire to do good for your fellow citizens." He spent 1908 writing The Presidential Succession of 1910 (published January 1909) at the direction, he believed, of spirits — "now including that of Benito Juárez himself."
  • The odd detail: the man who toppled a 34-year dictatorship wrote the book that launched him by automatic dictation from a dead child and a dead president.
  • People: Francisco I. Madero — president 1911–13, murdered 1913; Raúl Madero — the brother who died at four and "wrote" to him; Benito Juárez — the dead president among his spirit guides.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_I._Madero
  • Confidence: High on the spiritism, Kardec, Raúl and Juárez. (A Spiritist Manual under the pen name "Bhima" is widely cited — UNVERIFIED this session.)
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Coahuila is CEDED NORTH after 1917: the Revolution's founder's home country becomes American territory.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the setting's own trigger is a presidential séance gone wrong (Wilson, 1916). Mexico had a séance-president three years earlier, and he was murdered. Someone in the files will have drawn the line between the two.

Obregón's arm — lost 3 June 1915, displayed until 1989

  • Where: the Trinidad / Santa Ana del Conde lines near León, Guanajuato (the Celaya campaign); later the Monumento a Álvaro Obregón, Parque de la Bombilla, San Ángel, Mexico City.
  • What happened: In the battles of Celaya (April 1915) Álvaro Obregón broke Villa's Division of the North with trenches, wire and machine guns learned from the European papers. In the prolonged fighting at Trinidad and Santa Ana del Conde (29 April – 5 June 1915) a shell blew off his right arm; he nearly died. The arm was embalmed and kept, and from the monument's opening (1935) on the site of his assassination it was displayed in a jar of formaldehyde until it was incinerated in 1989 and replaced with a marble sculpture.
  • The odd detail: Obregón's own joke: his men could not find the arm until a comrade held up a gold azteca coin — and the arm "came skipping up… reached up and grasped it in its fingers."
  • People: Álvaro Obregón — Sonoran general, president 1920–24, murdered 1928; Francisco Villa — the loser at Celaya.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%81lvaro_Obreg%C3%B3n
  • Confidence: High on the arm and its display/incineration; the date 3 June is the usual one (the article gives the 29 April – 5 June battle range).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened (the wound). BENDS after: the monument (1935) stands in UNCEDED MEXICO, but Obregón himself is a Sonoran — his home state is CEDED NORTH after 1917. A Sonoran general ruling in Mexico City a country that lost Sonora is a political problem the setting must answer (see Part D).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a severed relic of a living-then-murdered strongman, displayed in public for 54 years, then burned — a hand that "grasps gold" by legend. Stealing it (or finding what was put in the jar instead) is a module.

Ambrose Bierce vanishes in Villa's Mexico — last letter 26 December 1913

  • Where: Ciudad Juárez and Chihuahua City, Chihuahua; legend puts the end at Sierra Mojada, Coahuila.
  • What happened: The 71-year-old writer left Washington in October 1913, crossed at El Paso, and attached himself to Villa's army as an observer; he saw the battle of Tierra Blanca. His last known letter, to Blanche Partington, dated Chihuahua 26 December 1913, closes: "I leave here tomorrow for an unknown destination." He was never seen again. One oral tradition (recorded by a priest, James Lienert) says he was shot against the cemetery wall at Sierra Mojada, Coahuila.
  • The odd detail: he had written that being "stood up against a Mexican stone wall and shot to rags" beat "falling down the cellar stairs" — and "To be a Gringo in Mexico — ah, that is euthanasia!"
  • People: Ambrose Bierce — author of The Devil's Dictionary and "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge"; Blanche Partington — the last correspondent.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambrose_Bierce
  • Confidence: High (disappearance, letter); the Sierra Mojada story is labelled legend.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Both Chihuahua and Coahuila are CEDED NORTH after 1917: an American search for Bierce's grave is, in the setting, a search on American soil.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the most famous American writer of the weird walks into the north and vanishes; twenty years later the north is American and the question can be asked officially. A Section 13 cold file.

Part B — The border war that became the Southern War (1914–1917)

The Tampico Affair — 9 April 1914

  • Where: Tampico, Tamaulipas — the Iturbide bridge and a civilian fuel dock on the Pánuco.
  • What happened: Nine unarmed sailors from the USS Dolphin, loading gasoline, were arrested at gunpoint by Huerta's Federal troops under Gen. Ignacio Morelos Zaragoza and released within the hour with apologies. Rear Admiral Henry T. Mayo demanded a formal disavowal, punishment of the officer, and that Mexico hoist the American flag on shore and salute it with 21 guns. Huerta refused the salute ("equivalent to accepting the sovereignty of a foreign state"). Congress approved force on 20 April. As the port boiled, American residents were evacuated onto US ships — and onto the German cruiser SMS Dresden and HMS Hermione; refugee camps opened in San Diego, Texas and New Orleans.
  • The odd detail: the war's first cause was a gasoline run and a demanded 21-gun salute; and the Americans of the oil town were rescued, in part, by a German warship.
  • People: Adm. Henry T. Mayo — who demanded the salute; Gen. Ignacio Morelos Zaragoza — Huerta's commander at Tampico; Victoriano Huerta — who refused.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tampico_Affair
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. TAMPICO ENCLAVE — held outright by America after 1917. In the setting this is the enclave's founding grievance, told as the reason the flag now flies there.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): in the setting the salute Mexico refused in 1914 is fired every day in 1930s Tampico by an American battery. The Dresden detail is "Germany as patron" in miniature — the Kaiser's navy was already in the harbour.

The occupation of Veracruz — 21 April – 23 November 1914

  • Where: Veracruz, Veracruz — the waterfront, the customs house, the Naval Academy.
  • What happened: Told that the German steamer Ypiranga would land arms for Huerta on 21 April, Wilson ordered the port seized. Rear Adm. Frank Friday Fletcher's sailors and Marines had the city by 11 a.m. on 22 April against naval cadets (urged on by Commodore Manuel Azueta), released prisoners from the La Galera military prison and civilian snipers. US dead 21 (plus one drowned), 74 wounded; Mexican dead ~152–172. Some fifty-odd Medals of Honor followed, controversially (Smedley Butler's among them; the usual count is 55–56 — UNVERIFIED this session). Gen. Frederick Funston ran a military government for seven months, with Douglas MacArthur on his staff; naval aircraft flew their first operational reconnaissance. On withdrawal Funston was ordered to carry off all funds — US money and the Mexican customs receipts and taxes — and the records needed to prove his accounts; the port and its stores of war material fell, in effect, to Carranza.
  • The odd detail: before the Americans, the city's refuse service was stray dogs and vultures and there was a 5-peso fine for harming a vulture; the Army ditched 61 miles, sprayed 69,000 gallons of oil on standing water, vaccinated 46,000 people — and "the city became sufficiently clean that the vultures left."
  • People: Frank Friday Fletcher — admiral of the landing; Frederick Funston — military governor (dies Feb 1917, see PEOPLE §3); Manuel Azueta — commodore who sent the cadets to fight; Douglas MacArthur — staff intelligence officer; Smedley Butler — Marine, Medal of Honor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_occupation_of_Veracruz · https://jmvh.org/article/joint-expeditionary-medical-support-in-1914-the-us-occupation-of-veracruz-mexico/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Government_of_Veracruz
  • Confidence: High (dates, casualties, sanitation figures). The customs-funds instruction comes from a search summary of State Dept. papers (FRUS 1916) — Medium; confirm the dollar amount before print.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. VERACRUZ — in the setting the Americans come back to the same customs house in 1917 and stay, under a Mexican flag. The 1914 occupation is the dress rehearsal and its account books are the precedent.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the vultures came back when the Marines left in 1914 — and in the setting's 1930s they have not come back, because the Marines never left. A town that has lost its carrion birds; what is eating the dead instead? (A three-layers scene: sanitation, a folk omen, and the thing underneath.)

The Santa Isabel massacre — 10 January 1916

  • Where: near Santa Isabel, Chihuahua (about 50 miles from Chihuahua City), on the Mexico North Western railway.
  • What happened: Villistas under Pablo López stopped a train carrying American mining men back to work and shot them beside the track — 16 (AP) or 17 (NYT) killed; one man, Thomas M. Holmes, escaped by running for the brush. The victims are usually named as Cusi Mining Co. staff under manager Charles R. Watson (Wikipedia says ASARCO employees). El Paso erupted in anti-Mexican violence. López, wounded at Columbus, was found in a cave near Santa Isabel on 23 April 1916 by Carrancistas, with a diary entry saying Villa had ordered the massacre, and taken to Chihuahua City to be shot.
  • The odd detail: the killer was found hiding in a cave near the scene of his crime three months later — with a written confession of whose order it was.
  • People: Pablo López — Villista colonel, executed 1916; Thomas M. Holmes — the one survivor; Charles R. Watson — mine manager among the dead (UNVERIFIED this session).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Isabel_massacre
  • Confidence: High on the event and López; Medium on which company employed the victims (sources differ).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (January), happened. CEDED NORTH (Chihuahua). In the setting this is the first atrocity of the Southern War's prologue — the massacre the recruiting posters remembered.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the cave where López hid is on American soil after 1917; a diary with "Villa ordered it" is a document three factions want for three reasons.

The Columbus raid — 4:15 a.m., 9 March 1916

  • Where: Columbus, New Mexico, and the 13th Cavalry's Camp Furlong beside it, three miles north of the line.
  • What happened: About 480–600 Villistas attacked the town in the dark at 4:15 a.m., looted, and burned the Commercial Hotel and several houses and stores. Ten civilians and eight soldiers were killed. Lt. John P. Lucas, barefoot, set up a Hotchkiss machine gun in the town and broke the attack; Maj. Frank Tompkins pursued 15 miles into Mexico. At least 67 raiders died in the town. The 63 Villista dead and their horses were dragged south of town, soaked in kerosene and burned. Of 11 captured, six were hanged (two on 9 June, four on 30 June 1916).
  • The odd detail: Susie Parks, a 20-year-old telephone operator, stayed at the switchboard in the dark and raised the alarm; and the burning pyre of raiders was photographed and sold as a postcard (postcards: Leads not chased).
  • People: Francisco "Pancho" Villa — the raider; Lt. John P. Lucas — the barefoot machine-gunner; Maj. Frank Tompkins — the pursuit; Col. Herbert J. Slocum — commanding the 13th Cavalry; Susie Parks — the switchboard operator.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Columbus_(1916) · https://www.nps.gov/places/village-of-columbus-and-camp-furlong-columbus-new-mexico.htm (via search)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: the seed of the Southern War — happens exactly as recorded (the brief's pre-1916 list includes it). Columbus is US soil in both worlds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): why did Villa raid a town he could not hold? The period argued it (revenge on a crooked arms dealer, the US recognition of Carranza, German money). The setting can let all three be true and add a fourth: someone needed the war to start in March 1916 — the same year Wilson was removed (the setting gives no month; worth fixing one, before or after Carrizal).

Pershing's Punitive Expedition — 14 March 1916 – 5 February 1917

  • Where: Chihuahua — Colonia Dublán (the Mormon colony that was its base), Namiquipa, San Gerónimo, Parral, Carrizal; the Texas and New Mexico border.
  • What happened: Brig. Gen. John J. Pershing crossed on 14 March 1916 with about 6,600 men, eventually more than 10,000. The Army ran its first motor-truck convoys (Jeffery Quads, FWDs) and flew the 1st Aero Squadron's eight Curtiss JN-3s (two lost en route). Apache Scouts under Lt. James Shannon rode with it. At Parral (12 April) Maj. Tompkins's 128 troopers were chased out of town by some 500 Mexicans (2 US dead). Villa, shot in the leg, hid for weeks near Santa Cruz de Herrera. Raiders hit Glenn Springs, Texas (5 May). On 18 June Wilson called up the National Guard — over 140,000 men went to the border. After Carrizal (next entry) the column sat in camp; it withdrew 28 January – 5 February 1917.
  • The odd detail: Pershing's supply line was the first in the US Army to run on trucks and his scouts on aeroplanes — and his most reliable trackers were Apaches, in the Sierra their grandfathers had fought the Army from.
  • People: John J. Pershing — commander (in the setting, the man who won the Southern War: PEOPLE §3); Frank Tompkins — major at Parral; James Shannon — lieutenant of Apache Scouts; Frederick Funston — Southern Department.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pancho_Villa_Expedition
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS from spring 1916. The real expedition stalls and goes home in February 1917 because Washington turns to Europe (ALTERNATE_HISTORY §8.1). In the setting the column does not stop at Carrizal: it becomes the first era of the Southern War, won by 1917, and the Guardsmen called up in June 1916 are its veterans. Colonia Dublán and the Mormon colonies sit inside what becomes CEDED NORTH.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Guard call-up of 140,000 is the setting's "forties veteran" generation — mechanics, truck drivers, pilots of the first air war the Army ever fought, and Apache scouts nobody thanked.

Patton's three bodies on the hoods — 14 May 1916

  • Where: San Miguelito ranch, near Rubio, Chihuahua.
  • What happened: 2nd Lt. George S. Patton, on a corn-buying run with 15 men in three Dodge touring cars, surrounded the ranch of Julio Cárdenas, commander of Villa's bodyguard. Cárdenas and two others were killed. Patton had the three bodies strapped to the hoods of the cars and drove them back to Pershing's headquarters. The press made him famous as the first American to fight from a motor car.
  • The odd detail: Patton notched his revolver for the kill (the story he told; UNVERIFIED this session).
  • People: George S. Patton — lieutenant, Pershing's aide; Julio Cárdenas — Villa's bodyguard commander.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pancho_Villa_Expedition
  • Confidence: High (the bodies on the hoods); the notched grip is UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: happened (May 1916, before the setting's war escalates). CEDED NORTH. In the setting Patton never goes to France; he rises through the Southern War's mechanised columns instead.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a period photograph a GM can describe exactly — dead men lashed to Dodge hoods. The first war fought with cars made its first trophies out of men.

The Battle of Carrizal — 21 June 1916

  • Where: Carrizal, Chihuahua, south of Ciudad Juárez on the road to Chihuahua City.
  • What happened: Pershing sent Troops C and K of the 10th Cavalry (Buffalo Soldiers) under Capt. Charles T. Boyd and Capt. Lewis Morey to scout reports of Villa. At Carrizal they found not Villistas but Carranza's Federal troops under Gen. Félix Uresti Gómez, ordered to let the Americans move only north. Warned, Boyd attacked anyway. 12 Americans were killed (Boyd and Lt. Henry Adair among them), 28 wounded, 24 captured; Gómez was killed with 20–50 of his men. The prisoners were taken to Chihuahua City and later released at the border. Pershing asked to attack Chihuahua City; Wilson refused.
  • The odd detail: it was the government army America recognised, not Villa, that beat the Americans — and the man who had ordered the fight died in it on each side.
  • People: Capt. Charles T. Boyd — killed leading the attack; Lt. Henry R. Adair — killed (Camp Adair, Oregon, named for him); Capt. Lewis Morey — survived, wounded; Gen. Félix Uresti Gómez — killed.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Carrizal
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — this is the setting's hinge (per the brief). In the record Carrizal "marked the effective end of the Mexican Expedition" because Wilson feared war with Mexico while Germany loomed. In the setting Wilson is removed in 1916 and Marshall has no European war to save the Army for: Pershing's request to march on Chihuahua is the one granted. Carrizal becomes the Southern War's Fort Sumter. CEDED NORTH.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Buffalo Soldiers who died first in the war that made the empire; the 24 prisoners who spent weeks in a Chihuahua jail. A Black veteran of Carrizal in 1930s Chicago (Bronzeville) is a character the core book can use without inventing a date.

The Zimmermann Telegram — sent 16 January 1917

  • Where: Berlin → Washington embassy → Mexico City (German minister Heinrich von Eckardt); intercepted in London (Room 40).
  • What happened: Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann offered Mexico alliance if the United States entered the war, with Germany's help to "reconquer her lost territory in New Mexico, Texas and Arizona," and asked Carranza to bring in Japan. Room 40 (Nigel de Grey) broke it. Wilson released it on 1 March 1917; on 3 March Zimmermann said, "I cannot deny it. It is true." Carranza's military commission judged the offer unworkable — Mexico's civil war, US strength, German money that could not be delivered, English-speaking territory that could not be held — and Japan publicly refused. Behind it lay German agents: Franz von Rintelen had given Huerta money in 1915 to start a US–Mexican war (Huerta was arrested at Newman, New Mexico, and died in El Paso in January 1916), and Lothar Witzke worked from Mexico City.
  • The odd detail: Germany's diplomats could send it only through Washington's own cable, which Wilson had lent them for peace talks.
  • People: Arthur Zimmermann — German foreign secretary; Heinrich von Eckardt — minister in Mexico; Venustiano Carranza — who declined; Franz von Rintelen — saboteur who funded Huerta; Lothar Witzke — saboteur in Mexico.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimmermann_Telegram
  • Confidence: High on the telegram; the "Washington's cable" detail is standard but not re-read this session (Medium).
  • Divergence check: BENDS hard. Its premise is "if the US enters the war" — in the setting the US never does, and is already at war with Mexico. Three options for the owner: (a) it is never sent (no US entry to hedge against); (b) it is sent earlier and differently — Germany offering the Carranza government help against the American invasion, making Germany Mexico's patron in the Southern War itself; (c) it is sent as recorded, intercepted, and published by Marshall's government as the casus belli for the 1917 treaty's harshness. ALTERNATE_HISTORY §3 and §8.6 note the offer "is still live." Option (b) is "Germany as the patron of other people's revolutions" exactly.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a victorious Germany with a signed, public promise to return Texas to Mexico — which Mexico never took up, lost its north anyway, and can hold Berlin to. The telegram is a debt Berlin owes in the 1930s.

Part B2 — The north, the oil coast, and the Revolution's dead (1908–1926)

The Torreón massacre of the Chinese — 13–15 May 1911

  • Where: Torreón, Coahuila — the Laguna cotton district's railway city.
  • What happened: When Maderista revolutionary forces took Torreón, they and townspeople killed 303 Chinese men, women and children and looted and destroyed Chinese homes and businesses. China demanded over 30 million pesos in reparation (100,000 per victim); Mexico granted 3.1 million pesos and an apology in November 1912, but Madero was overthrown and killed in 1913 and it was never paid. Mexico formally apologised in 2021. ⟨fixed: V_M01 — the 30 million was China's demand, not Mexico's offer; the dead were 303 Chinese plus 5 Japanese⟩
  • The odd detail: the first great massacre of the Revolution was not of soldiers or landlords but of a Chinese merchant colony, by the "democratic" side — and the debt for it died with Madero.

The Calles Law and the silence of the bells — 14 June / 31 July 1926

  • Where: all Mexico; the churches of Jalisco closed for two years.
  • What happened: President Plutarco Elías Calles's "Law for Reforming the Penal Code" (signed 14 June, in force 31 July 1926) enforced Article 130: a 500-peso fine for wearing clerical dress in public, up to five years' prison for a priest criticising the government, foreign priests expelled, convents, monasteries and religious schools closed. Mexico had about 4,500 priests before; by 1934 there were 334 licensed priests for 15 million people, and by 1935 17 states had none. The law stood until 26 December 1938.

  • People: Plutarco Elías Calles — president 1924–28, then the Jefe Máximo behind three presidents; the Mexican episcopate.

  • Divergence check: BENDS. Calles is a Sonoran (born Guaymas) — his home state is CEDED NORTH.   • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the image survives the divergence: a famous exile stepping off a tanker at Tampico under American guns, met by a painter with pistols. Swap the name.

The oil expropriation — 10 p.m., 18 March 1938

  • Where: the National Palace (radio broadcast); Tampico, Poza Rica and the Golden Lane; the Zócalo.
  • What happened: After the foreign companies defied a Supreme Court award to the oil workers' union, President Cárdenas went on the radio at 10 p.m. on 18 March 1938 and nationalised all oil reserves, installations and foreign companies (PEMEX followed). Crowds filled the Zócalo; people queued to give toward the indemnity — jewellery, wedding rings, clothing, and live chickens. Britain protested so hard that Mexico broke relations with London; the US companies boycotted Mexican oil. 18 March is still a national holiday.
  • The odd detail: among the gifts toward the indemnity, one person brought a live chicken — a hen to help pay off Standard Oil and Shell.
  • People: Lázaro Cárdenas — president; the oil workers' union (STPRM); El Águila (Royal Dutch Shell, formerly Pearson) and the US companies.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_oil_expropriation (via search) · https://history.state.gov/milestones/1937-1945/mexican-oil (via search) · https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/petroleum-expropriation-1938-mexico (via search)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the single hardest bend in the lane. Tampico and the Golden Lane are the TAMPICO ENCLAVE, American-held: Mexico cannot expropriate oil it does not hold. Options: (a) a 1938 expropriation of the oil outside the enclave (Poza Rica, discovered 1930, is south in Veracruz state — in the setting the one great field Mexico kept?) — a defiant nationalisation beside an American enclave that cannot touch it; (b) a demand under Article 27 that the enclave's oil is Mexican — a diplomatic crisis instead of a nationalisation; (c) a strike in the enclave. Recommend (a) + (b) together; Britain (defeated, broke) cannot protest in the setting, and Germany might buy the nationalised oil — as a German-won world's Europe would. Veracruz's customs receivership sits right between the two.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the queue of wedding rings and chickens is the "population" image; the enclave's fence is the empire. A module in March 1938 set on the line between American Tampico and Mexican Poza Rica writes itself.

Cedillo, the last rebel — May 1938 – 11 January 1939

  • Where: San Luis Potosí — his base at Las Palomas / Valle del Maíz.
  • What happened: Saturnino Cedillo, the agrarian warlord of San Luis Potosí and ex-Agriculture Secretary, broke with Cárdenas after 1937, opposed the oil and electric nationalisations, and when ordered out of his stronghold rose in May 1938 — the last military revolt in Mexico. Labour leader Vicente Lombardo Toledano charged that he had been courted by German agents and would be helped by them; the government press cast him as Mexico's "fifth column." He was hunted down and killed on 11 January 1939, with relatives (reportedly his sister and son) dying in the campaign.
  • The odd detail: the last caudillo of the Revolution died in the hills he had ruled, hunted by the army he had once led — and his rebellion is remembered through a foreign-agents accusation never proven.
  • People: Saturnino Cedillo — warlord; Vicente Lombardo Toledano — the accuser.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturnino_Cedillo (via search) · https://relatosehistorias.mx/nuestras-historias/saturnino-cedillo-el-ultimo-rebelde-del-periodo-posrevolucionario (via search)
  • Confidence: High on dates and death; the German-agent charge is the period's accusation (Medium, and labelled as such).
  • Divergence check: BENDS but survives: UNCEDED MEXICO. In the setting the "German agents" are Imperial Germany's, a victor with a live claim on Mexico's gratitude (Zimmermann), which makes the accusation far more plausible — "Germany as patron" again. San Luis Potosí borders the ceded north's Nuevo León and Coahuila.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a warlord in the hills between the rump state and the American north, courted by Berlin, killed in 1939. The Circle, Germany and Mexico City all have reasons to want him dead — or alive.

Part E — The divergence questions this lane raises (for the owner; nothing settled here)

  1. Who signed the 1917 treaty? Carranza was First Chief in Feb 1917 and elected president in March. If he signed, his murder at Tlaxcalantongo (1920) by the Sonorans is the losers-of-the-north killing the man who gave their states away — the strongest reading. (PEOPLE §8 gap 6.)
  2. Does the Sonoran dynasty rule Mexico at all? Obregón (Navojoa/Huatabampo), Calles (Guaymas), de la Huerta (Guaymas) and Rodríguez are all from ceded Sonora. Either they are exiles who came south and seized the rump state (a Mexican "lost-province" revanchist regime — very period), or the setting needs different presidents 1920–34. The first keeps every date and name.
  3. The Constitution of 1917 — written in Querétaro the week Pershing's column went home in our world; in the setting, the defeated country's charter. Article 27 (the subsoil is the nation's) becomes a standing legal claim on the ceded minerals and Tampico's oil — and the 1938 expropriation the day Mexico acts on it.
  4. The Zimmermann Telegram — never sent, sent differently (Germany as Mexico's patron in the Southern War), or published by Marshall as the treaty's justification. Each gives 1930s Germany a different standing in Mexico.
  5. The Cristero War's frontier. The Cristero heartland (Jalisco, Michoacán, Guanajuato, Colima, Zacatecas) is unceded; Chihuahua and Sonora are not. Do priests flee north into American protection? Does the IHS run its Mexican war from occupied Chihuahua? Does the occupier arm the Cristeros against the anticlerical rump state (the K of C lobbied for exactly that)?
  6. Events that cannot happen as recorded in the ceded north: Maldonado's martyrdom (Chihuahua 1937), Chihuahua's one-priest law, the 1926–27 Yaqui campaign (becomes America's war), the 1931 Chinese expulsion from Sonora, the 1929 Escobar rising (becomes a rising against the US), Villa's 1923 murder at Parral (on American soil). Each needs a ruling or a shift.
  7. Villa's fate (brief; PEOPLE §3) — the record gives a surrender (1920), a murder (1923) and a stolen head (1926). All three can stand on American soil.
  8. The 1938 expropriation vs. the Tampico enclave — the hardest collision. Poza Rica (south of the Golden Lane) as "the field Mexico kept" is the cleanest answer.
  9. Hitler's Mein Kampf — does it exist without a failed 1923 putsch and a prison term? (Touches the Calles exile anecdote.)
  10. What German "support" means in Mexico — the Gold Shirts (1934–36) and Cedillo (1938) were both tied, by the period, to the German embassy. In the setting that embassy represents the war's victor.
