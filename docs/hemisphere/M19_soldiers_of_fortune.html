M19 — Soldiers of fortune: the real pool a mercenary guild would recruit from (Mexico, the Caribbean and Central America, 1896–1940)

  • Lane: M19 — the American and European adventurers of the Mexican Revolution and the Caribbean/Central American wars, 1900–1940: their whole careers, their hangouts, their pay, their ghostwriters, the Marines who wrote it down, the aviators, and the members of Roosevelt's 1913–14 River of Doubt expedition (the setting's Guild founding voyage).
  • Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (research agent) · Brief: BRIEF.md in this folder + the Chicago sweep's BRIEF.md (entry format, sourcing rules, hard rules).
  • Setting sources read first: ALTERNATE_HISTORY.md §12.4 (the Guild "in the owner's own words": "I do not care what flag they fly under … I will use them — or we will kill them if I have to"), §13.1 and §15 (Roosevelt's Amazon voyage: he "stumbles into something bigger", builds the Guild from scattered solitary hunters, is eliminated 6 Jan 1919), and PEOPLE_OF_THE_TIMELINE.md (Funston "the general the war began under, dead in the middle of it"). Already covered elsewhere and not repeated: Sommerfeld (M06), the Parral head theft and the San Miguelito fight (M01, M14), Butler's "gangster for capitalism" quote (M05), Ocotal (M05), Wirkus/Craige/Hanneken (M04), Zemurray's Cuyamel as a company (M05).

Overview (41 entries). The Guild's founder-myth says Roosevelt found "scattered" hunters and organised them; the real record of 1896–1940 shows what that pool looked like. It was small (Lawrence Taylor's 1986 count for the Mexican Revolution: "several hundred" mercenaries in all), cheap or dear by skill ($500 gold a month for the two best machine gunners; $1,500 a month for a pilot), schooled in one place (New Orleans and the Honduran coast, 1897–1912, under a former Illinois Central engineer named Lee Christmas), networked at one address (the Sheldon Hotel bar in El Paso, burned 9 April 1929), and unashamedly for sale to both sides — Holmdahl served, by his own account, Díaz's Sonoran rural police, Madero, Villa, Carranza and Pershing; Dreben served Bonilla, Madero, Orozco, Villa's suppliers and Pershing. Their own memoirs are unreliable by trade (Lamb's dogfight, O'Reilly's "ten wars", Thord-Gray's ranks), which is a gift to a setting built on three layers of truth. Three findings the owner may not have: (1) the Guild's founding expedition carried a Notre Dame priest (Zahm, who turned back at Utiarity Falls before the river), an Arctic explorer who lost his ship (Fiala, who left on 3 February on a separate river route), a Chicago Field Museum bird man (Cherrie, who did run the River of Doubt), and a murderer who was left behind ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩ — only Roosevelt, Kermit, Cherrie, Rondon and Brazilians went down the river — plus Rondon's rule "die if need be, never kill"; (2) Roosevelt's circle of hunters already included a Boer spy who later ran a German spy ring (Duquesne — his place in Roosevelt's circle rests on his own claims ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩) and a scout who guarded a presidential summit (Burnham); (3) the mercenaries' showpiece battle, at La Ceiba on 25 January 1911 (Wikipedia says it "is believed to be" the first such use of automatic weapons ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩), was fought under the eyes of two U.S. gunboats whose captain later reported ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩ that Christmas had told him the State Department "was well aware of all the plans" — the soldier of fortune as a deniable arm of Washington and the fruit companies, which is exactly how the setting's Guild is compromised.

Part A — The Honduran school (New Orleans and the Caribbean coast, 1897–1912)

The American mercenary trade of the Mexican Revolution was trained on the Honduran coast, paid for by fruit companies and run out of New Orleans. Every name in Part B passed through it.

Lee Christmas — from engineer to "the Incredible Yanqui" — 1863–1924

  • Where: McComb, Mississippi; Puerto Cortés, Tegucigalpa and La Ceiba, Honduras; New Orleans.
  • What happened: Born 2 February 1863 in Livingston Parish, Louisiana, Christmas worked as a Lake Pontchartrain tugboat pilot, then an Illinois Central brakeman (McComb, 1879), fireman and, by 22, a locomotive engineer on the Memphis–New Orleans run. In 1897 he fell asleep after 54 hours on duty, caused a collision, was cleared, but failed a colour-blindness test, which ended his career. He had already moved to Puerto Cortés in 1894 as a railroad engineer. On 14 April 1897 rebels captured his train at the Laguna trestle; he joined Manuel Bonilla's rebels, was commissioned on the spot, and earned the name "the Incredible Yanqui". He then changed sides three times: 1899 defected to the government as a colonel, and police chief of Tegucigalpa from May 1902, under President Terencio Sierra ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩; 1903 back to Bonilla, a general when Bonilla became president; 1907 wounded in a failed Nicaraguan invasion and exiled to Guatemala; 1910–11 the coup in Part A-3. He lost influence when Bonilla died in 1913, was turned down as too old to volunteer in the Great War, patented a baited rattrap and a railroad device meant to prevent engineer-fatigue accidents, caught tropical sprue, married Ida Culotta in 1914, and died in New Orleans on 21 January 1924 of acute anemia, aged 60.
  • The odd detail: the most famous mercenary of the Caribbean began as a railroad man who fell asleep at the throttle — and patented a device against exactly that.
  • People: Lee Christmas — soldier of fortune; Manuel Bonilla — Honduran president he served; Terencio Sierra — president who made him police chief; Ida Culotta — his wife.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Christmas · https://libcom.org/article/ousting-president-honduras-1911
  • Confidence: High on the outline (Wikipedia); the exact wording of individual dates is Wikipedia's.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened (career through 1913). His 1924 death is a domestic New Orleans event and holds; the U.S. landings in Honduras of 1919, 1924 and 1925 sit comfortably in an occupation-era setting.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Guild's pre-history. The men who fought under Christmas are the exact type of "solitary hunters" the setting says Roosevelt organised in 1914; the Secret Service had a file on him from 1910 (see the Hornet entry). A New Orleans file that lists which of his men later appear on a Guild roll would date the Guild's real recruiting pool without a page of invented lore.

Guy "Machine Gun" Molony — from Boer-War runaway to police superintendent — 1884–1972

  • Where: New Orleans; Honduras (La Ceiba, Tegucigalpa); the Boer republics.
  • What happened: Molony was born in New Orleans (1884) and, as a teenager, "ran away to South Africa" to fight on the British side in the Second Boer War, where he reportedly got his nickname. He stood 6 ft 6. He was Christmas's chief lieutenant in the January 1911 invasion of Honduras, and the Hotchkiss machine gun at La Ceiba is credited to him. Back in New Orleans he became superintendent of police — Encyclopedia.com says 1921 (an earlier draft's 1920 is unsupported ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩) — and ran what one account calls one of the department's most successful administrations, with a leadership list heavy with Irish names. After four years he resigned (1925) and returned to Honduras as bodyguard to the president, acquiring plantations, an automobile dealership and a post at the national brewery; in the mid-1930s he fought rebels against President Tiburcio Carías Andino, kept a rice plantation into the early 1960s, was photographed with Vice President Richard Nixon, and died in New Orleans on 13 February 1972.
  • The odd detail: in Prohibition New Orleans, the city's police chief was the man who had shot his way through La Ceiba — and he left the job to go back to the same coast.
  • People: Guy Molony — machine gunner, later police superintendent; Lee Christmas — his commander; Tiburcio Carías Andino — Honduran president he defended.
  • Sources: https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/molony-guy-1884-1972 · https://www.irishcentral.com/news/the-adventures-of-machine-gun-molony-1018-237602201
  • Confidence: High on outline; Medium-High on the police dates (1921 to 1925, per Encyclopedia.com; see V_M19); the Nixon photograph is from IrishCentral only. His name is spelled Maloney in some sources (e.g., Wikipedia's Holmdahl article).
  • Divergence check: the Honduran work is pre-1916, happened. His 1920s New Orleans police term is domestic and holds; the 1930s Honduran rebel-fighting holds in a setting where the Caribbean stays occupied.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Secret Service and the New Orleans police superintendent in one file: the man who once slipped the Secret Service's watchers (see next entry) is the man the Secret Service must deal with when the Golden Circle's Gulf smuggling reaches Basin Street. A Chicago-facing version: the Outfit's Gulf liquor line runs through a chief who was a filibuster.

The Hornet plot: New Orleans to La Ceiba — December 1910 – 25 January 1911

  • Where: New Orleans (Basin Street's May Evans bordello; Bayou St. John); Ship Island, Mississippi; Roatán, Utila and La Ceiba, Honduras.
  • What happened: The Honduran president Miguel Dávila, backed by American bankers, proposed a loan that Sam Zemurray's Cuyamel Fruit Company opposed; Zemurray hired Lee Christmas to bring back exiled ex-president Manuel Bonilla. In December 1910 the conspirators — Christmas, Guy Molony, Bonilla and his aide Florian Dávadi — went out on the town in Storyville, visited the May Evans bordello on Basin Street, and slipped the Secret Service agents watching them — the agents gave up their posts at 2 a.m. on the night of 23 December 1910 ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩; they raced to Zemurray's private yacht at Bayou St. John, sailed across Lake Pontchartrain to Ship Island, and ferried hidden rifles and ammunition out to the Hornet, a surplus Navy vessel that had already left Algiers Point on 22 December and was waiting outside the three-mile limit. (Secret Service agents had told the ship's new owners she could not sail without federal certification that she carried no weapons; inspectors found only food, coal and twenty men, so she was not stopped — there was no failed first attempt ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩.) They took Roatán on New Year's Eve; at Utila they made the local commander run circles round his cabin in his underwear shouting "Viva Bonilla!". At La Ceiba on 25 January 1911 hundreds fought on each side; Molony's machine gun was decisive, a Krupp field piece was captured, and government General Francisco Guerrero was killed. The U.S. gunboats Tacoma and Marietta lay offshore; Captain George Cooper, commander of the Marietta (not the Tacoma) ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩, declared La Ceiba a "neutral zone" that government forces could not attack; in a dispatch after his 17 January meeting with Christmas he reported that Christmas told him "the State Department was well aware of all the plans of the revolutionists before they began" — Christmas's claim, which Cooper came to accept when Washington did not contradict it ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩. Dávila resigned (the assembly had voted 32–4 to reject the Morgan loan); Bonilla was inaugurated in early February 1912, with 75 U.S. Marines guarding Puerto Cortés, and Zemurray received roughly 20,000 hectares, duty-free imports and a $500,000 government loan.
  • The odd detail: Christmas's own line as they left the bordello: "the first time I've ever heard of anybody going from a whorehouse to a White House." (Reported in the libcom account, which follows Dan Koeppel's Banana.)
  • People: Lee Christmas; Guy Molony; Manuel Bonilla; Florian Dávadi — Bonilla's aide; Miguel Dávila — deposed president; Capt. George Cooper — USS Marietta commander ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩; Francisco Guerrero — federal general killed at La Ceiba; Sam Dreben — in the ~100 recruits (Wikipedia's Lee Christmas and Zemurray pages).
  • Sources: https://libcom.org/article/ousting-president-honduras-1911 · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Christmas · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Zemurray
  • Confidence: High on the outline; Medium on details (libcom is a secondary retelling; the two sources disagree on the weapon — Wikipedia's Lee Christmas page says surplus Colt Model 1895 machine guns (the potato-digger nickname is not on that page ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩), libcom says a Hotchkiss — and on the size of the 1910 first attempt).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. No bend. It is the template for the setting's "Germany as the patron of other people's revolutions": here it is a fruit company and a U.S. Navy captain who "knew".
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a coup done by contract with the navy watching — the model of how the Guild works (deniable, paid, tolerated). The May Evans bordello is a real Storyville address a Chicago-book team could visit on the way south; the lost Secret Service watchers are a Secret Service scandal in 1910 that a 1930s agent can find in the archive.

Sam Zemurray — the paymaster — 1910–1912 (and 1954)

  • Where: New Orleans; Honduras; later Boston (United Fruit).
  • What happened: Zemurray's Cuyamel Fruit Company stood to lose if Honduras's President Dávila enforced debt terms tied to a U.S. bank loan; Secretary of State Philander Knox rejected his appeals. So he bought a ship (the Hornet), had Lee Christmas recruit about 100 mercenaries in New Orleans (Sam Dreben among them), and chose the man to install: Bonilla. His reward was "very favorable tax and land concessions" (Wikipedia). Decades later, as president of United Fruit, he authorised Edward Bernays's campaign against Guatemala's Jacobo Árbenz, and the CIA coup of 1954 followed.
  • The odd detail: one man bought the ship, the army and the president — and forty-odd years later did it again with a public-relations man instead of a mercenary.
  • People: Sam Zemurray — banana magnate; Philander Knox — Secretary of State who refused him; Edward Bernays — propagandist (1954).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Zemurray · https://libcom.org/article/ousting-president-honduras-1911
  • Confidence: High on the outline.
  • Divergence check: the 1910–12 coup is pre-1916, happened; the 1954 coup is after the setting's window and would BEND (no CIA precedent in a setting without America's Europe-facing turn; whoever runs "intelligence" in the hemisphere would differ).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Guild's client. "Purchasable is what they are" needs a purchaser; Zemurray is the model: a fruit magnate who hires the Guild's ancestors to solve a debt. The Golden Circle's Havana-centred map would naturally include a fruit company that owns a Honduran president.

Cannon and Groce — American mercenaries shot at El Castillo — mid-November 1909

  • Where: San Juan River, Nicaragua; El Castillo (the old Spanish fort on the river).
  • What happened: Leonard Groce, a mining supervisor, and Lee Roy Cannon, a rubber planter, were Americans hired into Juan José Estrada's U.S.-backed rebellion against President José Santos Zelaya. They were captured mining the San Juan River to blow up a troop-carrying vessel, tried, and shot. Sources give the date as 16 or 17 November 1909 (Executed Today gives the 17th and notes some cite the 16th; the "12th" in an earlier draft is unsupported ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩). Zelaya's commander-in-chief and minister general reportedly opposed the execution; Zelaya ordered it. Secretary Knox answered with a diplomatic note calling Zelaya's regime "a blot upon the history of Nicaragua", ruled the pair entitled to prisoner-of-war treatment, and by late December, with U.S. Marines landing, Zelaya resigned. The American Journal of International Law and the State Department's Foreign Relations of the United States 1909 both carry the case.
  • The odd detail: two hired mine-layers were the casus belli for taking down a president — the deaths of mercenaries became a diplomatic emergency.
  • People: Leonard Groce; Lee Roy Cannon; José Santos Zelaya — Nicaraguan president; Juan José Estrada — rebel leader; Philander Knox — Secretary of State.
  • Sources: https://www.executedtoday.com/2009/11/17/1909-leonard-groce-and-lee-roy-cannon-american-mercenaries-nicaragua-zelaya/ · https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1909/ch88 (via search) · https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-journal-of-international-law/article/abs/execution-of-cannon-and-groce-in-nicaragua/BCF608EB26DFC77A88F962C7704A0BDB (via search)
  • Confidence: High on the event and Knox's response; date Medium (16/17 November); Holmdahl's Wikipedia entry places him "fighting alongside the San Juan River against Zelaya" when the two were caught — his presence there is UNVERIFIED (it is his own account).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. No bend; it is the origin of the Nicaraguan occupation the setting says never ends.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Guild contractor executed by a foreign state on a charge the U.S. government wants to be an outrage. Would the Guild ask for a body? Would the Secret Service? "Who was on the river with them" is a 1909 cold case with a Guild-sized cast.

Richard Harding Davis and the mercenary as a character — 1897 and after

  • Where: Puerto Cortés, Honduras; New York publishing; Cuba (1898); Veracruz (1914).
  • What happened: Davis, the most famous American war correspondent of his day, became fascinated with Lee Christmas at Puerto Cortés and used him as the model for his 1897 novel Soldiers of Fortune (Robert Clay, a mining engineer and mercenary who organises a counter-coup in the fictional republic of Olancho) and for Captain Macklin. In 1898, aboard a U.S. Navy ship, he witnessed the bombardment of Matanzas, Cuba; his story made headlines, and the Navy afterwards banned reporters from its vessels for the rest of the war. He also published a book of profiles titled Real Soldiers of Fortune (on Project Gutenberg, no. 3029; not read).
  • The odd detail: the phrase "soldier of fortune" as a category — the one the setting's 1930s people would use — comes from a novel written about a real engineer from Louisiana.
  • People: Richard Harding Davis — correspondent and novelist; Lee Christmas — his model.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Christmas · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Harding_Davis (via search) · https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3029/3029-h/3029-h.htm (via search, not read)
  • Confidence: High for the Christmas–Davis link (repeated in Wikipedia's Christmas and Davis pages); the Matanzas ban is from a search summary. UNVERIFIED: Davis's 1914 Veracruz coverage (not confirmed this session).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. (Davis's death in 1916 is from memory — UNVERIFIED — and would fall just as the Southern War begins.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the 1930s "period's own category" is "soldier of fortune" — a Guild hunter would call himself that, or be called that by Chicago newspapers. A quotable, real, printed source for it exists (Davis).

O. Henry and Al Jennings, hiding in Honduras — c. 1896–97

  • Where: Honduras (a country then without a U.S. extradition treaty); Ohio State Penitentiary, Columbus; New York.
  • What happened: According to Wikipedia's Al Jennings page, the writer William Sydney Porter (O. Henry) and the Oklahoma outlaw Al Jennings "met while both were hiding in Honduras, which did not have an extradition treaty with the US." Jennings later wrote Through the Shadows with O. Henry (1921), a memoir of their friendship "from a few years before they were sent to the Ohio State Penitentiary" (on separate charges) to after their release. O. Henry's short story "Holding Up a Train" (1904) was inspired by Jennings's career — and, the article says, Jennings himself was the actual author. O. Henry's 1904 novel of linked stories Cabbages and Kings gave the world the phrase "banana republic" (M05 has the phrase).
  • The odd detail: the man who named the "banana republic" learned Honduras as a fugitive, in a country chosen because it would not hand him back.
  • People: William Sydney Porter (O. Henry) — writer; Al Jennings — outlaw and memoirist.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Jennings
  • Confidence: Medium — the Honduran meeting is one Wikipedia sentence (dates and Trujillo not given there); Jennings's book was written by a man who told tall tales.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Honduras (and the whole no-extradition coast) as the refuge of the wanted — the reason a Guild hunter with a bounty on him and a Chicago warrant goes south. Also: the "banana republic" phrase originates in the setting's own tropics, in a book of comic stories.

Emil Holmdahl, the whole career — 1883–1963

  • Where: Fort Dodge, Iowa; Manila and Sulu; Shanghai; Honduras; Nayarit, Chihuahua, Sonora; El Paso; Van Nuys, California.
  • What happened: Born 26 August 1883 in the Swedetown district of Fort Dodge, Iowa; his father left the family. At 15 he enlisted in the 51st Iowa Volunteer Infantry (a hired actor posed as his father). The regiment sailed for Manila on 3 November 1898 (SS Pennsylvania), fought at San Roque (9 Feb 1899), Guadalupe Church (5 Mar), Quingua and Pulilan (24 Apr), Calumpit (25 Apr 1899 — the same river crossing that won Funston his Medal of Honor) and San Fernando (May); in September 1899 he chose to stay in the Philippines. In 1900 he took an ensign's commission in "General" Edmund F. English's mercenary foreign legion for the Qing — the "Royal Imperial Guards, Sinim Order of Dragoons" — which dissolved when the Boxer crisis cut its funds. He re-enlisted in the regular 20th Infantry, Co. I, saw the Sulu campaigns (Bud Dajo, March 1905), made sergeant 15 December 1905, guarded the U.S. Sub-Treasury in San Francisco after the April 1906 quake (125 hours, by his account) and took an honourable discharge on 31 January 1907. Then: a two-week job as a steamfitter, a banana boat from New Orleans, a walk through Nicaragua into Honduras and a place in Lee Christmas's band — where, by his own account, he met Dreben, Richardson, O'Reilly and Molony and was made an officer. His Mexican career is in the next two entries; his 1926 Villa-head arrest is in M01/M14.
  • The odd detail: he was fifteen when his first war started, and by twenty-three he had been a mercenary ensign in an Imperial Chinese guard, a Philippine sergeant, a San Francisco quake sentry, and a Christmas lieutenant.
  • People: Emil L. Holmdahl — soldier of fortune; Edmund F. English — mercenary "General" who recruited for the Qing; Lee Christmas; Sam Dreben; Tracy Richardson; Tex O'Reilly; Guy Molony.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Lewis_Holmdahl · https://iowahistoryjournal.com/soldier-of-fortune-emil-holmdahl-of-fort-dodge-fought-in-wars-around-the-world/ (preview only) · Douglas V. Meed, Soldier of Fortune: Adventuring in Latin America and Mexico with Emil Lewis Holmdahl (2002; via search)
  • Confidence: Medium. The Wikipedia article's early chapters draw heavily on Holmdahl's own interviews and memoirs (a 1913 interview is cited) and the article itself notes "much of his personal actions during this time are unknown, as no written sources exist" and carries several [citation needed] marks. Treat the outline as his story.
  • Divergence check: all of this is pre-1916 and happened as recorded (the Philippines, Sulu and Honduras as history; Central American work is the same coast as Part A).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the pattern of a Guild lifer: begins at fifteen, changes flags eight times, owns nothing. The Holmdahl file is also the best single character-generator for the Guild's "solitary hunter" era — the man who reached the age of thirty having been paid by five governments.

Holmdahl in Nayarit: the Tepic escape, the dynamite and the midnight firing squad — 1909–1911

  • Where: Tepic, Rosamorada, Rosario, Tuxpan (Nayarit); Mazatlán; Culiacán; the Buena Noche mine.
  • What happened (all of it his own account, as Wikipedia summarises it): Back from Central America in 1909, Holmdahl answered a want-ad — "Wanted: Man with military experience, who had nerve and is single" — and was handed a $100 bill by Mexican revolutionaries (Magonista) who sent him to Nogales, Culiacán and Mazatlán posing as a New York mining-company representative to count the Díaz garrisons at Tepic. The governor showed him the arsenal. When the governor was tipped off, Holmdahl escaped over the rooftops on a lariat, shot a policeman in the street, and — in his telling — shattered the stained-glass window above a fat priest's head to clear a church door. Caught near Tuxpán, dragged ten miles behind a horse to Rosamorada prison, beaten, and asked by a Díaz agent and a priest to sign a confession, he kicked the priest. A friendly guard freed him. He then took a captain's commission from Col. Emilio Kosterlitzky's Sonoran Rural Police (double agent from the start, he says), guarded a railroad's gold shipments near Mazatlán with 200 men, and in early 1911 changed sides on the road when captured horse-thieves said they served Madero. "Vámonos a Holmdahl" became a village cry; he took most of Nayarit's coastal towns; a failed Tepic jailbreak (betrayed by deserters) cost 300 executions. In May 1911, with Martín Espinosa, he took Rosario (using dynamite raided from the Buena Noche mine: 22 men, 27 cases) and then Rosamorada, where 700 prisoners were in the jail. His solution to the mob: shoot the worst — 112 killers picked from the prison book at midnight, six officers as the firing squad, 500 others freed with five pesos each. When Espinosa made himself a governor at Tepic, Holmdahl attacked him with 280 Cora men carrying bows and an old brass cannon; after 36 hours two-thirds of his men, all seven officers, were dead and he was wounded. He then rode to Madero.
  • The odd detail: the priest is the villain of the story twice — the informant with the confession paper, the fat priest at the church door — in the memoir of a man who told the priest he had "no religion".
  • People: Emil Holmdahl; Emilio Kosterlitzky — Díaz's border security chief; Martín Espinosa — rival Maderista; the Nayarit Cora — allied indigenous fighters; the Tepic governor (unnamed in the source).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Lewis_Holmdahl · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costa_Oeste_Campaign · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Rosamorada · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raid_on_Mazatl%C3%A1n_Railway (the last three seen in search results, not read)
  • Confidence: Low–Medium. One man's memoir, transcribed into Wikipedia; the numbers (300, 112, 700, 500, 3,000, 36 hours) are his. The Rosamorada prison massacre would be a serious war-crimes claim if true; no independent source found.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened (as a story). Nayarit is UNCEDED Mexico.

An IHS priest of the 1930s reading that memoir has a lead on a much older habit. For the Guild: a hired hunter whose self-reported body count nobody can check — the classic Guild recruit.

Part C — The first air wars (1913–1923)

Some of the Revolution's mercenaries flew. The first plane-on-plane and plane-on-ship combats in the Americas are both here — and both are partly legend.

Didier Masson and the Sonora: the first bombing of a ship in the Americas — 10 May 1913

  • Where: Guaymas harbour, Sonora.
  • What happened: The Constitutionalists bought a Martin pusher biplane, fitted it with a bombardier's seat and a primitive bombsight, and named it Sonora. Its pilot was Didier Masson, a Frenchman (born Asnières, 23 February 1886) holding Aero Club of America certificate no. 202 (January 1913, Glenn Martin's school), hired for $5,000 as the aeroplane's purchase fee, $750 for equipment, $300 a month, $50 a flight and at least $250 a bombing sortie ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩. On 10 May 1913, with observer Capt. Joaquín Bauche Alcalde, he overflew at least five federal gunboats at Guaymas and dropped four improvised pipe bombs (about 15 kg of explosive). All four missed, but the gunboats — seeing themselves defenceless — steamed out of the harbour, and some crew jumped overboard. It is the first known air-to-sea bombing in the Western Hemisphere. Masson left on 5 August 1913, citing unpaid wages and unease at bombing towns; he joined the French army in 1914, flew for the Lafayette Escadrille from 16 June 1916, later managed airports for Pan American in Central America, served as a French consular officer until 1940, and died in Mérida, Yucatán, on 2 June 1950.
  • The odd detail: a French jeweller's apprentice turned aviator, the first man to deliver newspapers by air (January 1911, Los Angeles Times), retired to and died in Mérida — the city of M15's Yucatán.
  • People: Didier Masson — pilot; Joaquín Bauche Alcalde — observer; Glenn L. Martin — aircraft builder.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didier_Masson · https://simanaitissays.com/2013/09/01/massons-mexican-adventure/ (via search) · https://azmemory.azlibrary.gov/nodes/view/21489 (via search)
  • Confidence: High on the pilot's biography (Wikipedia); the bomb count and weights are from search summaries (Medium).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Guaymas is in Sonora — the ceded north; his later Central American Pan Am career holds as an occupation-era airline. Masson's 1916 Lafayette Escadrille service is a real Great War fact and holds (the war goes on; only America stays out).

Lamb and Rader over Naco: the "first dogfight" — 30 November 1913 (disputed)

  • Where: the sky over Naco, Sonora (and its twin Naco, Arizona).
  • What happened: Two American mercenary pilots flew for rival sides: Dean Ivan Lamb in a Curtiss D pusher for General Benjamín G. On 30 November 1913, by Lamb's memoir, he spotted Rader, closed to less than 50 feet, and the two fired pistols (Lamb "held his gun inside his shirt when ejecting the shells", reloading from his belt, one hand on the wheel), each firing beneath the other's plane, until their ammunition ran out; then they waved goodbye.   • The odd detail: the "first air combat" in history is known only from the memoir of a man historians caught lying, and some readers place it a year later (November 1914).
  • People: Dean Ivan Lamb; Phil Rader — the opposing pilot; Benjamín G. Hill — Constitutionalist general.

Dean Lamb after Mexico — the "Incurable Filibuster" — 1886–1955

  • Where: Cherry Flats, Pennsylvania; Panama; Hammondsport, N.Y.; England; Honduras; Paraguay; Tucson.

Part D — The generals and the scouts

Frederick Funston, before he was a general — 1896–1902

  • Where: Cuba (insurgent artillery); Calumpit and Palanan, Luzon; San Francisco.

The Roosevelt–Rondon Scientific Expedition — 4 October 1913 – late April 1914

  • Where: New York; Rio de Janeiro; Cáceres; Tapirapuã; the Parecis plateau; the River of Doubt (Rio Roosevelt); the Aripuanã; Manaus.
  • What happened: The former President left New York on 4 October 1913 with his son Kermit and the AMNH naturalists George K. Cherrie and Leo E. Miller. In Brazil, Foreign Minister Lauro Müller proposed replacing the original route with exploration of the unmapped Rio da Dúvida (River of Doubt), and the Brazilian Colonel Cândido Rondon, director of the Indian Protection Service, took charge. The party went Cáceres → Tapirapuã → across the Parecis plateau → the river, reaching it on 27 February 1914. The party that went downriver numbered 22, of whom three were Americans (Roosevelt, Kermit, Cherrie). Zahm turned back from Utiarity Falls in late January, Fiala left on 3 February to descend the Papagaio–Juruena separately, and Miller went with Capt. Amilcar's Ji-Paraná party — none of the three ran the River of Doubt ⟨fixed: V_M19⟩. They lost canoes, starved, drowned a man (15 March), suffered a murder (early April), and Roosevelt's leg wound turned septic with fever. The expedition met rubber-tappers at the Aripuanã confluence on 15 April 1914 and was reunited with relief parties by about 26 April. Roosevelt was treated in Manaus and reached New York some weeks later; his health "never fully recovered". The river was renamed Rio Roosevelt in 1914. British explorer George Miller Dyott confirmed the discovery in 1927, and a 1992 expedition led by Charles Haskell and Elizabeth McKnight, with Roosevelt's great-grandson Tweed, ran the river in 33 days.

Cândido Rondon — the Colonel who would not kill — 1865–1958

  • Where: Mato Grosso; the telegraph lines; Rio de Janeiro; the Leticia frontier.
  • What happened: Born 5 May 1865 in Mato Grosso of Portuguese, Spanish, Guaná, Terena and Bororo ancestry; entered the army in 1881, second lieutenant 1888. From 1890 he led the Rondon Commission, building over 4,000 miles of telegraph lines through the interior; between 1900 and 1906 he extended lines to Bolivia and Peru, made peaceful contact with the Nambikwara and "pacified" the Bororo. His 1909 expedition discovered the River of Doubt. As first director of the Indian Protection Service (1910) he set its motto: "die if need be, never kill." After the Roosevelt expedition he mediated the Colombia–Peru dispute over Leticia (1934–38), backed the Villas-Bôas brothers' campaign for the Xingu National Park (1961), was made a Marshal on his 90th birthday (1955) and nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize (1957). The state of Rondônia bears his name. He died on 19 January 1958, aged 92.
  • The odd detail: the man who guided the Guild's founder was a positivist who forbade his men to fire — the philosophical opposite of the mercenary and the Marine.
  • People: Cândido Rondon; Theodore Roosevelt; the Nambikwara and Bororo — peoples he made contact with.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C%C3%A2ndido_Rondon
  • Confidence: High (Wikipedia). The Leticia dates differ elsewhere (1933–34).
  • Divergence check: holds — he is Brazilian and outside the setting's hemisphere-of-occupation, except that the setting's Germany "large settled communities" in South America (§12.4) could matter to Leticia.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Guild "recruit" who refuses. A colonel with 4,000 miles of telegraph lines and no ambition to kill is precisely the man the Guild cannot buy; his Indian Protection Service records could be the archive the Guild wants read before it moves into Amazonian ground. Respect: the peoples he contacted are real and living.

Kermit Roosevelt — the son who was there — 1889–1943

  • Where: Oyster Bay; Africa (1909); Brazil (1913–14); Mesopotamia; New York; Fort Richardson, Alaska.
  • What happened: Born 10 October 1889 at Sagamore Hill, Kermit went with his father on the 1909 African expedition as a Harvard freshman. On the River of Doubt his rope- and canoe-handling skill "was instrumental in saving his father's life"; he downplayed his own sickness to save quinine for his father, nearly dying himself. He married Belle Wyatt Willard in 1914, founded the Roosevelt Steamship Company and United States Lines, served the British Army in Mesopotamia (Military Cross, 1918), was Audubon Society president (1935–37), and served with British and U.S. forces in the Second World War until discharged on health grounds in 1941. He died by suicide at Fort Richardson, Alaska, on 4 June 1943, after years of depression and alcoholism.

Simplício drowns in the rapids — 15 March 1914

  • Where: the River of Doubt, Brazil (rapids of the middle river).
  • What happened: On 15 March 1914 the bowsman Simplício and Kermit were in a dugout in the rapids; a whirlpool tore them into mid-stream, the boat filled and overturned, and Roosevelt wrote: "Poor Simplicio must have been pulled under at once". Kermit clung to the overturned boat, was swept downstream and reached an overhanging branch and shore with his dog Trigueiro (the dog's name is in Roosevelt's text), almost drowned. Kermit and a man named João searched three or four miles downstream for the body and the canoe, finding neither, though they salvaged a box of provisions and a paddle. On 16 March a new large canoe was lost when its rope broke; Luiz was nearly drowned lowering it.
