Overview

43 place entries. Mexico City is the capital of what is left — UNCEDED MEXICO under the setting's treaty line (Mexico City, Puebla, Morelos, Estado de México, Querétaro all fall on the Mexican side). The most useful finding is structural: the capital's places carry three layers each — a pre-Hispanic layer (the Zócalo's Aztec precinct, the Templo Mayor under the townhouses, Cuicuilco under the lava, Cholula's hollowed hill, Tepoztlán's pulque temple, Chalma's cave), a viceregal-Inquisitorial layer (the Palace of the Inquisition that became the 1930s medical school; the Quemadero at the west end of the Alameda; a Judaizer's "house" on the Cacahuatal), and a Revolutionary layer (the Ciudadela's 1913 shelling, the Embassy Pact, Lecumberri, the unfinished legislative dome standing 25 years until it was made a tomb). The strongest period documents are: González Obregón's 1922 street-legend chapters (the woman shod like a mule; the stone arm with the monstrance; the priest blindfolded to hear two confessions on 15 Sept 1791 — a case with a real archive file and no solution; Treviño de Sobremonte's "Add wood, my money paid for it"); Rivera's National Palace stair (1929–35) and Bellas Artes (1934, sinking four metres and finished by a different architect); the Basilica's hidden image (a copy hung 1926 to June 1929); and the Embassy Pact place itself (a president chosen inside a US embassy in Feb 1913). Corrections to the prompt: the "Casa de los Perros" is in Querétaro, not Mexico City; the Island of the Dolls is a mid-20th-century, modern legend (dolls from "the mid-20th century"; Barrera d. 2001; the drowned girl unproved) — do not place it in a 1930s scene as fact; Trotsky's house was built up into a fortress (walls, watchtowers) only after the 24 May 1940 attack, so a 1937–39 scene has an open garden. What bends: Trotsky (no Soviet Union); the German colony and legation (Germany won; no Nazi Germany — Imperial patronage of other people's revolutions); the US embassy (an occupier's mission in a capital that lost a war — status open); Morrow at the embassy and Cuernavaca (Coolidge never president); Chapultepec's Sonoran/Coahuilan presidents (their states are CEDED NORTH); Rivera's National Palace history, which stops in 1930 and would have to contain a lost war.

Part 1 — The Centro Histórico: the square, the palace, the cathedral, the pit

(All of Mexico City is UNCEDED MEXICO — the capital of what is left after the treaty. Where an entry does not say otherwise, "holds" means the place and its record are untouched; "BENDS" says how the setting's 1916 break changes it.)

The Zócalo (Plaza de la Constitución) — the main square, Centro Histórico

  • What it was in the 1930s: The great square: the Metropolitan Cathedral on the north, the National Palace on the east, the Federal District buildings on the south, the Portal de Mercaderes on the west, the Nacional Monte de Piedad at the NW corner and the Templo Mayor site just NE, out of sight. The plaza was re-landscaped in 1914 (the century-old ash trees taken out, lawns and paths laid, palms planted at each corner); the 1878 iron kiosk (donated by Antonio Escandón, modelled on one in the Bois de Boulogne) still stood in it; the trams and lighting had been electric since 1894; market stalls kept creeping back in. The Wikipedia page records no specific 1920s–30s works — treat the plaza's 1930s look as the 1914 layout (UNVERIFIED for later changes). The Grito is shouted from the Palace balcony to this square every 15 September; 20 November 1935 it is the Battle of the Zócalo (Gold Shirts vs. taxi drivers — see M01).
  • Why an investigator goes there: Every parade, riot, funeral procession and presidential appearance in the country ends here; it is where a crowd can be planted, counted and photographed. It is also the place where the city's oldest layers are stacked: the Aztec sacred precinct NE, the colonial Plaza Mayor (with the Parián — the shops selling Manila-galleon goods, 1703 until it was looted and destroyed in the Parián Riots of 1828), and, per M02, the ground where the Coatlicue (13 Aug 1790) and the Sun Stone (17 Dec 1790) were dug up and where the Inquisition burned Luis de Carvajal in 1596.
  • The odd detail: "Zócalo" means plinth. The name comes from the base of a monument to independence that Santa Anna began and never finished — the plaza is named for the pedestal of a statue that was never raised. "Though 'buried long ago,'" the article says, the name stuck.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C3%B3calo (read via fetch; extraction limited to what the page states) · M01 Gold Shirts entry · M02 Coatlicue entry (their own sources)
  • Confidence: High for the dates above; Low for the 1920s–30s appearance beyond the 1914 layout.
  • Divergence check: holds (pre-1916 layout; 1913 shelling of the Palace happened). What bends is who watches it: in a capital of a rump state, an American ambassador's window may look on this square (see the US embassy entry).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a plinth with nothing on it. Someone finally intends to raise the thing the pedestal was made for — and the trial of that intent is a Zócalo rally on 15 September, with the Grito as the trigger.

The Metropolitan Cathedral — north side of the Zócalo

  • What it was in the 1930s: The largest cathedral in the Americas, built 1573–1813 (interior consecrated 1667; towers, façade and dome finished last) on soft lake clay that has been sinking it ever since. Inside: the Altar of Forgiveness and the Altar of the Kings (1718–1737, Jerónimo de Balbás, Churrigueresque, 13.75 × 25 × 7.5 m — "la cueva dorada", the golden cave; the Altar of Forgiveness carries the first estípite column in the Americas) and two of the largest 18th-century organs in the Americas. The page records that the building was closed "in the days of the Cristero War" (dates not given; the Calles Law and the silence of the bells are 14 June / 31 July 1926 — see M01).
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is the seat of the Archbishop of Mexico and the Church's face in a state that has just tried to strangle it. Records, relics and the IHS's closest neighbours; and the archive of what lay under the church floor.
  • The odd detail: The Sun Stone hung on the cathedral's west tower for 95 years (1790 → the National Museum, Aug 1885 — M02). Later (post-1940, so marked as modern): the 1947 bell accident that killed a novice, after which the bell was "punished" by taking out its clapper — "la castigada"/"la muda" until 2000; the 1967 fire; a 1742 time capsule found in 2007. None of these is period, but "the mute bell" is a ready-made local legend if the GM lets a 1930s bell be silent for its own reasons.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico_City_Metropolitan_Cathedral (read; it gives no detail on the Calles-Law years or any 1930s stabilisation works) · M01 / M02
  • Confidence: High on construction dates; the Cristero-War closure is stated without dates.
  • Divergence check: holds — the cathedral is in the unceded capital, and the Calles Law and Cristero War are in unceded Mexico (M01). In the setting the Church has also lost a war's worth of northern dioceses to the occupier; the archdiocese in the capital is the Church's last Mexican head.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): IHS — a building that sinks a little each year because it stands on something that does not want a church. The registers of the cathedral's Sagrario (parish) record baptisms — and a name that appears in the 1690s and again in 1934.

The National Palace — east side of the Zócalo (Rivera's stairway murals, 1929–35)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The seat of the federal executive and, in the 1920s–30s, of the Ministries of Finance, War, Justice, the Interior and Foreign Relations and the Supreme Court; the president no longer lived in it (the residence had moved to Chapultepec Castle and, from 1934, to Los Pinos). Under Calles an engineer (Alberto J. Pani) and an architect (Augusto Petriccioli) added a third floor in 1926–29 and the red tezontle façade. Diego Rivera painted "The Epic of the Mexican People" on the main stairwell 1929–35: 450 m², three panels — pre-Hispanic Mexico (Quetzalcóatl at the centre); the Conquest and Independence; and, on the left, a critique of early-20th-century capitalism; eleven further panels on the middle floor were left unfinished. The Independence Bell rung by Hidalgo at Dolores on 16 Sep 1810 hung over the balcony (moved here 1896; its niche rebuilt 1926–30).
  • Why an investigator goes there: Federal files, the president's ear, a minister's signature — and, as the wall itself, a long history of the country painted by a man who would paint anything. Also the Grito (15 Sept, ~11 p.m.) and the audience the bell is rung to.
  • The odd detail: The palace stands on Moctezuma II's "New Houses"; the stairway mural shows it. Recent digs beside it (post-1940 — modern) uncovered part of the Casa Denegrida, "the Black House" — a windowless, black-painted chamber where the emperor consulted seers, per the article. And, from the period's own book of street legends (González Obregón, 1922): every morning at seven the Viceroy Revilla Gigedo was shaved by the barber Teodoro Guerrero while he read complaints left overnight in a box at the palace's main door — the first "complaint box" in the building, older than the ministries.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Palace_(Mexico) · https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015 (Luis González Obregón, Las calles de México: Leyendas y sucedidos, 1922, "Tradición del Palacio Nacional")
  • Confidence: High on the mural dates and the third floor; the "Black House" is a modern find (date not stated).
  • Divergence check: BENDS. Rivera's wall ends its history in 1930 with "early 20th-century capitalism"; in the setting a lost war has happened in between (1916–17: the treaty, the ceded north, an American customs house at Veracruz). Either the mural has a fourth panel a real Rivera would have painted, or the middle-floor panels were "left unfinished" on purpose. Who occupies the Palace is open (M01 Part E: Mexican leaders after the treaty are a named gap).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers of truth on one staircase: the official history of the wall; the folklore (the bell, the ghosts of the ministers); and what is actually behind a panel that was never finished. Secret Service wants to know who ordered the eleven panels left blank.

The Templo Mayor site — NE of the Zócalo, behind the Cathedral (Calle Seminario / Guatemala)

  • What it was in the 1930s: Not yet an archaeological zone. The Aztecs' great twin temple lay southwest of where scholars first looked — Leopoldo Batres dug "under the Cathedral" at the end of the 19th century because the cathedral was wrongly believed to stand on it. Manuel Gamio, 1913–14, "found part of the southwest corner of the temple" and put the finds on public display, but "the discovery did not generate great public interest in excavating further as the zone was an upper-class residential area." In 1933 Emilio Cuevas located "part of a staircase and beam." (Later: 1948 serpent heads and offerings; 1966; 21 Feb 1978 electricity workers strike the Coyolxauhqui disk — modern, and the reason it is a museum today.)
  • Why an investigator goes there: A 1930s dig is a trench in a wealthy man's yard. Who owns the lot, who signs the permit, and who took the finds home. The site is also the ritual centre a "revivalist cult" (the setting's Aztec-revivalist cults — ALTERNATE_HISTORY §29) would insist on.
  • The odd detail: The most sacred place in the Aztec world was left covered by townhouses on purpose — a public discovery in 1913–14 was put on show and then ignored because the neighbours were rich. A 1933 staircase and beam sat under the ground for 45 years before the big disk.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Templo_Mayor (read). Note: the page names "Emilio Cuevas" for 1933; some sources write "Cueto" — ⟨fixed: V_M12⟩ Arqueología Mexicana confirms Emilio Cuevas, 1933, at the corner of Seminario and Guatemala (18 test pits; an alfarda and steps of tezontle in the first), and confirms Gamio's dig of 6–16 May 1914 (https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/la-zona-arqueologica-del-templo-mayor-1913-1933).
  • Confidence: High (dates), High (the 1933 finder's surname is confirmed).
  • Divergence check: holds (1913–14, and the 1930s digs are in unceded Mexico). The 1978 find and later is out of period.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Aztec-revivalist cult does not need the Coyolxauhqui — it already knows the 1933 staircase. An investigator finds a cellar of a Roma-side townhouse that connects to a beam of the old temple, and someone has been sweeping it clean.

The Plaza de Santo Domingo and the Palace of the Inquisition — north of the Zócalo, República de Brasil/Venezuela

  • What it was in the 1930s: The Holy Office's palace (Pedro de Arrieta, 1732–36) was the National School of Medicine from 1854 to 1956 — so all through the 1930s Mexico's doctors trained in the building where the Inquisition kept its prisoners (M02). The Dominican church beside it is where the 15 June 1625 "auto particular de la fe" reconciled the judaizer Tomás Treviño de Sobremonte (as printed in the summary of his case that González Obregón quotes) — the same Treviño who would later be burned alive (see Casa del Judío below).
  • Why an investigator goes there: Autopsies, anatomical specimens, medical-school gossip, and an archive that rarely comes up: the school's own museum of preserved specimens (UNVERIFIED for the 1930s; the Museum of Mexican Medicine opened in 1980 — modern). The neighbouring Plaza de Santo Domingo was the city's public-scribe square (the evangelistas who wrote letters for the illiterate; UNVERIFIED, not on a page read).
  • The odd detail: The Inquisition's palace became a teaching hospital for cutting up the dead — and the Inquisition's own Puerta Falsa (the false door) is a street name a block away (see the next entry).
  • Sources: M02 (its entry for the Palace of the Inquisition; not re-fetched here) · https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015 ("La casa del judío", pp. 62–64)
  • Confidence: High on dates via M02; UNVERIFIED on 1930s museum specifics.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A medical student's anatomy specimen carries a tattoo of a symbol the Inquisition recorded in 1625. Kabbalahs (contracted by the Church for work it forbids its own people) are the natural consultants — and a Church that has a stake in the anatomy school's cellars.

The Calle de la Mujer Herrada (Puerta Falsa de Santo Domingo, now Calle del Perú) — Centro

  • What it was in the 1930s: A narrow street behind Santo Domingo, once crossed by an acequia (canal). The legend is period-attested in print: González Obregón, 1922, retells it from an older source, saying it is recorded in the Life of Padre Don José Vidal (printed 1752 at the Colegio de San Ildefonso) and in Francisco de Sedano's Noticias de México, and set "between 1670 and 1680". Legend: a priest lived in sin at house no. 3, calle de la Puerta Falsa de Santo Domingo; his compadre, a blacksmith on Rejas de Balvanera (the "Casa del Pujavante"), warned him in vain. One night two men (the text says dos negros — the period's framing, which then turns them into demons) brought the priest's mule to be shod at once; the blacksmith did it and rebuked the men for beating the animal. In the morning the priest was still in bed with his companion, denied sending anyone — and the woman was dead, with the same four horseshoes nailed to her hands and feet, a bit in her mouth and the marks of the beating on her. The men, they concluded, were demons. Three witnesses (the parish priest of Santa Catarina, Padre Vidal, and a Carmelite) buried her in the house floor and swore silence.
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is a documented rumour of a body under a house floor in the old town. A 1930s renovation that finds a skeleton — or a set of horseshoes.
  • The odd detail: González Obregón notes the blacksmith's house was called the "Casa del Pujavante" because over its door were carved a pujavante (farrier's paring knife) and crossed tongs — "memoria" of the miracle — which Sedano says he saw several times. The period's own historian names the carved lintel as the proof (the house has since been rebuilt).
  • Sources: https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015 (pp. 109–112) — text read this session. Label: FOLKLORE, first attestation 1752 (Vidal's Life, by report of González Obregón); not re-checked against the 1752 print.
  • Confidence: High that the legend is in the 1922 book; Low on any historical event.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers. The period reads it as demonic justice; the folklore keeps it as a warning to priests; what is actually under the floor is a grave with a horseshoe count that does not match the legend.

The Casa de los Azulejos — Avenida Madero (Calle Madero) at Calle Condesa, Centro

  • What it was in the 1930s: The tile-clad palace of the Counts of the Valle de Orizaba, remodelled by the Sanborn brothers in the two years from 1917 to 1919 as the famous restaurant and drugstore — with José Clemente Orozco's Omniscience (1925) on the stairway, and a 1931 national-monument designation. Before that: the top floor rented to the Jockey Club from 1881 ("the most exclusive social club" 1880–1914); a banquet for Victoriano Huerta in 1914; Carranza seized the property in 1915 for some months; the Zapatista army occupied it briefly; one floor housed the Casa del Obrero Mundial (the anarcho-syndicalist union hall) for a short time.
  • Why an investigator goes there: The best Anglophone meeting-place in the capital — American drugstore fittings under a Mexican aristocrat's roof — which makes it neutral ground. Society lunches, tip-offs, a message left at the counter.
  • The odd detail: The name legend is printed in 1922 (González Obregón): the spendthrift heir of the Count of Orizaba was told by his tired father, "Hijo, tú nunca harás casa de azulejos" ("Son, you will never make a house of tiles") — and the stung heir then rebuilt the palace in tiles. The house had been bought in 1596 (possession 2 December 1596) by Don Diego Suárez de Peredo for 6,500 pesos at a creditors' auction.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casa_de_los_Azulejos (read; the page does not itself give the name legend) · https://archive.org/details/gonzalez-obregon-luis.-las-calles-de-mexico-epl-fs-2015 (pp. 47–55)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds for 1914–1919 (the Sanborns' remodel began in 1917 — exactly the year the setting's Southern War was won, so an American concessionaire arriving at the Count's palace as the treaty lands is a natural setting detail). Wilson's fall in 1916 changes nothing here.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Golden Circle's Mexican man meets his contact at the Sanborns counter under the Orozco mural; the old Jockey Club membership rolls are why the lunch counter has a private door.

The Alameda Central and the Quemadero — between Avenida Juárez and Hidalgo

  • What it was in the 1930s: The city's central park, established 11 January 1592 by Viceroy Luis de Velasco II. Its 1920s–30s furniture: a bandstand and electric lamps, the central kiosk and a starburst of paths; the white semicircular Hemiciclo a Juárez (1910); and the Palacio de Bellas Artes rising at its east edge. (Hotel del Prado and Rivera's Sueño de una tarde dominical en la Alameda Central are 1947, modern.)
  • Why an investigator goes there: Stroller's park: a place to be seen, to hand over an envelope, to sit on a bench with a newspaper while the Bellas Artes doors open.
  • The odd detail: The western part of the Alameda was the Quemadero — the Inquisition's burning place, where "witches and others convicted by the Inquisitors were publicly burned at the stake", until Viceroy Marqués de Croix demolished it in 1770 to enlarge the park. Two centuries later couples stroll the paths over it.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alameda_Central (read)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A bench under a certain tree is where every "witch" of the 1600s left her last testimony; a Sunday visitor feels the same "stopped wind" in the same spot every week.

The Palacio de Bellas Artes — east end of the Alameda, Avenida Juárez / Eje Central

  • What it was in the 1930s: Adamo Boari began the "Gran Teatro de Ópera" on 1 October 1904; it missed the 1910 centenary, "hardly begun" by 1913, and stalled with the Revolution; Federico Mariscal resumed work in 1932, redoing the interior in Art Deco, and it was inaugurated 29 November 1934 with La verdad sospechosa. Meanwhile the building had sunk about four metres into the clay. Its stage curtain is a Tiffany glass foldable panel of nearly a million pieces, weighing 24 tons, showing Popocatépetl and Iztaccíhuatl. Rivera recreated Man at the Crossroads here in 1934 after the Rockefeller Center mural was destroyed and painted over in New York; Orozco's Katharsis and Siqueiros's New Democracy are on the walls.
  • Why an investigator goes there: A gala night: every minister, ambassador and banker in one lit hall, and a curtain of glass that can be made to hide a stage manoeuvre. The murals are the story of a country's quarrels painted on its opera house.
  • The odd detail: The opera house sank about four metres into the clay over the thirty years it took to finish — and was finished, in Art Deco, by a different architect than the Italian who began it. Its most famous object is a 24-ton glass curtain of two volcanoes, made in New York.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_Bellas_Artes (read)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly. Man at the Crossroads was removed from Rockefeller Center in 1933–34 (widely reported to be over a portrait of Lenin — not confirmed on the page read); in the setting there is no Bolshevik Russia, so the offending panel could not be Lenin. What survives is the fight over a mural, and the reason for the repaint would have to be something else — e.g. an American face on the wall.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Southern War's treaty was, in this timeline, signed — or shown — under a glass volcano. Secret Service wants the stage plans from 1934.

The Edificio La Nacional — Avenida Juárez, beside Bellas Artes

  • What it was in the 1930s: Mexico City's first high-rise: 13 storeys, 56 m, built 1929–1932 by Manuel Ortiz Monasterio for the Compañía Nacional de Seguros; steel frame engineered against earthquakes, five elevators, 15,000 m² of floor; Art Deco. It was the tallest building in the city until the Edificio Corcuera (1937). (Survival of the 1957 and 1985 quakes and the 1949 "La Nacional II" are out of period.)
  • Why an investigator goes there: An insurer's ledger — policies on lives, cargoes, ships and burned churches. In a Depression, insurance frauds and fires are how fortunes move; the same building where the top floor faces Bellas Artes.
  • The odd detail: A skyscraper on lake clay in a city that sinks a little every year — its engineers designed the steel frame for the shaking; the sinking is the part nobody had a plan for.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edificio_La_Nacional (read)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — a domestic Mexican commercial building; a colonial-speculation Depression (the setting's cause) would have had insurers overextended as well.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): An insurance clerk who processes every claim for burnt churches in the Cristero War discovers the same three names on the "act of God" line.

Part 2 — Prison, monument, markets, barrios, cemeteries, and the streets that carried the legends

Lecumberri, "the Black Palace" — east of the Centro (street/colonia not confirmed on the page read)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The federal penitentiary, opened in 1900 by Porfirio Díaz. Designed by Miguel S. Macedo (who was himself imprisoned there during the Revolution) on Bentham's panopticon plan — one guard-post sees every cell. It had 804 cells, built for 800 men, 180 women and 400 children, with workshops, a nursery and a medical section; in practice it was chronically over-full (the page gives the design capacity only; 1930s headcounts UNVERIFIED). Madero and Pino Suárez were murdered during their transfer from the National Palace to Lecumberri, 22 Feb 1913 (M01); José de León Toral, Obregón's assassin, was shot at Lecumberri on 9 Feb 1929 (M01, "¡Viva Cristo Rey!"). The page also lists Pancho Villa as escaping in 1912 — ⟨fixed: V_M12⟩ WRONG for Lecumberri: Villa was held at Belem, transferred to the Santiago Tlatelolco prison on 7 June 1912 and escaped there on Christmas Day 1912 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pancho_Villa); Lecumberri's page has it loosely. Decommissioned 1976; turned over to the Archivo General de la Nación in 1980 — modern; the archive now holds the paper record of everything the prison held.

The Calle de Chavarría and the stone arm holding a monstrance — Justo Sierra (2a) / Donceles, Centro

The Calle del Olmedo (now 6ª Correo Mayor) — and the priest taken blindfolded to hear two confessions, 15 September 1791

  • What it was in the 1930s: A street with a documented 1791 case and a legend grown from it. The "Casa del Judío" — Calle del Cacahuatal, barrio de San Pablo (south-central); and the Quemadero on 11 April 1649

  • What it was in the 1930s: A ruin, "demolished not many years" before 1922: a decayed 16th-century house popularly said to have been the home of Tomás Treviño de Sobremonte, a merchant burned alive by the Inquisition. Cuicuilco and the Pedregal — the buried pyramid, south of San Ángel

  • What it was in the 1930s: A circular pyramid with a conical base, begun a few centuries BCE (Late Preclassic), abandoned c. 150–200 CE, then buried by the eruption of Xitle (dates given range from 245–315 CE to c. 400 CE), leaving the Pedregal de San Ángel lava field (about 80 km², over 10 m deep in places). Chalma — the Black Christ's cave — Malinalco, Estado de México

  • What it was in the 1930s: A pilgrimage sanctuary, reached on foot. Cholula — the largest pyramid in the world, with a church on top — Puebla state

  • The odd detail: The world's largest pyramid was mistaken for a hill by the people who built a church on it — and then hollowed out with an archaeologist's tunnels in 1931.

  1. What is the American presence in the capital? The 1913 Embassy Pact happened (pre-1916). After the break, does Mexico City host an ambassador, a resident commissioner, or a customs-and-treaty office attached to the embassy? The setting says only "held" (north), "held outright" (Tampico), "held by the ledger" (Veracruz customs) — Mexico City itself is unruled.
  2. Who governs the rump state? Chapultepec's presidents (Díaz, Carranza, Obregón, Calles) are mostly northerners; Carranza (Coahuila), Obregón and Calles (Sonora) come from CEDED NORTH states. M01 Part E already asks this; this lane adds the place: whose window looks onto the Zócalo?
  3. What does Rivera's National Palace stair show? It ends in 1930; a lost war has intervened. Either the Southern War is on the wall, or the middle-floor panels were "left unfinished" for a reason.
  4. What does the German legation do in a world Germany won? The Colegio Alemán (1894) and the German embassy that backed the Gold Shirts become the "patron of other people's revolutions" — a quiet second power.
  5. Trotsky's house: erased with the USSR, or refilled with a German-world exile (M01)? The fortress after 24 May 1940 is a post-1938 event, so a 1930s scene has an open garden.

Top 10 for the owner

  1. The priest blindfolded on the Calle del Olmedo, 15 September 1791 — a real case with a real archive file, two people confessed and (presumably) killed, no bodies, the priest keeping the seal to his grave; the legend grew a mad friar out of it. A perfect three layers scene, printed in a 1922 book.
  2. The Basilica's hidden image — from 1926 to June 1929 the nation's holiest picture on the wall was a copy by Rafael Aguirre; the original was "kept safe". Which one was returned?
  3. The Templo Mayor under the townhouses — found and put on display in 1913–14, then left because the quarter was rich; a 1933 staircase and beam; nothing more until 1978.
  4. The Inquisition's palace as the 1930s medical school — Mexico's doctors learned anatomy where the Holy Office held prisoners, for 102 years (1854–1956).
  5. Treviño de Sobremonte's "Echen leña, que mi dinero me cuesta" — a documented burning at the Alameda's Quemadero (11 April 1649), a "haunted house" nobody can date, and the Santo Niño that kept the scars.
  6. The Monument to the Revolution — a legislative palace left as a steel skeleton for 25 years, then turned into a tomb; the country's parliament became its mausoleum.
  7. The US Embassy and the Pact (19 February 1913) — a president of Mexico chosen inside an embassy; the building is the setting's oldest American intrusion into the capital.
  8. Cholula — a church on the world's largest pyramid, hollowed by archaeologists from 1931; consecrated ground ready-made.
  9. Tepoztlán — Redfield's 1926–27 village, published by the University of Chicago Press (1930): the bridge between the Chicago book and the Mexico book.
  10. The Casa de los Azulejos — the tile palace where a Jockey Club, Huerta's banquet, a Carranza seizure, the Zapatistas, an anarcho-syndicalist union hall and the Sanborn brothers all lived under one roof between 1881 and 1919, with the name legend printed in 1922.
