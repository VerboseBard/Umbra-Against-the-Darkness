M18 — Mexican Chicago, 1916–1940: the colonias and their people

  • Lane: M18 — the city's Mexican colonias (Near West Side / "Colonia Hull House", Back of the Yards and Brighton Park, South Chicago and the Southeast Side), their institutions, press, music, sport, crime and the scholars who studied them.
  • Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 · Method: WebSearch/WebFetch (search worked this session); the richest source is the Newberry's Chicago Foreign Language Press Survey (FLPS) — WPA-era English translations of the Chicago Spanish-language papers México (1928–30), El Nacional (1930–33), El Liberal (1933), La Lucha (1932–34) — plus the Spanish edition of Robert Redfield's 1924–25 Chicago field diary (Arias & Durand, Mexicanos en Chicago, 2008; chapter 3 read in full from a downloaded PDF). FLPS URLs below are https://flps.newberry.org/id/<id> (listing pages: https://flps.newberry.org/?group=spanish&page=N, 43 pages, read pp. 1–16, 18, 20, 22, 24, 26, 28, 30).
  • Cross-references (not repeated here): Our Lady of Guadalupe, South Chicago, its cornerstone (14 Apr 1928), dedication (30 Sep 1928) and the St. Jude shrine (Fr. James Tort, first novena 17 Feb 1929) → M07, R15 §J, R05; the exiled archbishop of Morelia's Chicago years and St. Francis of Assisi's Claretians (1927) → M07 (this file adds what Chicago's own surveys say about it); the Indiana Harbor colonia, its Cuadro Dramático, El Amigo del Hogar, the $15-a-head trains, Judge Crites and the Legion → R37, M06 Part E, R15 (repatriation, "two faiths"); Memorial Day 1937 and Earl Handley → R37, R03; Mexican repatriation overview → M06, R10. Entries below give the Chicago detail those files lack.
  • Overview. 46 entries. The record is unusually good because a University of Chicago graduate student walked the colonias in 1924–25, a Protestant survey walked them again in 1928–31, and the WPA translated the colony's own newspapers in the 1930s. What comes out is not a quaint immigrant story. It is a colony of railroad-camp men living in boxcars, boarding-house families on Halsted Street beside Hull House, and mill hands in South Chicago; a consulate that ran fundraisers and (quietly) repatriation trains; a Masonic lodge called "Lux in Umbra"; a stage that put on Pancho Villa in four acts on 2 March 1929; and a steady low-level war with Polish, Italian and Irish neighbours and with the police, told in the press as robberies, knife fights and one dead policeman on Buffalo Avenue (killed 27 January 1930; México reported it 30 January) ⟨fixed: V_M18⟩. The best single fact for the divergence: the Chicago colonia came overwhelmingly from Guanajuato, Jalisco, Michoacán, San Luis Potosí and Zacatecas — states that stay in Mexico under the setting's treaty — or from Texas via the railroads. The annexed north (Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila, Monterrey) supplies a minority in the real record. So "many of these Mexicans are American nationals from the annexed north" is a choice the setting can make (and each entry says what it changes), not something the real record already shows.
  • Divergence rule for this lane (proposal, for the owner). Three legal classes stand in one Chicago pew: (1) subjects of the rump Mexican state (Jalisco, Michoacán, Guanajuato…), whom the Chicago consul represents; (2) people from the ceded north, whom the treaty may have made American subjects/citizens (still open: who governs the ceded north; citizen, subject or alien?) and whom the consulate can no longer speak for; (3) Tejano and other US-born Mexican Americans, citizens either way. Real-record events that depend on Mexican politics after 1916 (Calles, Portes Gil, Ortiz Rubio, Vasconcelos, Cristeros) BEND because "Mexico's leaders after the treaty" is a named gap (PEOPLE_OF_THE_TIMELINE.md). Events on the American side (steel, packing, police, Depression relief, the Memorial Day Massacre) hold, with "no Red Scare" recolouring the rhetoric (1919 strikers as German-funded or anarchist instead of Bolshevik).
  • Note on sources. FLPS items are translators' English; I paraphrase. Where an FLPS listing summary was all I read (not the article) the entry says "listing only". Two internal date oddities found in the FLPS are flagged in their entries (a Saturday arrest dated a Tuesday; a 1933 film in a "1927" scrapbook).

A. Arrivals and work

01. Three doors into Chicago — Redfield's finding, 1924–25 (migration began 1916)

  • Where: Chicago's railroad yards, plants and cheap housing; the beet fields of Michigan.
  • What happened: Robert Redfield's 1924–25 diary (per Arias & Durand) fixes the start of the Mexican migration to Chicago in 1916, in the middle of the Great War, and its real growth after 1921, after a short depression his informants still remembered. He found three ways in: (1) company-induced migrants, mostly railroad hires recruited from track gangs in the US South; (2) men who arrived on their own and went straight to industrial plants; (3) itinerant beet workers who came to Chicago in early winter after the Michigan harvest, with savings, and were often jobless by January–March, when charity agencies saw the worst distress. Once inside a plant, hiring ran by kin: a foreman told a worker to fetch a brother or cousin and next day one appeared. Origins named in the diary and the studies it cites: Guanajuato, Jalisco, Michoacán, San Luis Potosí, Zacatecas; many families already had long histories as farm labour in the US Southwest.
  • The odd detail: the winter tide — beet-field men drifting into Chicago in December with a few dollars, spending down through the snow, showing up at relief desks in February.
  • People: Robert Redfield — the graduate student; Ernest W. Burgess — his supervisor; Manuel Bueno — his Mexican contact and interpreter.
  • Sources: https://www.princeton.edu/~jdurand/PDF_varios/Mexicanos_en_Chicago_2008/Cap%C3%ADtulo_03.pdf (downloaded and read) · https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S1665-89062009000200009
  • Confidence: High for what the editors report Redfield found; the diary itself was not read.
  • Divergence check: BENDS. 1916 is the setting's break year and the Southern War starts that year, so the flow's start date holds but its shape changes: a war fought on Mexican soil by Americans would push far more refugees north in 1916–19; and the central-western states these migrants came from stay in Mexico under the treaty (foreign nationals of the rump state), while a minority from Sonora/Chihuahua/Coahuila/Monterrey would be people of the annexed north. The Michigan beet-field leg is American and holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the winter drift is a cover for anyone moving through the Midwest unnoticed; a GM can seat a refugee with something from a burned Jalisco chapel in a December boxcar camp.

02. Traqueros and the boxcar colonias — 1900s–1930s (camps mapped 1928)

  • Where: Chicago's rail corridors — 19th and Rockwell, 66th and Martin Luther King Dr., 102nd St. (East Side), 59th and Cicero; suburban camps at Elgin, Aurora (about 1,000 residents by the late 1920s), Hegewisch, Broadview, Bridgeview; the CB&Q's Eola parts-recycling plant (closed 1934).
  • What happened: From the turn of the century through the 1950s the railroads (Burlington, Santa Fe, Rock Island, Chicago & Alton, Chicago & North Western, Elgin Joliet & Eastern, New York Central, Pennsylvania, Illinois Central) recruited Mexican track workers ("traqueros") by promising steady pay and family housing — and delivered abandoned, wheel-less boxcars parked beside the tracks. Residents added porches, gardens and "places of worship" to boxcars with no windows and only wood stoves. Anita Edgar Jones's 1928 University of Chicago study identified about two dozen boxcar communities (the museum curators later mapped her list: more than twenty on the South Side, reaching Elgin and Aurora) ⟨fixed: V_M18⟩. In 1918 the Chicago & Alton raised its wage from 21 cents to 30–37 cents an hour when its Mexican workers refused to act as strikebreakers. Redfield (1924) confirms the boxcar camps and that men liked the rail jobs and the nearness to work.
  • The odd detail: a Mexican workforce that refused to scab in 1918 and won a raise of nearly 50 percent — the reverse of the image the mills' 1919 hiring created.
  • People: Anita Edgar Jones — 1928 mapper; Antonio Delgado — planner and historian of the camps; José Ordáz, Julia Pérez, Emilio Aguirre — camp residents whose objects and stories the 2016 exhibit displayed.
  • Sources: https://southsideweekly.com/traqueros-de-chicago-museum-exhibit-takes-mexican-labor-history-out-of-the-shadows/ · https://old.ilhumanities.org/news/2016/04/explore-history-of-the-mexican-railroad-boxcar-communities-in-chicago/ · Redfield chapter (as in entry 01)
  • Confidence: High on the camps and the 1918 raise (one source for the numbers).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic railroads. The recruits' origins may include annexed-north men who already knew rail work on the Texas lines; the setting's Golden Circle (Havana-centred) has a natural interest in railroads that end in Mexico.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a boxcar camp is a parish, a hospital and a hiding place with no street address and no city services; the IHS's Chicago desk would minister to it from a distance, and something hunted by day can live beside a switching yard for a winter.

03. The 1919 steel strike's Mexican strikebreakers — September 1919 (South Chicago and the Calumet)

  • Where: South Chicago's mills (Illinois Steel's South Works); across the state line at Inland Steel, Indiana Harbor (see R37); Gary under martial law.
  • What happened: The national strike began 22 September 1919. To replace strikers, mills brought in Black and Mexican labour: one summary puts it at 30,000–40,000 Black and Mexican workers brought in nationally. At Inland Steel (Indiana Harbor) the company had ~90 Mexicans in 1918, recruited several hundred more for the strike and housed and fed them inside the plant gates; it hired ~3,600 Mexicans in 1919–25 and by 1926 had 2,526 (about 30% of 7,000 workers). A South Chicago account (search summary; not confirmed in a primary source I read) says some managers feared Black strikebreakers would trigger violence in South Chicago and so turned to Mexicans; by 1925 Mexicans were about 8 percent of the region's mill workforce and the second-largest immigrant group on the mill floor after Poles (search summary).
  • The odd detail: men were fed and slept inside the mill gate for the duration; a strike that built two communities (Black and Mexican) that the Depression then turned on.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/imh/article/download/10337/14385 (Rosales & Simon, East Chicago; read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel_strike_of_1919 and https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02619288.2024.2418508 (search summaries only)
  • Confidence: High for Inland's numbers; Medium for the South Chicago "fear of violence" claim and the national totals (search summaries).
  • Divergence check: holds in the domestic calendar; BENDS the rhetoric — with no Soviet Union or Red Scare, management's story is "a revolution by foreign-born radicals" from German-funded anarchists or IWW men; and for the strikebreakers, the 1918 wartime labour agreement (cited in R37) has a different form in a setting where the US is already invading Mexico.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Golden Circle's steel men use the strike to sort Chicago's poor by colour; Section 13 could find that the hiring lists were kept in one hand.

04. Who hired them, and at what wage — the jobs of Mexican Chicago, 1924–28

  • Where: Chicago plants and offices; South Chicago mills; Michigan and Minnesota beet fields.
  • What happened: A December 1928 Social Service Review piece (in the FLPS) says Mexicans held everything from clerical jobs at Montgomery Ward and Wrigley to industrial work at International Harvester and Western Electric; South Chicago Mexicans were mostly steelworkers at 42.5–54 cents an hour on rotating shifts; many were seasonal men who went to Michigan and Minnesota beet fields and came back to a winter with little work; about 162 pupils (9.8 percent) of Mexican schoolchildren were in Catholic schools. Redfield's 1924 informants told him some firms had a rule against hiring Mexicans, big firms took only a few, and a few seemed to be pushing them out; Mexicans competed with Black workers in some plants and with Poles, who got the better jobs, in others; firms that wanted to keep Mexican labour offered to pay the fare back to the border. One paint firm hired Mexicans, informants said, because "their colour" kept the paint from harming them — a period racial belief, reported as such.
  • The odd detail: the same colony had a Montgomery Ward clerk and a South Works furnace hand, and a firm that hired men for a supposed immunity to lead paint.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5423405_1_0058 · Redfield chapter (as above)
  • Confidence: High for the FLPS summary; Medium for Redfield's informants' generalisations.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic labour market. The "colour" logic is the period's own; in the setting the same firms would also need a position on "Mexican" applicants who are, by treaty, nationals of the ceded provinces.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the "colour and paint" belief is a period superstition that a factory doctor could turn into a real poisoning; a real lead-paint plant is a place investigators can visit.

05. The Halsted Street employment-agency swindle — 15–25 October 1928

  • Where: 40 S. Halsted Street, Chicago (fake agency); the depot on departure day; Desplaines Street police station.
  • What happened: A man calling himself José Durán, also "A. B. Deming," opened a fake agency and registered over 200 Mexican men from 15 to 22 October 1928 for "jobs at the Navy Yard in Cleveland" at "five dollars a day minimum," free transportation, furnished rooms, coal, stoves and bedding "guaranteed for six months." He took $5–15 from each — about $3,000 — timed to the week after the seasonal men were paid, and vanished on 20 October, five days before the train. At the depot the "employer, Mr. Deming" proved not to exist; the victims filed complaints at the Desplaines station. On 31 October the paper reported him (now spelled "José Durand," and the victims now "more than three hundred") arrested in Ciudad Juárez by Mexican authorities at the Mexican consul's request ⟨fixed: V_M18: "extradited" is not in the FLPS reports; the Chicago detectives were only "informed of the apprehension"⟩. México ran repeated warnings against "unscrupulous employment agencies," fake utility-company agents and a "fraud ring."
  • The odd detail: the con used a government shipyard's name — and the consul, not the Chicago police, got the man.
  • People: José Durán ("A. B. Deming") — the swindler; the consul (Mexican consulate, Chicago) — who requested the arrest.
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5423405_1_0890 · https://flps.newberry.org/?group=spanish&page=2 and page=3 (listing)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — a Chicago con; the setting's Cleveland Navy Yard would be busier (a war-of-hemisphere fleet), so the bait is more believable.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): 200 Mexican men shipped nowhere and 200 names on a list: a recruiter for a cult or a mine could use the same "Cleveland" promise for real, then send the train elsewhere.

B. The colonias

06. Colonia Hull House — Halsted between Harrison and 15th, 1924–1931

  • Where: Near West Side: Halsted Street from Harrison south to 15th, around Jane Addams's Hull House and the University of Chicago Settlement.
  • What happened: The Church Federation survey by Robert C. Jones (1928–31) counted 7,000–8,000 Mexicans here, "in the poorest quarters in the city," with cheap rents and the employment agencies and plants close by. Neighbours: Greeks between Harrison and Polk, Italians west of Halsted (Polk–Roosevelt), Poles east of Halsted, Jews and Russians north of Roosevelt Road. Institutions Jones lists: Hull House (classes, clubs), St. Mark's Presbyterian-Congregational Church, St. Francis (Catholic; "nearly a thousand" at its special Mass for Mexicans ⟨fixed: V_M18⟩), the Old Town Boys' Club (200+ Mexican boys), and a settlement house for mothers' and daughters' clubs. A 1927 scrapbook headline says the Maxwell Street "ghetto" was "changing its complexion" toward Mexican and Black residents (listing only). A search summary of the Redfield diary edition says ~2,500 Mexicans lived at the Hull House end when he walked it in 1924 (UNVERIFIED — not in the chapter I read). City-wide, ~20,000 Mexicans by the late 1920s, falling to ~14,000 by 1939 (Wikipedia).
  • The odd detail: Redfield's first field day, 5 October 1924, was a walk through this district; the Hull House colonia is the first place a sociologist looked.
  • People: Robert C. Jones — Church Federation surveyor; Jane Addams — founder (d. 1935); Redfield.
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5423405_1_0775 · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexicans_in_Chicago · https://flps.newberry.org/?group=spanish&page=1 (listing)
  • Confidence: High on Jones's layout; Medium on the totals (surveys differ).
  • Divergence check: holds — Chicago geography. BENDS status: part of this Halsted colony would be people of the ceded north (American by treaty, status open) beside refugees of the rump state.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Hull House and a Claretian parish on adjacent blocks, two sets of case files on the same Halsted families; whoever reads both files can find the ones that disappear from one.

07. Fourteen restaurants, five pool halls and a Spanish-language bank window — Halsted Street commerce, 1927–33

  • Where: South Halsted Street; the "West Side Auditorium" (W. Taylor St. and S. Racine Ave.); Ashland Ave.; the Atlas State Bank.
  • What happened: The 1928 Social Service Review counted, near Hull House, fourteen restaurants, five pool halls, five grocery stores and bakeries. The Atlas State Bank opened a Spanish-language department in October 1927 and had about 600 Mexican depositors averaging $600 (and ~400 who bought money orders). The colony's own business names in the press: the South America restaurant on Halsted (Masonic dinners, Feb 1929), La Gardenia Restaurant (Nov 1928), Casa Alex / Café de Alex (Oct 1929, Jan 1932), the Puerto de Veracruz Restaurant (first "popular dinner," 11 Aug 1933), Mexican Joe's (proprietor Francisco Pedroza, 1931–33), Ignacio M. Valle's Mexican store and importer's premises ("El Arte Mexicano," damaged in a May 1931 attack), Salvador Esteiro's import firm (moved to larger premises Apr 1929), the Rialto Music House's "Salon Azteca" (May 1929), and the Arte Mexicano Music House selling Vocalion records at 75 cents by mail (Dec 1928). A May 1929 article announces a planned Mexican commercial center to protect colony trade. By 1929 the community press was already arguing whether Mexican merchants should form a united front.
  • The odd detail: a Mexican importer, a record shop and a Spanish-language bank window in one Depression-era block — the first cash economy the colony built for itself.
  • People: Ignacio M. Valle — merchant and store owner; Salvador Esteiro — export merchant; Francisco Pedroza — "Mexican Joe's."
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5423405_1_0058 · https://flps.newberry.org/id/5423405_1_0807 · https://flps.newberry.org/?group=spanish&page=5, page=6, page=7, page=28 (listings)
  • Confidence: High on the counts; the restaurant names are from event notices (listings), not menus.
  • Divergence check: holds — Chicago commerce; in the setting the importers' stock could come through the Veracruz customs house in American receivership (a customs house on one leg of every shipment).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Ignacio Valle's store is the only shop where anyone in the colony will speak — a natural informant post; and a merchant who sells Mexican pottery and records also sells veladoras and herbs that a curandera requests.

08. Back of the Yards and Brighton Park — the stockyards colonies

  • Where: Stock Yards district — "three colonies, the largest near Ashland and 46th Street" (Jones); Brighton Park (rail terminals, packers); neighbouring Polish and Irish blocks.
  • What happened: Jones's survey found three Mexican colonies at the Stock Yards; the largest near Ashland and 46th. Redfield's informants told him Brighton Park had been Jewish in the 1890s, Polish in the next decade and Mexican from the Great War on — an area still "Mexicanizing." Mexican arrival lowered property values but raised rents, they said, and Mexican shops caused friction with Jewish, Polish and (less) Irish shopkeepers. Redfield's classmate Samuel Cohen, who lived in Brighton Park, was one of his two channels for information there (the other was Manuel Bueno). The neighbourhood's older Central and Eastern European residents "reluctantly allowed" the Mexican enclave as long as it stayed within a few blocks (a general-history summary). Mexican packers appear in the union story only faintly: the 1921–22 Amalgamated Meat Cutters strike (broken with Black strikebreakers) is not documented for Mexicans in the sources I read.
  • The odd detail: three successive tenants of the same Brighton Park blocks in thirty years — Jewish, Polish, Mexican — with the rents rising each time.
  • People: Samuel Cohen — Redfield's Brighton Park contact; Manuel Bueno — Mexican intermediary.
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5423405_1_0775 · https://www.princeton.edu/~jdurand/PDF_varios/Mexicanos_en_Chicago_2008/Cap%C3%ADtulo_03.pdf · https://www.packingtownmuseum.org/people-of-the-stock-yards · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexicans_in_Chicago
  • Confidence: High on the layout; Medium on the "reluctantly allowed" line (secondary).
  • Divergence check: holds. In the setting the stockyards ship beef to a hemisphere at war; Brighton Park's Mexicans are the people who process it.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the packing district is where Chicago's oldest "cult versus population" story lives: a small group in the sheds, a large indifferent neighbourhood, a colony that keeps its lights low.

09. South Chicago and the Southeast Side — the mill colony, 1915–40

  • Where: South Chicago (90th–93rd Sts., Buffalo, Burley, Mackinaw and Commercial Aves.), Irondale/South Deering, Hegewisch, East Side; Illinois Steel's South Works; the Community Hall; the church at 91st and Brandon (see M07).
  • What happened: By 1915 South Works had 10,000 workers and Mexicans were "flowing in" (Chicago Reader/Springs summaries). The FLPS gives a street-level picture: Alfonso Reyes lived at 8425 Mackinaw Ave.; Albert Velasco at 8722 Buffalo Ave.; Juvencio Horta at 9232 Burley Ave.; Joseph García's meat market stood at 9022 Burley Ave.; the mill's own Illinois Steel Company Hospital took the wounded; the Walkowiak mortuary (8803 Commercial Ave.) and Murphy's Funeral Parlor handled the dead. Mill wages ran 42.5–54 cents an hour. Irondale was the older name of the South Deering neighbourhood; residents counted all the Southeast Side neighbourhoods as one Mexican community (search summary of a South Chicago study). The colony's organisations included the Sociedad Mutualista Obreros Libres Mexicanos (Brandon Ave.), the Mexican Band of South Chicago, a Masonic lodge (entry 14) and, from May 1930, an auxiliary of the Mexican Blue Cross (entry 18). The Jackson Baur WPA survey of ~2,000 South Chicago Mexican families is in entry 46.
  • The odd detail: the address book of one small killing is the address book of the colony — a boarding house, a meat market, a funeral parlour, all within a mile of the mill gate.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5423405_1_0869 · https://flps.newberry.org/id/5423405_1_0857 · https://chicagoreader.com/news/quantum-crossroads/south-works-quantum-computing-us-steel/ (search summary only)
  • Confidence: High on addresses (police-report translations); Medium on "Irondale" usage (secondary).
  • Divergence check: holds — steel town. In the setting the mills feed a hemispheric war economy and buy ore from the ceded north (Sonora copper, Coahuila coal); the colony's fathers could be mining that ore's people.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): South Works' hospital, the funeral parlours and the meat market are the cast list of a South Chicago noir on any day.

10. "A photograph, a metate" — what the colony owned, 1924–25

  • Where: Chicago rooming houses, brick tenements and boxcar camps, as walked by Redfield.
  • What happened: Redfield's diary notes Mexican housing as the worst he saw: dark rooms, no windows, cement floors, brick tenements with unmaintained halls and rail-car camps; two United Charities caseworkers told him Mexicans, unlike other immigrants, arrived with no luggage, no valuables, no religious images and no furniture, sometimes only the clothes on their backs; the most a family owned might be a photograph and a metate (a grinding stone). Nearly every family took boarders; he was told only one in ten migrants lived with a family, the rest being single men sending money home; Redfield's own case files show a widow, Sofía Chávez, living with her mother, "serving meals and keeping a lodger." Households of ~8 were normal; single men shared rooms, more than four to a room. Births were at home, with the husband or a midwife, and women refused doctors and birth control (the informants' own pride).
  • The odd detail: United Charities' surprise that Mexican families came with no saint on the wall, in a city whose Catholic immigrants normally carried one.
  • People: Sofía Chávez — a widow in Redfield's case files; two unnamed United Charities workers.
  • Sources: https://www.princeton.edu/~jdurand/PDF_varios/Mexicanos_en_Chicago_2008/Cap%C3%ADtulo_03.pdf
  • Confidence: High for the editors' summary of the diary; individual claims are caseworkers' impressions.
  • Divergence check: holds. The setting's Church would read "no religious image" as an omission worth checking: an IHS priest asks why a devout population arrived without its saints.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the caseworker's note is a lead: a family left its images behind because it left in a hurry from something a picture could not protect them from.

11. The pool halls — Halsted and the Southeast Side, 1924–31

  • Where: Pool halls on Halsted (five near Hull House, 1928), South Chicago, Brighton Park; El Gato Negro billiard parlour (raided Jan 1931).
  • What happened: Redfield found "a great many" pool halls and saw men playing almost everywhere; some rented rooms and beds, and Mexican and Black men mixed there. On 19 March 1929 police raided a poolroom and arrested thirteen Mexicans after finding hidden beer bottles (México); on 7 January 1931 El Nacional protested that police entered "El Gato Negro" with guns drawn, cursed the customers and removed innocent men and asked the consulate to complain to the police chief. The 1925 East Chicago arrest tally (Indiana; R37) — 31 of 44 arrests for alcohol, prostitution or concealed weapons — has a Chicago echo in these reports.
  • The odd detail: the poolroom as post office, employment agency and boarding house for the colony's single men.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5423405_1_0139 · https://flps.newberry.org/id/5423405_1_0881 (listing) · Redfield chapter (as above)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — Prohibition raids, then Repeal (Dec 1933).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a black-cat billiard hall is a good place for an informant with an unlicensed rack of mezcal, a loaded deck and a story about the Cristero war.

C. Faith and health

12. St. Francis of Assisi — the Near West Side's Mexican parish, 1927–1930s (cross-ref M07)

  • Where: St. Francis of Assisi Church, on Roosevelt Road, Near West Side (exact street address UNVERIFIED — M07 gives 813 W. Roosevelt), just south of the Hull House blocks.

19. The consulate — Aveleyra, the Domínguezes and the colony's watchdogs, 1928–33

21. The tide: leaving and hiring — Chicago's Mexican numbers, 1929–36

  • Where: Chicago; Cook County relief agencies; railroad yards.
  • What happened: On the eve of the Depression Chicago had the Midwest's largest Mexican community, 25,000+ (Chicago sources via search summary; Wikipedia gives ~20,000 in the 1920s falling to ~14,000 by 1940; one summary says a ~75 percent drop — the sources conflict). El Nacional (20 Feb 1932) reported more Mexicans leaving the US than entering in the 22 months to Oct 1931. But El Nacional also reported on 7 May 1932 that a railroad would hire 1,500 day laborers for track repair and on 17 Feb 1934 that the Chicago & North Western was about to hire "hundreds of Mexican workers." Ernest Burgess began a 1933 household survey ("Mexicans on relief"): 939 households and 4,272 people were censused and 42 families interviewed (per Arias & Durand). By Jan 1936 La Defensa said Cook County social agencies had paid the fares of "many Mexican families" to the border (R15). Also earlier: México (23 Jan 1929) wrote a column on the border patrol's deportation practices, and on 17 Aug 1929 reported a federal raid in the Calumet district that netted prisoners facing deportation.
  • The odd detail: the railroads that had recruited the colony in the boom were hiring track gangs again as early as spring 1932, while the same paper carried the consul's departure fares.
  • People: Ernest W. Burgess — sociologist behind the 1933 survey.
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/?group=spanish&page=20, page=22, page=30 (listings) · https://www.princeton.edu/~jdurand/PDF_varios/Mexicanos_en_Chicago_2008/Cap%C3%ADtulo_03.pdf · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexicans_in_Chicago
  • Confidence: Medium (figures conflict; the survey's results were not found).
  • Divergence check: holds domestic; BENDS in destination and in the 1924 Johnson–Reed quota law, whose existence in the setting is UNVERIFIED for this file.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Burgess's 939 households are a real dataset of who was on relief: a census-taker's list that includes families who later vanished.

23. El Tringuete and the libel arrest — Maxwell Street station, 29 March / 1 April 1930

  • Where: the El Modelo printing plant, Chicago; Maxwell Street police station; a courtroom.
  • What happened: Elfego M. Romero, editor of the satirical weekly El Tringuete, was arrested at the El Modelo plant on a warrant sworn by Francisco F. Bentacourt, editor of La Raza. The trigger was a typographical error in La Raza that had put Ignacio M. Valle's name in a legal notice; El Tringuete had mocked the correction. The bond began at $1,500, fell to $200 and then $100, and the case was continued to 15 April after Bentacourt failed to appear; attorney Mary Belle Spencer got Romero out. The arrest suppressed El Tringuete's Sunday edition and drew community support for the "patriotic" journalist. Note: the FLPS text dates the arrest "Saturday, April 1, 1930"; 1 April 1930 was a Tuesday, so the arrest was probably Saturday 29 March.
  • The odd detail: a Chicago-Mexican newspaper war over a misprinted legal notice, litigated by the same Valle whose Halsted store was attacked in 1931.
  • People: Elfego M. Romero — editor, El Tringuete; Francisco F. Bentacourt — editor, La Raza; Mary Belle Spencer — attorney; Ignacio M. Valle — the man named in the notice.
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5423405_1_0548 · https://flps.newberry.org/?group=spanish&page=11 (listing)
  • Confidence: High (with the weekday discrepancy).
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a satirical sheet and a libel warrant are how the colony argues; an investigator can find something about a merchant that the merchant's friends would rather not print.

24. Ortiz Rubio in Chicago, and the colony's Mexican factions — March 1929 – January 1930

  • Where: Michigan Central station; Union League Club; Ashland Auditorium; Hull House; Gary, Indiana.

  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5423405_1_0812
  • Confidence: High (the paper's report). ⟨fixed: V_M18: the FLPS text names "agents of the federal secret service" and dates the arrest "yesterday" = 28 April; 29 April is the report date⟩
  • Divergence check: holds — the Secret Service's founding mandate is counterfeiting (R33).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Secret Service's old-guard case: a bleached dollar bill reported in a Mexican colony's newspaper, and the workshop it came from doing other printing for other customers.

42. Julio Antonio Mella and the Kenosha march — 10 January – 6 February 1929

  • Where: Kenosha, Wisconsin (march); Mexico City (killing); Chicago and Milwaukee (organisers).
  • What happened: México (6 Feb 1929) reported a demonstration in Kenosha, Wisconsin by Spanish-speaking workers from Chicago, Milwaukee, Racine and nearby, over the assassination of the labour organiser Julio Antonio Mella in Mexico City (killed 10 Jan 1929 — general knowledge, not read this session). Marchers arrived on foot, in cars and trucks, carrying signs attacking U.S. imperialism, paraded through the streets while residents cheered, and blamed "the governor of Cuba and his Wall Street advisers" for the murder; the paper noted the march was "more effective and luckier than the last one," not stopped by local police. Discrepancy: the listing's headline calls the victim "Julio Acosta," while the article text I fetched says Mella — I could not reconcile the two.
  • The odd detail: a Cuban communist's murder in Mexico City drew a march through a Wisconsin lake town.
  • People: Julio Antonio Mella — Cuban student and communist leader, killed in Mexico City.
  • Sources: https://flps.newberry.org/id/5423405_2_0569 · https://flps.newberry.org/?group=spanish&page=5 (listing)
  • Confidence: Medium (the victim's name is inconsistent in the FLPS record).
  • Divergence check: BENDS hard. In the setting Cuba is occupied and the Marines "never left," so "the governor of Cuba" is literally an American governor (or a Marine general); and "no Soviet Union, no Red Scare" changes who Mella's comrades are. (Cross-ref M03.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Havana-based conspiracy's map (the Golden Circle) meets a Chicago march; the Chicago–Milwaukee–Racine network that organised it is a ready-made roster of Cuban and Mexican exiles the Secret Service would want.

43. Memorial Day 1937 — Max Guzmán and Lupe Gallardo Marshall

  • Where: South Chicago: "Sam's Place" (the union hall) and the Republic Steel plant; the South Chicago police station; Hull-House.
  • What happened: In the 30 May 1937 Memorial Day Massacre, ten protesters were killed, 40 shot and more than 90 wounded by police; the Chicago History Museum records two Mexicans: Max Guzmán, 26, a steelworker struck on the head, detained at the South Chicago station for up to three days without medical care, fingerprinted, questioned about communist ties and threatened with deportation; and Guadalupe "Lupe" Gallardo Marshall, 31, an immigrant and Hull-House volunteer social worker, who was among about 200 women in the march and was beaten while helping unconscious protesters. (R37 has Earl Handley; Rosales & Simon report Mexicans were up to 75 percent of the Inland/Youngstown picket in East Chicago and 6 of 24 East Chicagoans injured were Mexican.)
  • The odd detail: a Hull-House social worker was beaten at the plant gate, in the district her clients lived in.
  • People: Max Guzmán — steelworker; Guadalupe "Lupe" Gallardo Marshall — social worker; the 264 police officers stationed that afternoon.
  • Sources: https://www.chicagohistory.org/memorial-day-massacre-of-1937/ · https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/imh/article/download/10337/14385
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (the strike's rhetoric BENDS with no Red Scare: "communist ties" becomes "German-funded agitators"). The deportation threat to Guzmán has the setting's status question.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Guzmán was fingerprinted, questioned and threatened for three days — and released; whoever kept the sheet on him has a file on a Mexican steelworker that later matters.

G. The scholars

44. Redfield's fieldwork — 1 October 1924 to April 1925

  • Where: the Hull House colonia, Brighton Park, the South Side; the University of Chicago (Department of Sociology and Anthropology; Local Community Research Project).
  • What happened: Redfield signed a contract with the Local Community Research Project (Rockefeller-funded) to study "Los mexicanos en Chicago," starting 1 October 1924 at $500 (a half-time fellowship), supervised by Ernest W. Burgess and with monthly reports to Fay-Cooper Cole; his father-in-law, Robert E. Park, was one of the department's leading sociologists. He began fieldwork on 5 October 1924 with a visit to Hull House; on 31 October 1924, after his would-be interpreter failed to show twice, he began interviewing in Spanish himself. He made twenty visits to Mexican neighbourhoods, drew maps, read social-agency case files and collected the Mexican papers; his contacts were Manuel Bueno and Samuel Cohen (Brighton Park). On 31 January 1925 he attended a dance in the colony with his wife. The study ended in April 1925; his diary was never written up. He went on to Tepoztlán (Nov 1926), and the diary was published in 2008 (Arias & Durand, Mexicanos en Chicago). His original interest in Chicago's Mexican migrants was cut from the published Tepoztlán book.
  • The odd detail: a 1924 sociologist's Mexican study was never finished, but the field notebook survives — the best day-by-day record of the colony's first decade.
  • People: Robert Redfield — the student; Ernest W. Burgess — supervisor; Fay-Cooper Cole — reviewer; Robert E. Park — father-in-law; Manuel Bueno, Samuel Cohen — his contacts.
  • Sources: https://www.princeton.edu/~jdurand/PDF_varios/Mexicanos_en_Chicago_2008/Cap%C3%ADtulo_03.pdf · https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S1665-89062009000200009
  • Confidence: High (editors' account of the diary and its context).
  • Divergence check: holds; Redfield's later Tepoztlán (Morelos, unceded Mexico) work carries over.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Chicago graduate student's notebook, a father-in-law who founded the school, a funding body: the Rockefeller connection is the setting's own way into a Hull House case.

45. What Redfield found — no naturalisations, "no discrimination," and a colony that meant to go home

  • Where: the Cook County court; the colony's homes and workplaces.
  • What happened: From the diary (per Arias & Durand): Mexicans did not naturalise, even when caseworkers told them it would improve their chances; naturalisation felt like "a betrayal of the homeland," and the one naturalised man the colony spoke of was distrusted by it; Redfield checked the court: of 732 naturalisation files between 1 December 1924 and 26 January 1926, none was Mexican. [UNVERIFIED — V_M18: the 732 figure and the "none" are exactly as Arias & Durand print them (chapter 3, p. 76), but their end date is nine months after Redfield's fieldwork ended in April 1925 — possibly a misprint for 1925 — and they say only "la Corte", not Cook County.] Informants said they were not discriminated against (a striking claim from men in boxcars; the editors read it as transience, since discrimination is felt by those who stay). He recorded only two mixed marriages (a Mexican woman with a Native American man; a Black–Mexican couple). Some informants said they carried guns "to defend themselves from Black neighbours" — an informant claim, recorded as such. One remarked that "the Poles pretend to be Americans; they're ashamed to be Polish." Another said Americans "see only the common Mexicans, the rough ones, the Indians, and do not appreciate the real Mexico." Most Mexican migrants were literate in Spanish, and informants described the colony as peaceable — few arrests for its size, only one raid on a party mentioned to him.
  • The odd detail: 732 court files, zero Mexicans — the number Redfield chased down in person.
  • People: the unnamed informants; the one naturalised man.
  • Sources: https://www.princeton.edu/~jdurand/PDF_varios/Mexicanos_en_Chicago_2008/Cap%C3%ADtulo_03.pdf
  • Confidence: High for the editors' summary.
  • Divergence check: BENDS interestingly. The refusal to naturalise is the real colonia's political stance; in the setting people of the ceded north are already American subjects by treaty (nobody asked them) — so for them "not naturalising" is not available and "betrayal of the homeland" is a charge others will levy at them.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): 732 files and no Mexican among them: any Mexican in 1930s Chicago who is on the naturalisation rolls is someone the colony distrusts and the Bureau values.

46. Taylor (1932), the Joneses, Burgess and Baur — the studies of Mexican Chicago

  • Where: the University of Chicago; Berkeley; the Chicago Church Federation; the WPA.
  • What happened: Paul S. Taylor, a Berkeley social scientist, published Mexican Labor in the United States: Chicago and the Calumet Region in 1932 (reviewed in the American Journal of Sociology, vol. 39, no. 1, 1933; vol. II = Migration Statistics). He hired Robert J. Jones as research assistant and traded field notes with him; he followed his Chicago interviewees to Arandas, Jalisco, for his 1933 village study. Anita Edgar Jones wrote a 1928 University of Chicago study of Mexicans in Chicago (the boxcar maps; the view that every Mexican planned to return). Robert C. Jones (the FLPS names him "C."; Arias & Durand give "J." — one man or two is not settled here) ran the 1928–31 Church Federation survey (entry 06) and lectured to University of Chicago students in May 1931. Jackson Baur's WPA-supported master's thesis on "Juvenile Delinquency in the Mexican Colony of South Chicago" surveyed about 2,000 cases. Burgess's 1933 "Mexicans on relief" survey is entry 21. I did not read Taylor's own chapters.
  • The odd detail: three Joneses — Anita Edgar, Robert C. and Robert J. — turn up across these sources, and the editors themselves say the name is uncertain.
  • People: Paul S. Taylor; Robert C./J. Jones; Anita Edgar Jones; Jackson Baur; Ernest W. Burgess.
  • Sources: https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/216353 (search summary only) · https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/imh/article/download/12624/18918 · https://www.princeton.edu/~jdurand/PDF_varios/Mexicanos_en_Chicago_2008/Cap%C3%ADtulo_03.pdf
  • Confidence: High on titles and dates; Taylor's contents unread.
  • Divergence check: holds; the studies are from the 1920s–30s American academy.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Chicago School's maps and case files are the setting's own institutional memory of the colonias; an investigator who needs "the file on that family" reaches for Burgess's 939 households first.
