M07 — Catholic Mexico's persecution reaching the United States and Chicago, 1914–1940

  • Lane: M07 — the thread that ties the Mexico book to the Chicago book: Mexican bishops and priests in exile in the US (San Antonio, Los Angeles, Chicago); Chicago's own link (the Catholic Church Extension Society, Cardinal Mundelein); the exile seminaries (Castroville 1915, Montezuma 1937); the Knights of Columbus and the American Catholic press campaign against Calles; Ambassador Morrow and Fr. John Burke's 1929 arreglos; Chicago's Mexican colonias and their parishes; relics and devotions carried north (the Cristero martyrs, Fr. Miguel Pro); the Liga and Cristero fund-raising and gun-running on US soil; the anticlerical state's own agents and exiles in the US; and the Mexican shrine, miracle and exorcism traditions an IHS priest would know.
  • Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent) · Brief: BRIEF.md in this folder + the Chicago sweep's BRIEF.md (entry format, sourcing rules, hard rules).
  • Setting sources read first: ALTERNATE_HISTORY.md §0, §3, §7.4, §8, §29 (Mexico/Caribbean theatres); M01 Part C (the Cristero War in Mexico — the Calles Law, Pro's execution, Toral, the arreglos, Garrido Canabal: not repeated here; this file takes the American side of each); M02, M06 (headings); the Chicago sweep's R05 (Our Lady of Guadalupe South Chicago and the St. Jude shrine are R05's — cross-referred, not repeated) and R35.

Treaty-line tags (as in M01/M02): CEDED NORTH (Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila, Nuevo León — American after the 1917 treaty) · TAMPICO ENCLAVE · VERACRUZ (Mexican flag, American customs receivership) · UNCEDED MEXICO (Mexico City, Jalisco, Michoacán, Guanajuato, Zacatecas…) · US SIDE (Texas, New Mexico, California, Illinois — American before 1848). The key bend for this lane: in the setting the ceded north is American — its bishops are American bishops, its priests are protected by the occupier, and a Mexican exile fleeing Calles in 1926 may only have to reach Chihuahua or Monterrey, not San Antonio. Where the record has an exile crossing "into the United States," the setting may have him crossing only the treaty line.

Overview. 32 entries. The thread the owner wanted is real and runs straight through Chicago. Chicago was the Mexican Church's American desk from 1914: Mgr. Francis C. Kelley's Catholic Church Extension Society spoke for the exiled bishops to Wilson (1915), paid for a Mexican seminary at Castroville (sponsored with Chicago's Archbishop Quigley), and represented them at Paris; the Archbishop of Morelia, Leopoldo Ruiz y Flores — later the Pope's delegate who co-signed the 1929 peace — lived in Chicago in his first exile (one scholarly source). In the Cristero years the exiled Bishop of Tabasco, Pascual Díaz, laid the cornerstone of Our Lady of Guadalupe in South Chicago (14 April 1928), a bishop led a 500-strong anti-Calles march there that September, and Cardinal Mundelein received the militant exile bishop Manríquez y Zárate. The US side of the war is richer than the Mexico side suggests: a Catholic rising planned in a San Antonio hotel room (the Tenorio plot, 1927), the Mexican state's consular spies, the Knights of Columbus' $1 million fund and five million leaflets, Fr. John Burke's four-hour secret meeting with Calles in the Veracruz island fortress (4 April 1928), the bishops riding home in Ambassador Morrow's private car, the delegate deported through Tampico (1932), Daniels's 1934 speech, the Borah resolution (1935) and Montezuma Seminary (1937) — Mexico's priesthood trained in exile in a Chicago-designed railway castle. The divergence bites hard in this lane: Coahuila, Chihuahua and Sonora are ceded in the setting, so the border that exiles crossed (Nogales, El Paso–Juárez, Del Rio–Acuña) is not the border any more; Tampico and Veracruz's harbour are American; and the ceded north's bishops are American bishops. Several real events (the Tenorio raid, the Tampico deportation, the Ulúa meeting) become events inside the occupier's perimeter. The shrines (San Juan de los Lagos, Chalma, Plateros, Chimayó) give the IHS a gazetteer of old images with "three layers" already built in.

Part A — Chicago's Mexico desk: the Extension Society and the first exiles (1914–1918)

Mgr. Francis Clement Kelley and the Catholic Church Extension Society — Chicago's Mexico desk, 1914–1924

  • Where: the Extension Society's headquarters, Chicago (the Society moved there from Lapeer, Michigan, in January 1907 ⟨fixed: V_M07⟩); Washington; Paris (1919).
  • What happened: Kelley, a Canadian-born priest, founded the Catholic Church Extension Society in 1905 with Chicago's Archbishop James Quigley; it moved to Chicago in January 1907 ⟨fixed: V_M07⟩ and ran Extension magazine (over 3 million subscribers in his time) and the famous railway chapel cars. When the Constitutionalist revolution drove all of Mexico's bishops into exile in 1914, Kelley became their representative in the United States. On 23 February 1915 he wrote to President Wilson that the exiled bishops wanted no intervention, only guarantees of liberty of conscience, and that the Constitutionalist movement (he named Gen. Antonio I. Villarreal) was "antireligious and persecuting" — "economic and agrarian questions … were only side issues; the great issue of Mexican politics was that of liberty of conscience." He thought Villa and Zapata might be reachable; Carranza not. The same year he published, in Chicago, The Book of Red and Yellow: Being a Story of Blood and a Yellow Streak (1915) — affidavits of outrages against priests and nuns by Constitutionalist troops, sent also to the Secretary of State. At the 1919 Paris Peace Conference he represented the Mexican bishops (and quietly worked on the Roman Question with Orlando). He became Bishop of Oklahoma in 1924 and wrote Blood-Drenched Altars (1935, see Part D).
  • The odd detail: the man who spoke for Mexico's exiled episcopate in Washington and at Versailles did it from a Chicago office that also ran chapels on railway wheels — the Church's own rolling network across the American West.
  • People: Francis Clement Kelley — Extension founder, later Bishop of Oklahoma (d. 1 Feb 1948); James E. Quigley — Archbishop of Chicago (d. 1915); Antonio I. Villarreal — Constitutionalist general Kelley blamed.
  • Sources: https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1915/d1511 · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Kelley · https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/kelley-francis-clement
  • Confidence: High (the 1915 letter is in FRUS; the book's title/date from catalogue listings). The Society's exact 1915 street address not confirmed this session.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. BENDS after 1916: Paris 1919 is the victors' peace in reverse (Germany's peace) — Kelley at a Berlin-dictated conference representing Mexican bishops whose north America now holds is a stranger errand. His successor in the setting's 1930s is a Chicago office speaking for two Mexican churches: the bishops of the ceded north (now American bishops) and the persecuted bishops of unceded Mexico.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Extension Society's chapel cars as an IHS logistics arm hidden in plain sight — a consecrated railway car can carry what cannot go by ordinary freight. Its Chicago files on 1914–15 "outrages" are the IHS's first dossier on what walked with the Constitutionalist columns.

The Seminary of St. Philip at Castroville, Texas — January 1915 – May 1918; revived September 1929 – July 1930

  • Where: Castroville, Texas (US SIDE), just west of San Antonio, in the former novitiate buildings of the Sisters of Divine Providence.
  • What happened: Opened in January 1915 for Mexican seminarians whose studies the Carranza persecution had stopped, dedicated to St. Philip Neri and financed largely by Kelley's Chicago-based Extension Society. Bishop John W. Shaw and Archbishop Arthur J. Drossaerts of San Antonio, together with Archbishop Quigley of Chicago, sponsored the 108 Mexican students who entered in its first three years; by May 1918 59 had been ordained, and it closed that month. When Calles's persecution came, it was revived in September 1929 (after the arreglos) with 24 students, run by the Missionaries of the Holy Spirit, supported by the Knights of Columbus and the dioceses of San Luis Potosí, Mérida and Saltillo at $25 a month per seminarian; the Archdiocese of San Antonio bought the buildings. It closed again in July 1930.
  • The odd detail: a Mexican seminary on American soil, paid for from Chicago, had already ordained 59 priests before most Americans knew there was a religious war south of the border.
  • People: Francis C. Kelley — financier; Arthur J. Drossaerts — San Antonio's archbishop, host of the exiles; James E. Quigley — Chicago's sponsor.
  • Sources: https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/seminary-of-st-philip-for-mexican-students
  • Confidence: High (Handbook of Texas).
  • Divergence check: 1915–16 happened. BENDS after 1917: with Saltillo (Coahuila) in the CEDED NORTH, the 1929 revival's Saltillo money is American-diocese money — and the ceded north's own seminaries could simply take the refugees. In the setting a refugee seminary may sit in Monterrey or Saltillo under the American flag, not in Texas.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a register of 108 names, 59 ordained — a ready-made list of Mexican priests trained in American exile who went home to the Cristero country. An IHS recruiter's roll call, and the Golden Circle's target list if it ever leaked.

Part B — The exiled bishops in the United States (1926–1944)

The Archbishop of Mexico "buried on charity" — San Antonio, 22 April 1928 (and Bishop Valdespino, May 1928)

  • Where: San Antonio, Texas (US SIDE) — the exiled Mexican hierarchy's principal refuge.
  • What happened: Archbishop José Mora y del Río of Mexico City, who had protested the 1917 Constitution and the Calles Law and signed the bishops' 1926 statement, went into exile on 21 April 1927 with other prelates and religious. He died in San Antonio on 22 April 1928, a year and a day later. TIME (28 May 1928) reported Archbishop Drossaerts of San Antonio saying that the archbishop of Mexico City had been "buried on charity"; the week before, Bishop Ignacio Valdespino of Aguascalientes also died in San Antonio, and Drossaerts preached the funeral sermon at his requiem Mass in San Fernando cathedral ⟨fixed: V_M07⟩. The same article repeated exile claims of priests killed and people forcibly "inoculated" (UNVERIFIED claims, reported as the source's).
  • The odd detail: the primate of Mexico died a pauper refugee and his funeral was paid for by strangers — in the same city where the Alamo sits.
  • People: José Mora y del Río — Archbishop of Mexico (1854–1928); Ignacio Valdespino y Díaz — Bishop of Aguascalientes; Arthur Jerome Drossaerts — Archbishop of San Antonio.
  • Sources: https://time.com/archive/6776880/religion-death-in-mexico/ · https://visualizingcristerowar.com/mexican-exiles-and-cristero-activists-in-the-united-states/ · (search summary of) https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/mora-y-del-rio-jose-1854-1928
  • Confidence: High on dates and the "buried on charity" phrase; atrocity claims are the exiles' own.
  • Divergence check: BENDS: Mexico City is UNCEDED MEXICO; the exile route is the same, but in the setting San Antonio is no longer the nearest safe ground — Monterrey (American since 1917) is. That San Antonio stays the refuge says the exiles trust the old American dioceses more than the occupier's new ones.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a dead archbishop's effects sold to pay debts — a crozier, a breviary, a reliquary — scattered through San Antonio pawnshops in 1928. One of them was the thing the IHS had asked him to carry out of Mexico City.

Bishop Pascual Díaz lays the cornerstone of Our Lady of Guadalupe, South Chicago — 14 April 1928

  • Where: E. 91st Street and Burley/Brandon Avenue, South Chicago (today 3200 E. 91st St.), by the Illinois Steel South Works.
  • What happened: The Mexican steelworkers' mission (founded after Mundelein's decision of July 1923; first Claretian October 1924; first church a converted army barracks at 9024 S. Mackinaw Ave.; Fr. James Tort, C.M.F., pastor from 1925) bought land at 91st and Brandon in 1926. On 14 April 1928 the cornerstone of its new church was laid by Rt. Rev. Pascual Díaz y Barreto, the exiled Bishop of Tabasco, who was living in New York after his expulsion in 1927 [UNVERIFIED — V_M07: the reprinted caption, "South Chicago church and Mexican center dedicated by bishop of Tabasco," does not say where he lived; sources say only that much of his exile was spent in the US]. Díaz — a Jesuit of Huichol descent ⟨fixed: V_M07 — "full" unsupported; Wikipedia says "of Huichol indigenous descent"⟩, born in Zapopan — would, fourteen months later, take part in the arreglos with Portes Gil and become Archbishop of Mexico (appointed 25 June, installed 28 June 1929 ⟨fixed: V_M07 — was "22 June", his birthday; catholic-hierarchy.org⟩). The St. Jude shrine begun here in 1929 is in R05.
  • The odd detail: the stone of Chicago's first Mexican church was laid by the man who would end the Cristero War — an Indigenous Jesuit bishop exiled from the Tabasco of Garrido Canabal's Red Shirts.
  • People: Pascual Díaz y Barreto, SJ — exiled bishop, later archbishop (d. 19 May 1936); James Tort, C.M.F. — pastor; George Mundelein — the cardinal who founded the mission; Alice Bourke — Comet associate editor who pressed for the church.
  • Sources: https://chicago.suntimes.com/2021/10/1/22698907/our-lady-of-guadalupe-chicago-history · https://shrineofstjude.org/the-shrine/history-of-our-lady-of-guadalupe-church/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascual_D%C3%ADaz_y_Barreto
  • Confidence: High (dated photo caption); Díaz's New York residence per the caption.
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic calendar (Chicago). BENDS Díaz's world: Tabasco is UNCEDED MEXICO; he is an exile of the rump state. The parishioners (many from Jalisco, Michoacán, Guanajuato — unceded) are in the setting refugees of a Mexico that lost its north, working in American mills that also buy ceded-north ore.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): what did a Jesuit bishop seal into a Chicago cornerstone in 1928? Cornerstones take relics and documents. An IHS deposit in a steel-town church, placed by the man who would be the Church's peacemaker — and opened, one day, by someone who should not have.

The church dedicated and the Cristero march — South Chicago, September 1928

  • Where: Our Lady of Guadalupe, South Chicago (as above).
  • What happened: The three-storey brick church-and-centre was dedicated by Cardinal Mundelein on 30 September 1928, serving about 8,000 Mexicans (the Southeast Chicago Historical Society holds a photograph of the dedication crowd, dated 30 Sept 1928; the Sun-Times text gives "1930," apparently an error). The historian Julia G. Young's work on the "Cristero diaspora" (as quoted in search summaries and the Visualizing the Cristero War project) records that some 500 parishioners listened to a visiting bishop rail against Mexico's anticlerical leaders and then marched, singing and waving religious placards, to the newly built church — dated in that project to 23 September 1928 [UNVERIFIED — V_M07: the passage is Young's, Catholic Historical Review 98 (2012) 271–300; neither project page cited here contains it or that date, and the bishop is unnamed]. At that date the Cristero war was at its height in Jalisco.
  • The odd detail: a Cristero-style religious procession — placards, hymns, a bishop denouncing Calles — in the shadow of the South Works blast furnaces, a week before the cardinal blessed the building.
  • People: George Mundelein — dedicator; the visiting bishop (name not confirmed this session — Díaz or Manríquez are candidates; see Leads); Justino Cordero — a Zacatecan steelworker (arrived 1923) whose family kept the dedication photograph.
  • Sources: https://www.sechicagohistory.org/archive/browse/our-lady-of-guadalupe-dedication-ceremony-2/ · https://visualizingcristerowar.com/mexican-migration-to-the-united-states-evolved/ · https://chicago.suntimes.com/2021/10/1/22698907/our-lady-of-guadalupe-chicago-history
  • Image: dedication-crowd photograph, Southeast Chicago Historical Society (Cordero collection), CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 — https://www.sechicagohistory.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/1981-102-2c-822x1200.jpg
  • Confidence: High on the dedication (dated photograph); Medium on the 500-marcher procession and its date (secondary summary; bishop unnamed).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic. BENDS the politics: a public anti-Calles march in Chicago is, in the setting, a march against the government of a defeated neighbour that America already partly occupies — the Mexican consul, not the police, is the one who minds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the IHS's first Chicago congregation of witnesses — people who saw the war in Jalisco. A South Chicago parish register of refugee families is where an investigator finds the survivor of a burned chapel (see M01 Part C) now pouring steel.

Bishop Manríquez y Zárate — "the standard-bearer" in exile, 24 April 1927 – 8 March 1944

  • Where: exile in the United States (cities not pinned down this session; received by Cardinal Mundelein in Chicago per the Visualizing the Cristero War project and Julia Young's summary).
  • What happened: José de Jesús Manríquez y Zárate (b. León, Guanajuato, 1884), first Bishop of Huejutla (Hidalgo), defied Calles in a pastoral of 10 March 1926, was imprisoned for a year and exiled on 24 April 1927. In the United States he became the openly militant voice of Catholic armed defence: the project names him among those who "smuggled weapons into Mexico," and he condemned the 21 June 1929 arreglos as a shameful modus vivendi. José Vasconcelos called him "the standard-bearer" of the persecuted faith and the de facto leader of the militant Church. He stayed out seventeen years, returning on 8 March 1944; he died in 1951.
  • The odd detail: a bishop who spent longer in American exile than the whole Cristero War lasted — and argued, from American pulpits, for the right to take up arms.
  • People: José de Jesús Manríquez y Zárate — Bishop of Huejutla; George Mundelein — his Chicago host; José Vasconcelos — who named him standard-bearer.
  • Sources: https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/manriquez-y-zarate-jose-de-jesus · https://visualizingcristerowar.com/mexican-exiles-and-cristero-activists-in-the-united-states/
  • Confidence: High on dates; Medium on the Mundelein reception (secondary); weapons-smuggling claim is the project's, unverified in a primary source.
  • Divergence check: BENDS: Huejutla is UNCEDED MEXICO. A militant bishop raising guns on US soil for a war in a neighbour America already occupies in part is a direct problem for the occupation's quiet — the War Department, not only the State Department, wants him watched.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "no one is clean" — the IHS's natural ally in exile is also the one most likely to buy rifles with IHS money. A Secret Service file on the bishop (the Service allied with the IHS since 1866) that the Service's own agents are told not to read.

Mother Luisita crosses at Nogales in disguise — 20–24 June 1927

  • Where: Guadalajara / Atotonilco (Jalisco, UNCEDED MEXICO) → by train → Nogales, Arizona (23 June 1927) → Los Angeles (24 June); Long Beach, 1851 Locust Street (from 3 August 1927).
  • What happened: María Luisa Josefa of the Most Blessed Sacrament ("Mother Luisita," 1866–1937), foundress of the Carmelite Sisters of the Sacred Heart in Mexico, left in disguise on 20 June 1927 with Sisters Teresa Navarro and Margarita María Hernández after public worship had been forbidden; they crossed at Nogales on 23 June, reciting a Te Deum, and were received by Bishop John J. Cantwell in Los Angeles on 24 June. They lodged in Long Beach with a Mrs. Nicolasa (as spelled on the site: "Nicolastia") Flores; more sisters followed. The exile house became the Carmelite Sisters of the Most Sacred Heart of Los Angeles, with a TB sanatorium (Santa Teresita, opened 2 August 1930). She returned to Mexico in May 1930 in poverty and died in Guadalajara on 11 February 1937, receiving Benediction moments before death. Declared Venerable.
  • The odd detail: the nuns fled Mexico in lay clothes and sang the Te Deum at the border crossing.
  • People: Mother María Luisa Josefa (Luisita) — foundress; John J. Cantwell — Bishop (later Archbishop) of Los Angeles.
  • Sources: https://carmelitesistersocd.com/timeline/ · (search summary of) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mar%C3%ADa_Luisa_Josefa
  • Confidence: High (the order's own dated timeline).
  • Divergence check: BENDS: in the setting Nogales, Sonora is CEDED NORTH — the nuns would be safe the moment their train crossed the treaty line, far south of the old border. The setting's Te Deum is sung at the new line, somewhere in Sinaloa or southern Sonora.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): where does the treaty line run on the Southern Pacific of Mexico? A border station where refugees sing is a scene; a convent that moved north carrying its enclosure's relics is a lead.

The apostolic delegate flown out through Tampico — 4 October 1932 ⟨fixed: V_M07 — was "17 October", which is the date of the TIME issue; the Chamber voted on 3 Oct and he was expelled next day⟩

  • Where: Mexico City → by chartered plane toward Matamoros (turned back by weather) → overnight at Tampico → special train to the US border.
  • What happened: After Pius XI's encyclical Acerba animi (29 September 1932) accused Mexico of breaking the 1929 arreglos, the government declared Archbishop Leopoldo Ruiz y Flores of Morelia — apostolic delegate and co-signer of the arreglos — a "pernicious foreigner" who had lost his citizenship by serving a foreign power, and gave him until midday to leave. TIME reports a plane piloted by Floyd Clevenger flying so low in bad weather that "the prelate's nervousness increased to terror"; they turned back, he was held overnight in Tampico, then put on a special train (engine and one car) with 30 soldiers, reaching the border near midnight, where priests and US officials received him. It was his third exile. He returned only in 1938, after resigning the delegation.
  • The odd detail: the Pope's man in Mexico was deported as a foreigner from his own country — via an American pilot and a night in the oil port.
  • People: Leopoldo Ruiz y Flores — Archbishop of Morelia, apostolic delegate; Floyd Clevenger — pilot; Pius XI — author of Acerba animi.
  • Sources: https://time.com/archive/6750156/religion-third-exile/ · (search summary of) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopoldo_Ruiz_y_Fl%C3%B3res
  • Confidence: High (TIME's contemporary report); exact border crossing town not stated.
  • Divergence check: BENDS sharply: in the setting Tampico is the American TAMPICO ENCLAVE. A Mexican government deporting the Pope's delegate into an American-held port overnight is either impossible or a pointed insult to the occupier — a GM's choice. The Mexico-to-Matamoros flight path crosses toward ceded territory as well.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): one night in Tampico under American guard. The IHS has twelve hours with the apostolic delegate — or someone else does. What he carried, and what was missing when the train reached the line.

Montezuma Seminary — the Mexican national seminary in a Chicago-designed railroad hotel, opened 23 September 1937

  • Where: Montezuma Castle, Gallinas Canyon near Las Vegas, New Mexico (US SIDE).
  • What happened: The Santa Fe Railway's Montezuma Hotel (1886, designed by the Chicago firm Burnham & Root; the third hotel on the site — its two predecessors of 1881 and 1885 both burned) had hosted Theodore Roosevelt, Grant and Sherman and closed as a hotel in 1903; it was a Baptist college 1922–31. In 1937 the Southern Baptists sold it to the Catholic Church, and a committee of American bishops headed by Bishop John Mark Gannon of Erie founded there the Pontificio Seminario Central Mexicano de Nuestra Señora de Guadalupe, run by the Jesuits, for Mexicans who could not be trained at home. It opened on 23 September 1937 (the same year as Pius XI's letter to the Mexican bishops, Firmissimam constantiamque, 28 March 1937 ⟨fixed: V_M07 — date confirmed from the Vatican text⟩). The American bishops poured millions of dollars into it over decades while the Mexican bishops kept control of its teaching and administration; ambassador Josephus Daniels framed it as "religious good neighborliness." It closed on 8 June 1972 after about 2,977 students — including the future cardinal Carlos Aguiar Retes and, notoriously, Marcial Maciel, founder of the Legion of Christ.
  • The odd detail: a whole nation's priesthood was trained for 35 years in exile, in a haunted-looking Queen Anne railway castle designed in Chicago — by the same firm as the Rookery.
  • People: John Mark Gannon — Bishop of Erie, committee chairman; Josephus Daniels — US ambassador (1933–41); Marcial Maciel — its most infamous alumnus.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montezuma_Castle_(hotel) · https://berkleycenter.georgetown.edu/responses/montezuma-s-gold-u-s-mexican-diplomacy-and-the-rebuilding-of-mexican-catholicism-1937-1960 · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mark_Gannon
  • Confidence: High on hotel history, dates and totals (Wikipedia/marker summaries); purchase price not found.
  • Divergence check: holds (New Mexico is US SIDE). BENDS the need: in the setting a Mexican seminary could sit in the ceded north; that the bishops still choose New Mexico says the ceded north's church is the occupier's, and unceded Mexico's bishops will not send boys there.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a castle of exiled seminarians in a canyon of hot springs, 1937 — the IHS's Mexican school, hidden inside a seminary, built in a hotel whose two predecessors burned. And the "no one is clean" note the record itself supplies: one of its boys became Maciel.

Part C — The Cristero diaspora: guns, money, spies and marches on American soil (1926–1939)

The Robert E. Lee Hotel plot and Simón Tenorio's desert uprising — San Antonio / Del Rio / Coahuila, summer 1927 – 13 August 1927

  • Where: the Robert E. Lee Hotel, San Antonio (a new ten-storey "skyscraper"); Del Rio, Texas (US SIDE); Villa Acuña and the Sierra del Burro, Coahuila; Torreón.
  • What happened: Julia G. Young's Mexican Exodus (Oxford, 2015) opens with Simón Tenorio, 36, of Del Rio, drawn in by his neighbour, the exiled priest Fr. Pascual Robles (who ran a paper, La Razón). In midsummer 1927 the two drove to San Antonio and met, in the Robert E. Lee Hotel, the Liga's chief Luis Bustos, its purchasing agent Juan Lainé, and Bishop Manríquez y Zárate. Tenorio's orders: recruit a band, cross into Coahuila, meet a larger expedition in the foothills of the Sierra del Burro, then join armed bands from Laredo and El Paso for a Catholic rising. Seven men crossed on 6 August 1927 carrying a manifesto "to overthrow the Bolshevik tyranny in its most recent incarnation, Callismo"; Genaro Valadez's suitcase held scapulars, rosaries, mass books, portraits of Guadalupe, and letters — one from Robles, one from a well-wisher in Detroit warning they would "sacrifice your lives … without glory." "Bishops, Knights, Border Guards, and Spies" — the Mexican state's watchers in the United States, 1926–1929

The Knights of Columbus' Mexican campaign — the $1 million fund, 5 million leaflets, and the President — August 1926 – 1929

A Hyde Park lecture hall is where an IHS priest first sees the face of the man whose schools will empty his seminaries.

Calles in San Diego — deported 9–10 April 1936, in California until 1941; the séance circle after

  • Where: San Diego, California (US SIDE); Mexico City after 1941.
  • What happened: Having made and unmade three presidents as the Jefe Máximo, Plutarco Elías Calles was deported by Cárdenas on 9 April 1936 with his labour ally Luis Morones (M01 records the famous detail of the book at his bedside). He lived with his family in San Diego — an exile in the country whose Catholics had spent a decade lobbying against him. Allowed home by Ávila Camacho in 1941, the old anticlerical began attending weekly sessions of the Mexican Circle of Metapsychic Investigations — a spiritist/psychical-research circle — and came to profess belief "in a Supreme Being." He died in Mexico City on 19 October 1945. (His 1928 visit to the healer Niño Fidencio is in M02; another anticlerical, Garrido Canabal, died in Los Angeles in 1943 — M01.)
  • The odd detail: the persecutor of the Church ended as a regular at a séance table.
  • People: Plutarco Elías Calles — ex-president; Luis N. Morones — CROM boss, deported with him.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarco_El%C3%ADas_Calles
  • Confidence: High (dates); the Metapsychic circle is after the period (1941+), reported as Wikipedia gives it.
  • Divergence check: BENDS: Calles is a Sonoran (Guaymas) — CEDED NORTH; in the setting he could be "exiled" to his own birthplace, now American. Whether the Sonoran dynasty rules Mexico at all is an M01 question for the owner. The séance thread rhymes with the setting's own Wilson (removed after "a cult's séance gone wrong," §0).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a persecutor who turned to the spirits — "three layers": the period says an old man's comfort; the folklore says the dead priests came for him; the setting can decide what answered him at the table. Five years in San Diego, watched by everyone.

José Vasconcelos — from Calles's rival to the Cristero bishop's admirer to a pro-German editor — 1929–1940

  • Where: exile in the US and elsewhere after 1929; Mexico City (Timón, 1940).
  • What happened: Mexico's great revolutionary education minister (1921–24) ran for president in 1929 against the Calles machine, lost to Pascual Ortiz Rubio in a disputed election, and left the country again. Over the following decade he turned to a militant Catholic conservatism — he called the exiled Bishop Manríquez y Zárate "the standard-bearer" of the persecuted faith — and in 1940 edited the magazine Timón, writing that Hitler, "although he disposes of absolute power, finds himself a thousand leagues from Caesarism." Wikipedia lists him among Mexican collaborators with Nazi Germany (the claim that Timón was German-funded is widely repeated but not confirmed this session).
  • The odd detail: the same man bridges the revolution, the Cristero bishops and Berlin.
  • People: José Vasconcelos (1882–1959) — philosopher, minister, candidate, editor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Vasconcelos · https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/manriquez-y-zarate-jose-de-jesus
  • Confidence: High on the 1929 campaign, the Timón quotation and the "standard-bearer" phrase; German funding UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: BENDS: no Nazi Germany in the setting (Hitler has no office). A Vasconcelos who admires Germany in 1940 admires the Kaiser's victorious empire — "Germany as the patron of other people's revolutions," here patron of a Catholic-nationalist Mexican who wants the north back.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the hinge between the Cristero diaspora and Berlin: a Catholic Mexican intellectual courting the German legation — the Golden Circle and the IHS would both want him, for opposite reasons.

Part F — Devotions and martyrs carried north

Miguel Pro the novice in California — Los Gatos, 1914–1915

  • Where: the Jesuit novitiate at Los Gatos, California (Sacred Heart Novitiate, above the Santa Clara valley).
  • What happened: When the Constitutionalists broke up the Mexican Jesuit novitiate in 1914, the novices were sent abroad; Miguel Agustín Pro studied at Los Gatos, California, before going on to Spain, Nicaragua and Belgium (ordained 31 August 1925). He returned to Mexico in 1926, ran a clandestine ministry signing his letters "Cocol," and was shot without trial on 23 November 1927 (the execution and its photographs are in M01). Beatified 25 September 1988.
  • The odd detail: the most famous Cristero martyr was a novice in California a decade before his death — the Pro cult has an American address.
  • People: Miguel Agustín Pro, SJ.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_Pro
  • Confidence: High on Los Gatos; length of his stay not pinned (UNVERIFIED).
  • Divergence check: 1914 happened. Pro's 1927 death is in UNCEDED MEXICO (Mexico City) — holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the IHS's file on Pro opens in California, not Mexico — a novice noticed at Los Gatos, trained abroad, sent home under a pet name. The photographs Calles had taken are the most-circulated images of a Catholic death of the decade — a relic that is a photograph, and an IHS problem when copies turn up annotated.

St. Toribio Romo — killed at Tequila, 25 February 1928; the migrants' saint (legend from the late 1970s)

  • Where: born Santa Ana de Guadalupe, Jalostotitlán (Los Altos de Jalisco); killed at Agua Caliente, Tequila, Jalisco (UNCEDED MEXICO); venerated on the US border.
  • What happened: Toribio Romo González (b. 16 April 1900) was shot by government troops at dawn on 25 February 1928; his sister María was present and cried, "Courage, Father Toribio … merciful Christ, receive him! Long live Christ the King!" Canonised 21 May 2000. FOLKLORE (attested from the late 1970s): migrants crossing the US–Mexico desert began to report a young priest who guided them, gave them water or money and told them to visit him at Santa Ana — later recognised as St. Toribio; he is now called the patron of migrants. The irony the record keeps: in 1920 he wrote a play warning people against going north.
  • The odd detail: a priest who wrote against emigration became, fifty years after his death, the saint who walks the border with those who go.
  • People: Toribio Romo González — priest and martyr; María Romo — his sister.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toribio_Romo_Gonz%C3%A1lez
  • Confidence: High on the death; the legend is modern folklore, labelled with its first attestation (late 1970s) — not period material.
  • Divergence check: Jalisco is UNCEDED MEXICO — holds. The legend is post-period; use as texture only.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a 1930s table, the seed is period: a martyred priest's ghost reported near the treaty line in the setting's own decade — the IHS would have to decide whether to investigate a saint.

Chicago's other Mexican parish — St. Francis of Assisi, Near West Side (Claretians from 1927), and the colonias' numbers

  • Where: St. Francis of Assisi, 813 W. Roosevelt Rd. (address from general knowledge — UNVERIFIED), south of Hull House on the Halsted corridor; Back of the Yards; South Chicago.
  • What happened: A former German parish on the Near West Side, St. Francis was taken over by the Claretians as administrators in 1927; in their first December there they held a novena to Our Lady of Guadalupe, and through the 1920s its school became ever more Mexican. With Our Lady of Guadalupe in South Chicago, it was one of the archdiocese's two Mexican parishes of the 1920s — though Mundelein opposed new national parishes in principle. Chicago had about 3,000 Mexicans c. 1920, about 20,000 by the late 1920s, falling to about 14,000 by the end of the 1930s after the repatriations (M06). The mutualistas of the colonias preached Mexican nationalism, sometimes in tension with the Spanish (Catalan) Claretian priests (inference from the sources' framing, not a documented quarrel).
  • The odd detail: Chicago's Mexican Catholics were pastored by Spanish Claretians in German churches — the Church's own "three layers."
  • People: the Claretian Missionaries (founded by St. Anthony Mary Claret, 1849); George Mundelein.
  • Sources: https://uscatholic.org/articles/202006/how-mexican-immigrants-changed-chicagos-parishes/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexicans_in_Chicago · (search summary of) https://www.commonwealmagazine.org/refounding-chicago
  • Confidence: High on the Claretians and the two parishes; population figures approximate (Wikipedia).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic. BENDS status: in the setting the Near West Side colony includes families from the ceded north — American nationals by treaty — beside refugees from unceded Mexico: two legal classes in one pew.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Hull House (R-files) and a Claretian parish on the same street — the settlement's social workers and the Church's priests keeping two sets of records on the same refugee families; the IHS reads the second.

The Catholic workers' circle of Indiana Harbor and El Amigo del Hogar — 1925 – early 1930s

  • Where: Indiana Harbor / East Chicago, Indiana (the Inland Steel colony); Our Lady of Guadalupe, East Chicago (3510 Deodar St., first Mass 1940).
  • What happened: The Mexican Catholic mutual society El Círculo de Obreros Católicos "San José" published the Spanish-language paper El Amigo del Hogar ("The Friend of the Home") from 1925 until it was discontinued in the early 1930s; some of the Círculo's leaders were repatriated to Mexico between 1930 and 1937 (M06 covers the Indiana Harbor repatriation trains). The colony's own Guadalupe church had its inaugural Mass only in 1940, when a photograph shows Enrique "Henry" García holding the Mexican flag beside the American one.
  • The odd detail: a Catholic workers' newspaper in a steel town, published through the Cristero years — the Cristero diaspora's voice in the Calumet.
  • People: Enrique García — flag-bearer, 1940; the Círculo's leaders (not named in the source).
  • Sources: https://visualizingcristerowar.com/mexican-migration-to-the-united-states-evolved/
  • Confidence: Medium (one scholarly project page; the paper's Cristero stance is implied by its inclusion there, not quoted).
  • Divergence check: holds — domestic. BENDS: repatriation "to Mexico" in the setting must say which Mexico — the American north or the rump south.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a run of El Amigo del Hogar in a parish basement — the Cristero war as the Calumet's Mexicans read it, week by week. Where an investigator finds the name of a village that burned, and a family that came north with something from its church.

Part G — The shrines, miracles and old images an IHS priest would know

The Virgin of San Juan de los Lagos — the image that raised the acrobat's child (1623); Los Altos de Jalisco, the Cristero heartland

  • Where: San Juan de los Lagos, Los Altos de Jalisco (UNCEDED MEXICO) — the region that gave the Cristero army its core.
  • What happened: A small image of the Immaculate Conception (about 20 inches), brought to the village by Fr. Miguel de Bolonia in 1543 and made by Tarascan craftsmen in the cornstalk-paste technique (titzingueni), became famous in 1623: by the local account an acrobat's daughter, six or seven years old, fell on upturned swords during a performance and died; the chapel caretaker's wife, Ana Lucía, laid the image on the body, "a slight movement under the shroud" followed, and the child was found whole. The present church was begun in 1732, the image installed in 1769, towers finished 1790. Pilgrims walk there in their hundreds of thousands (now over a million) from late January to 2 February (Candlemas), and on 15 August and 8 December. Devotion reached the Texas Rio Grande Valley (a copy at San Juan, Texas) only in the 1950s.
  • The odd detail: a Virgin made of maize paste who raised a circus child killed by swords.
  • People: Miguel de Bolonia — Franciscan who brought the image; Ana Lucía — the caretaker's wife of the 1623 account.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Lady_of_San_Juan_de_los_Lagos
  • Confidence: High on dates; the miracle is the shrine's own tradition (labelled).
  • Divergence check: Los Altos is UNCEDED MEXICO — holds. In the setting the February pilgrimage in 1927–29 crosses active Cristero country; the image's fate during the suspension of worship (1926–29) is a lead (not found).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): three layers of truth in one shrine: the Church's approved miracle; the folk belief that she brings back the dying; and what the setting decides actually answers at San Juan. The pilgrim road of 1928 is where Cristero couriers, federal patrols and whatever follows the pilgrims all walk together.

The Lord of Chalma — the crucifix found in the idol's cave (1530s), where pilgrims dance their sins away

  • Where: Chalma, municipality of Malinalco, State of México (UNCEDED MEXICO) — the second pilgrimage site of Mexico after Guadalupe.
  • What happened: Augustinian friars Nicolás de Perea and Sebastián de Tolentino came to the region about 1537 to preach against the cult of a cave god (Oztoteotl, "god of the cave"); by the shrine's tradition, returning to destroy the idol, they found a life-sized crucified Christ standing where it had been. The sanctuary built around the image draws over two million a year (up to 50,000 on great feasts). Its customs are older than the friars' reading of them: pilgrims dance before the image "to cleanse sins," bathe at the spring, are crowned with flowers, and first-timers circle the ancient ahuehuete cypress three times, some hanging a newborn's umbilical cord in a bag on it. Folk belief: skip the tree-and-spring ritual and you will be turned to stone — the boulders along the Malinalco road are said to be such pilgrims. The proverb for an impossible request: you won't get it "without going to dance at Chalma."
  • The odd detail: the stones on the road are the pilgrims who skipped the rite (legend).
  • People: Nicolás de Perea, Sebastián de Tolentino — Augustinian friars.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalma,_Malinalco · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malinalco (search snippet)
  • Confidence: High on the customs as practised; the apparition and the petrification are tradition/folklore (labelled; the 1537/1539 dating varies by source).
  • Divergence check: UNCEDED MEXICO — holds. (Malinalco also has the Aztec rock-cut temple of the eagle and jaguar warriors, an Aztec-revival cult's natural shrine — M02's territory.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the setting's secular destruction of consecrated ground seed (ALTERNATE_HISTORY §29) in reverse: here the Church itself was built over a cave god, and the Christ "appeared" where the idol stood. The IHS knows the old question: was the cave's power replaced, or only covered?

The Santo Niño de Atocha at Plateros — the child whose shoes wear out (Zacatecas silver country)

  • Where: Plateros, municipality of Fresnillo, Zacatecas (treaty status in the setting UNSETTLED — Zacatecas is silver country, but it is not in §7.4's list of the ceded north).
  • What happened: The devotion began in Spain: legend says that when the Moors held Christians prisoner at Atocha and only children under twelve could bring them food, an unknown child in pilgrim's dress fed the prisoners who had no children — the Christ Child. An image came to Zacatecas about 1554; after a silver strike at Plateros and a mine explosion at Fresnillo, trapped miners said a child came to help them, and each time "the image of the child … was found to be dirty and his clothes had little holes in them." Pilgrims still bring the Niño new shoes, because he wears his out walking at night. The devotion crossed north early: a Santo Niño chapel stands at Chimayó, New Mexico (see next entry).
  • The odd detail: a holy image that leaves its shrine at night and comes back with mud on its shoes — the miners' saint, the prisoners' saint.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santo_Ni%C3%B1o_de_Atocha
  • Confidence: Legend labelled as legend; the Plateros sanctuary's building date (often given as the 19th century) not confirmed this session.
  • Divergence check: Flag for the owner: is Zacatecas ceded? If the treaty took the mineral north "by the rail heads" (§7.4), Fresnillo's silver might be inside it; if not, Plateros sits just south of the line — a border shrine either way.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a child who visits prisoners and trapped miners at night is the kindest possible supernatural in the setting — and exactly what a monster would imitate. An IHS case: miners in an American-owned Zacatecas mine report the Niño; the image's shoes are clean.

El Santuario de Chimayó — the healing earth, sold to save it in 1929

  • Where: Chimayó, New Mexico (US SIDE), in the Sangre de Cristo foothills.
  • What happened: Bernardo Abeyta, a landowner of the Penitente brotherhood, built a chapel about 1810 to the Christ of Esquipulas (a Guatemalan black Christ); on 15 November 1813 he asked leave to build a larger church because of reported healings, and the present sanctuary was finished by 1816. Its side room holds el pocito — a pit of "holy dirt" (tierra bendita) believed to heal; the Church quietly replaces it from the hillsides, up to 25–30 tons a year. Abeyta's descendants owned the chapel into the 20th century; in 1929, in financial trouble, they sold it to the Spanish Colonial Arts Society, which gave it to the Archdiocese of Santa Fe. A Santo Niño de Atocha chapel nearby carries the Plateros devotion north (its founding date not confirmed this session).
  • The odd detail: a shrine whose relic is dirt — refilled by the sacristan — and whose sale in the year of the Crash saved it for the Church.
  • People: Bernardo Abeyta — founder (Penitente); the Spanish Colonial Arts Society — buyers (1929).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Santuario_de_Chimayo · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santo_Ni%C3%B1o_de_Atocha
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds (New Mexico, US SIDE since 1848). The 1929 sale lands in the setting's own crash year (the colonial-speculation boom's collapse).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the IHS's American shrine of the southern devotions — Penitente brothers, a black Christ from Guatemala, earth that heals. Who else bid on the chapel in 1929? The Hunter Guild buys ground like this; so might something that needs the earth for a different purpose.
