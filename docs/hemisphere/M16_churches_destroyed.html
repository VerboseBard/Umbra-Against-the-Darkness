M16 — Churches destroyed, desecrated, closed, seized or turned to other uses: Mexico and the Caribbean, 1910–1940

Lane: M16 (Mexico–Caribbean sweep, 2026-09-30) · Owner's request: "destruction of old churches." Date written: 2026-09-30 · Model: Sonnet 5.5 · Status: COMPLETE for this pass (32 entries; time-boxed ~40 min)

Overview

The destruction of old churches in this hemisphere between 1910 and 1940 has three separate engines, and the file is organised by them. (1) The revolutionary state — first the Constitutionalist armies of 1914–17 (Guadalajara's Cathedral opened and its bishops' tombs exhumed in July 1914; Mérida's Cathedral sacked on 24 September 1915 and its famous crucifix lost; churches and convents handed to a union in Mexico City), then the Calles-era and Cárdenas-era state governments of 1926–37 (Tabasco under Garrido Canabal — a cathedral closed, burned, made a school and demolished; 68 of 85 churches turned into schools by 1930; villages renamed off the saints' calendar; Veracruz under Tejeda; Sonora under Calles's son; Chiapas under Grajales). (2) The people's answer — hidden images (the Guadalupe tilma in a Chinese wardrobe, 1926–29; a Tacotalpa patroness carried into Chiapas), reoccupied ruins (La Conchita, Villahermosa, May 1937; Hermosillo's women, 1937), and reprisals (Tlapacoyan, 1931; Coyoacán, 1934). (3) Nature and the empire in the islands — the 1918 San Fermín earthquake and the 1928, 1930, 1931 and 1932 hurricanes and quakes did most of the church-breaking in Puerto Rico, Cuba, Belize and Nicaragua; the only state campaign against religious practice I could attest in the islands is Haiti's 1935 decree-law against Vodou. I found no page that puts US Marines quartered in a church in Haiti, the Dominican Republic or Nicaragua (see "Leads not chased").

What matters for the setting. The owner's own line in ALTERNATE_HISTORY §29.3 — anti-clerical modernisers pull down a chapel that was built where it was because something is underneath it; the records were lost with the priest who kept them — has an almost literal real-record match in Villahermosa's Cathedral of Esquipulas (closed 1928, burned 1930, a "rationalist" school, razed 1934; the black Christ vanished) and in Guadalajara's Cathedral (soldiers who came "for weapons" opened the bishops' tombs, 1914). The divergence line is clean: everything in the 1914–17 section is pre-1916 and happened; Jalisco, Yucatán, Tabasco, Chiapas, Puebla, Oaxaca, Veracruz and Mexico City are all UNCEDED MEXICO, so the Cristero-era church wars run in the setting exactly as recorded if a Mexican state exists to run them (M01's open question). The one place the record cannot happen is Sonora (CEDED NORTH) — Rodolfo Elías Calles's campaign of the 1930s — and there the occupier's role is the whole story: does America close, or protect, thirty churches? Two small corrections to popular accounts: the capital's "San Juan Bautista → Villahermosa" change was Governor Múgica's decree of 3 February 1916, not Garrido's (Garrido renamed the villages); and the 1926–29 tilma hiding is dated by different sources to 26 or 28 June 1929 for its return.

Respect: Catholic folk devotion, Indigenous Catholic practice and Vodou are living religions; the setting's monsters are an overlay, not the faithful. The anticlerical states in this file were small fanatical groups acting on a devout population — the "cult versus population" rule inverted. Sources on both sides are partisan and each entry says whose account it is.

PART A — The revolutionary decade: sackings, closures and seized churches (1914–1917)

Method note: the anticlerical acts of 1914–17 came from the Constitutionalist (Carrancista) armies, not from the Villistas or Zapatistas as a rule; the most-quoted sources are partisan on both sides (Canudas writes as a defender of the Constitutionalists; the Catholic chapter memorials and the exiled Carmelite prioress write as victims). Each entry says whose account it is.

Guadalajara Cathedral looted, the bishops' tombs opened, 120 priests jailed — July 1914

  • Where: Guadalajara, Jalisco — the Cathedral and the city's churches; the "Escobedo" Penitentiary.
  • What happened: Constitutionalist troops under Gen. Álvaro Obregón entered Guadalajara on 8 July 1914 and Gen. Manuel M. Diéguez took the state government. Soldiers occupied churches including the Cathedral, dug in them "looking for weapons", and — according to the cathedral chapter's memorial to Carranza — scattered consecrated hosts, took the ciboria and monstrances, stripped the jewels and vessels, and exhumed and desecrated the remains of bishops from the Cathedral's tombs (the soldiers apparently expected treasure). The chapter put the Cathedral's loss at over 800,000 pesos of objects. On 21 July 1914 more than a hundred priests were jailed; Canudas counts over 120 priests of all nationalities in the Escobedo Penitentiary, held incommunicado on charges of conspiracy and hiding arms. Soldiers are said to have ridden into the Cathedral on horseback, and Diéguez is said to have demolished the Cathedral's atrium that year (both from a search summary, not a page I could read). Foreign priests (Jesuits, Marists, Salesians and others) were given five days to leave in early August 1914 and were put on a train to Manzanillo. The churches were reopened when Villa took Guadalajara on 17 December 1914.
  • The odd detail: the soldiers were digging in the Cathedral for weapons, and what they opened were the tombs of bishops; the sources I read say only that the remains were "desecrated," not what the tombs held.
  • People: Manuel M. Diéguez — Constitutionalist governor and military commander of Jalisco; Álvaro Obregón — commander of the army that took the city; Francisco Orozco y Jiménez — archbishop of Guadalajara (in hiding; the Constitutionalists searched for him); Manuel Aguirre Berlanga — state secretary who served the expulsion notices.
  • Sources: https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S1665-44202019000200068&script=sci_arttext_plus&tlng=es (read — quotes the cathedral chapter's memorial to Carranza) · https://archivos.juridicas.unam.mx/www/bjv/libros/7/3101/10.pdf (Canudas, read — the 120 priests, the fine on the archbishop, the expulsion train) · https://www.sinembargo.mx/1172566/villistas-y-carrancistas-disputaron-guadalajara-como-una-joya/ (via search — horses, 17 Dec reopening; page returned 403)
  • Confidence: High on the occupation, the priests jailed and the expulsions; Medium on the 800,000-peso figure (the chapter's own estimate) and on the horse detail (a search summary, not a page I read).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened as recorded. Jalisco is UNCEDED MEXICO in every version of the setting.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Secular destruction of consecrated ground" (ALTERNATE_HISTORY §29.3) with a tomb in it: soldiers who came looking for weapons opened a bishop's tomb, and a canon writing the chapter's inventory of losses knows one entry is not on the treasure list. An IHS archivist finds it in the 1914 memorial in the 1930s. Treat the soldiers as ordinary men in a violent year, not as a cult.

Eight Guadalajara churches and the Cathedral shut — mid-1917

  • Where: Guadalajara, Jalisco — the Cathedral and seven other temples where the archbishop's pastoral letter was read.
  • What happened: After the bishops protested the Constitution of 1917, the Jalisco government identified eight temples in Guadalajara where Archbishop Orozco y Jiménez's pastoral letter had been read from the pulpit, searched them, arrested priests, and closed all eight, the Cathedral among them, posting a decree on the doors that churches are national property and that meetings inciting people against the supreme law would not be permitted. Canudas puts the pastoral about four months after the Constitution was sworn (so about June 1917; the page I read gives no day). Later "as an act of tolerance" the state treasury official handed the Cathedral back to Dean Manuel Alvarado and the governor promised to return the other seven.
  • The odd detail: the closure decree cites the President's authorisation to "retire from service" a church, the way a ship is retired from service.
  • People: Francisco Orozco y Jiménez — archbishop, in hiding; Dean Manuel Alvarado — received the Cathedral back; the unnamed governor of Jalisco (name not on the page read).
  • Sources: https://archivos.juridicas.unam.mx/www/bjv/libros/7/3101/10.pdf (Canudas, read; partisan for the government)
  • Confidence: Medium (single source; year and month inferred from the source's own chronology).
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly. In the setting the Constitution of Querétaro is either a defiant charter of a defeated rump Mexico (M01 recommends) or is written differently; if it exists, Jalisco (unceded) still acts against its own bishops. If it does not exist, the 1917 closure vanishes — flag for the owner.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the first "the churches are shut and the Bishop is hiding" summer, ten years before the Cristero War. In an IHS file this is the first time a diocese runs without its priests — and an episode to compare with the 1926–29 shutdown (M01).

Santa Brígida's church and convent handed to a labour union — January–February 1915

  • Where: Mexico City — the church and convent of Santa Brígida and the Jesuit Colegio Josefino (exact streets not on the page read).
  • What happened: In January 1915 Obregón, commanding the Constitutionalist garrison of the capital, "officially handed" the church and convent of Santa Brígida and the Colegio Josefino to the Casa del Obrero Mundial, the anarcho-syndicalist union federation that raised the Red Battalions. A month later (21 February 1915) union members marched from the Colegio Josefino armed with clubs against Catholics protesting the arrest of priests. Obregón had demanded half a million pesos from the archdiocese for the city's poor, and when it was not paid he summoned all 400 priests of the archdiocese to the Palacio Nacional: 168 came (129 Mexicans, 33 Spaniards, 3 Germans, a Pole, a Syrian, an Argentine) and were arrested. The US government's pressure won the release of 39 foreign priests, who left through Veracruz.
  • The odd detail: the union that became Carranza's Red Battalions got its first headquarters out of a church and a Jesuit college.
  • People: Álvaro Obregón — commander; the Casa del Obrero Mundial — the labour federation; the vicar general Paredes — who ignored the half-million-peso demand.
  • Sources: https://archivos.juridicas.unam.mx/www/bjv/libros/7/3101/10.pdf (Canudas, read). Not confirmed by the English or Spanish Wikipedia pages on the Casa del Obrero Mundial (read; they give the Tepito address only).
  • Confidence: Medium-Low (single, partisan source; the handover is not corroborated).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened as recorded; Mexico City is unceded.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Jesuit college and a convent — the IHS's own kind of house — turned into a union hall. The Jesuit order's records for the Colegio Josefino are exactly the kind of thing "lost with the priest who kept them." Keep the workers as workers.

"They rode into the churches on horseback" — the claim and the record, 1914–1919

  • Where: several dioceses; the source is an open letter from the Discalced Carmelite prioress of Querétaro, writing from the Carmelite convent of San José in Havana, to the Archbishop of New Orleans, late 1915.
  • What happened: The prioress, María Elías del Santísimo Sacramento (a Mme. María Thiery), wrote that "our temples are closed and our churches are profaned … entering on horseback, trampling the relics and throwing the hosts on the floor," that "in some churches the Carrancistas have pretended to say Mass and done obscene things," and that nuns "are taken to the barracks." The letter was circulated as an open letter and, in Canudas's telling, helped make the religious conflict international. The historian Canudas, writing from the Constitutionalist side, notes the claim and answers it with the counter-charge that the clergy backed Huerta. Separately, a Mexican Church history summarised in a 2007 review says the Church's temples, seminaries and schools were confiscated between 1914 and 1919 and used as barracks, stables and stores, and that Obregón returned them after taking office in 1920 — a claim I have only in a search summary, not a page I read.
  • The odd detail: the loudest account of Carrancista desecration reached the United States from a Mexican convent in Havana — exile networks already ran through the city the setting puts at the centre of the Golden Circle's map.
  • People: María Elías del Santísimo Sacramento — the Querétaro prioress in Havana exile; the Archbishop of New Orleans, James Blenk — the letter's addressee.
  • Sources: https://archivos.juridicas.unam.mx/www/bjv/libros/7/3101/10.pdf (Canudas, read); https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S0187-57952007000300005 (via search — the 1914–1919 confiscation-and-return claim, UNVERIFIED this session)
  • Confidence: Medium that the letter exists as quoted (Canudas quotes it); Low on any specific church (no church is named in the letter).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 in origin; BENDS on the Havana link — in the setting Havana is under American occupation from the start, so exiled Mexican nuns in Havana are in an American-run city (see M03/M11).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): an exile convent in Havana is a ready-made IHS way-station and a listening-post for the Golden Circle. What did the nuns take with them out of Querétaro? "They took the relics with them" is the line.

Mérida Cathedral sacked; the Cristo de las Ampollas lost — 24 September 1915

  • Where: Cathedral of San Ildefonso, Mérida, Yucatán (on the Plaza Grande); the Episcopal Palace beside it.
  • What happened: Salvador Alvarado was the Constitutionalist governor and military commander of the peninsula; his arrival "sharpened" the religious conflict (Canudas). Under his predecessor, in 1914, 37 foreign secular priests and 8 regulars had been expelled within five days, and by 1918 state law let only six priests function (Signos Históricos). On 24 September 1915 after speeches in the main plaza against "fanaticism," a crowd — the historian Ángel Gutiérrez Romero says dockworkers from Progreso — broke into the Cathedral. They smashed the 17th–18th-century baroque and churrigueresque altarpieces, the treasury and the Walcker organ (German-built, installed 1902), wrecked the pulpit and its stair, and beheaded or burned the images of San Ildefonso (patron), San Juan Nepomuceno, Our Lady of Mercy, the Purísima, the Santo Niño de Atocha, Sts. Peter, Paul and Andrew, San Isidro and the Trinity. In the chapel of the Cristo de las Ampollas — the Cathedral's wealthiest and best-loved image — the municipal band, forced by the crowd, is said to have played La Cucaracha while the mob tried to burn the crucifix; when it would not take fire they threw it in the street. Police took it to the military headquarters and its fate is unknown: the cathedral's own history says the original was destroyed, burned or stolen, and Archbishop Martín Tritschler had a copy carved in Querétaro and blessed it on 24 September 1919, the fourth anniversary of the sack (PorEsto; the copy was paid for by Rafael Quintero) ⟨fixed: V_M16⟩. Three days after the sack the building was handed to canon José Correa and Father Pablo Ortiz. In 1916 the Chapels of San José and of the Rosary were demolished to separate the Cathedral from the Episcopal Palace; the gap is now the pedestrian Pasaje de la Revolución.
  • The odd detail: the legend of the image is that it was carved for the church at Ichmul by a stranger sculptor (the folk story says an angel), and survived that church's fire with blisters in the paint — ampollas — and here it was, 270 years later, a crowd unable to burn it again. (Legend, from the Ichmul tradition; not a documented event.)
  • People: Salvador Alvarado — governor and military commander of Yucatán; Martín Tritschler y Córdova — Archbishop/Bishop of Yucatán; Ángel Gutiérrez Romero — the UADY historian whose lecture gives the Progreso-dockworker detail; Juan de la Huerta — the priest of the legend who is said to have bequeathed the image to the Cathedral (d. 1644).
  • Sources: https://www.marista.edu.mx/noticia/760/evocan-el-saqueo-a-catedral (read) · https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catedral_de_San_Ildefonso_(M%C3%A9rida) (read — the organ, the retablos, the chapels of 1916; it says the Cristo "survived" — see discrepancy) · https://www.meridadeyucatan.com/la-catedral-de-merida-yucatan/ and http://catedraldemerida.org.mx/santos/el-cristo-de-las-ampollas/ (via search — the list of decapitated images, the band, the fate of the Christ) · https://archivos.juridicas.unam.mx/www/bjv/libros/7/3101/10.pdf (Canudas, read — Alvarado's demonstration; the Catholic press said the doors of the churches were knocked down)
  • Confidence: High on the date and the sack; Medium on the Christ's fate — sources disagree (destroyed/unknown vs. "survived"); the La Cucaracha detail is from one local history.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened as recorded. Yucatán is UNCEDED and far from the northern line. Germany's henequen interest in Yucatán and the Walcker organ's German maker make a light Germany thread; not otherwise BENT.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the IHS's own long file on a crucifix that was already said to have come through a fire and a stranger's hands, and was lost in 1915 with the police station as its last known address. The 1919 copy from Querétaro is the visible image; someone in the 1930s asks which one. Report the devotion with respect; the hook is the object's history, not the people who venerate it.

PART B — Mexico City: the bomb, the schism and the hidden tilma (1921–1929)

The bomb in the flowers — Basilica of Guadalupe, 14 November 1921

  • Where: the old Basilica of Guadalupe, Tepeyac, north of Mexico City (see M12 for the site).
  • What happened: On the morning of 14 November 1921, around 10:30, after Mass, a bomb of dynamite hidden in a flower arrangement placed at the foot of the Virgin's image exploded on the main altar. It shattered the windows, pulverised the marble beneath the picture, wrecked the altar rail and steps and the brass candlesticks, and twisted a 34-kilo bronze-and-iron crucifix into an arc. The tilma and its glass were undamaged. The bomber, Luciano Pérez (sources give Pérez Carrillo and Pérez Carpio), was described as an employee of the Private Secretariat of the Presidency (one source: a railroad worker; one: an army soldier) with, in the sacristan's notarial record, "saffron-coloured hair." Authorities stopped a lynching, reportedly on Obregón's own order. The crucifix — the Santo Cristo del Atentado — is displayed in the new Basilica; repairs raised the image about a metre. The English Wikipedia says the bent crucifix was preserved.
  • The odd detail: the notary's record from the Basilica's sacristan Ignacio Díaz de León describes the bomber's hair; the crucifix bent in front of the image, not around it.
  • People: Luciano Pérez (Carrillo/Carpio) — the bomber; Ignacio Díaz de León — the Basilica sacristan; Álvaro Obregón — president; Feliciano Cortés — abbot at the time of the 1926 hiding (below).
  • Sources: https://www.excelsior.com.mx/nacional/el-cristo-que-protegio-a-la-virgen-de-guadalupe-se-cumplen-hoy-100-anos-del-atentado (read) · https://www.infobae.com/mexico/2024/12/13/quien-fue-la-persona-que-atento-contra-la-imagen-de-la-virgen-en-la-basilica-de-guadalupe-hace-mas-de-100-anos/ (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_of_Our_Lady_of_Guadalupe (read) · https://www.kofc.org/en/news-room/our-lady-of-guadalupe/truths/9bomb.html (via search)
  • Confidence: High on date, bomb, damage and crucifix; Medium on the bomber's name and employment (sources differ); the bomber's motive and sentence are not on any page I read.
  • Divergence check: after 1916, so BENDS lightly: it needs a Mexican President in Mexico City in 1921 (Obregón in the record; who governs the rump state in the setting is M01's open question). The Basilica is UNCEDED MEXICO, so nothing in the event needs the occupier.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the strongest single image in this lane for the IHS: a bomb at the foot of the tilma and the image untouched. The Church calls it a miracle; the setting can leave that alone. The hook is the bomber: a man employed by the Presidency, hair the colour of saffron, kept from a lynching on a President's order — who sent him, and was anyone ever asked?

La Soledad seized for a "national church" — Mexico City, 21 February 1925

  • Where: the church of Nuestra Señora de la Soledad (finished 1787, consecrated 1792), La Merced quarter, Mexico City.
  • What happened: On 21 February 1925 about a hundred men [UNVERIFIED — V_M16], backed by the CROM labour federation, seized La Soledad from its pastor (Father Silva, per TIME) and installed the ex-priest José Joaquín Pérez Budar (a Freemason) as "Patriarch" of the Iglesia Católica Apostólica Mexicana (ICAM), calling themselves the "Knights of the Order of Guadalupe." The new church said Mass in Spanish, allowed priests to marry and charged no tithes. Parishioners rioted the next day; fighting over churches followed in Chilpancingo, Orizaba, Veracruz, Morelia and Tabasco (TIME), with government troops sometimes defending the Catholic side. On the first Sunday of Lent Pérez preached to thousands from the church steps; President Calles, tacitly supportive at first, had La Soledad padlocked as Lent ended. ICAM later held about 70 churches (mostly in small villages) between 1925 and 1937, most soon closed again; two ICAM priests were killed in disputes over buildings.
  • The odd detail: Calles's government was tacitly friendly to a schismatic church taking over Catholic churches, then padlocked the schismatic's flagship when Lent ended (TIME's reading).
  • People: José Joaquín Pérez Budar — "Patriarch"; Father Silva — the ousted pastor; the CROM — the union federation; Plutarco Elías Calles — president 1924–28.
  • Sources: https://time.com/archive/6653449/religion-mr-perez/ (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Catholic_Apostolic_Church (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_La_Soledad,_Mexico_City (read; architecture only)
  • Confidence: High on the date, the seizure and the padlock; Medium on the number of buildings (Ramírez, via Wikipedia).
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly. Calles is a Sonoran (his home state is ceded north in the setting; M01's open question is whether his government exists); the CROM and the capital are UNCEDED. The event needs only a Mexican government in 1925.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a church seized by armed men who call themselves "Knights of the Order of Guadalupe" — a knightly-order name-form that echoes the Knights of the Golden Circle. Treat the ICAM's rank and file as ordinary believers in a state-sponsored schism; the hook is who paid the hundred men.

The tilma in a wardrobe — Basilica of Guadalupe, 29 July 1926 – June 1929

  • Where: the old Basilica of Guadalupe, Tepeyac; a private house in central Mexico City; the wall of the neighbouring Sacramentarian convent.
  • What happened: With the Calles Law about to take effect (31 July 1926) and fearing Callista attacks on the Basilica, the abbot Feliciano Cortés y Mora had the original tilma removed on the night of 29 July 1926, wrapped in silk and burlap, and smuggled out through a hole cut in the wall of the Sacramentarian convent and driven in "a dilapidated moving truck filled with household items" to a private house, where it was hidden in the double bottom of a small Chinese wardrobe. The painter Rafael Aguirre made a replica, "with somewhat more vivid colours," which hung in the frame so that the faithful would not suspect. The house belonged to the engineer Luis Felipe Murguía Terroba (Excélsior; on calle de Meave per Infovaticana). The wardrobe was opened before a notary by the abbot, two canons, Murguía and three witnesses on 28 June 1929 (Infovaticana; desdelafe.mx says 26 June; the Excélsior and ACI Prensa pages give no day) ⟨fixed: V_M16⟩, two days before public worship resumed after the arreglos, and the original was re-framed that night with no ceremony. (Excélsior's line — that the image stayed in Murguía's house from 30 July 1926 to 30 July 1927 — is not a typo: ACI Prensa and Infovaticana say the wardrobe was then moved, about 15 July 1927, to the Murguía family's bookstore on Avenida 16 de Septiembre, where it stayed until 1929 ⟨fixed: V_M16⟩.)
  • The odd detail: for three years the pilgrims of the Americas prayed to a copy, and only the abbot, the canons and a wardrobe's owner knew.
  • People: Feliciano Cortés y Mora — abbot of the Basilica; Rafael Aguirre — the painter; Luis Felipe Murguía Terroba — the engineer whose house hid the tilma; Ana Rita Valero and Arturo Cortés — historians (Santa María de Guadalupe a la luz de la historia) cited as source.

PART C — Tabasco under Garrido Canabal: the buildings

(M01 has Garrido's summary entry. Everything below is new and named.)

The Cathedral of Esquipulas, Villahermosa — closed 1928, sacked 1930, demolished 1934

  • Where: Villahermosa (then still often called by its full "San Juan Bautista"), Tabasco — the cathedral church of the Lord of Esquipulas, built 1776 to house a black Christ donated on 18 March 1774 by the Bishop of Yucatán, Diego de Peredo (the image came from Esquipulas, Guatemala).
  • What happened: Governor Tomás Garrido Canabal ordered churches closed and religious images destroyed across the state. The Cathedral was closed in 1928, sacked, burned and stripped of its images in 1930, and turned into a "Rationalist School" (the "Francisco Ferrer i Guardia" school, named for the Spanish anarchist-educator); before that it also served as headquarters of the Liga Central de Resistencia, Garrido's party machine. The building was demolished in 1934. The historian Carlos Martínez Assad (quoted in the SciELO study) says the state's cathedral and principal churches were "partially destroyed to house schools from 1925." The DHIAL dictionary says the black Christ of Esquipulas and an image of the Virgin "brought by Cortés" disappeared in the destruction; a Villahermosa source says every image was thrown on a pyre in 1930. After Garrido, Bishop José de Jesús del Valle y Navarro began rebuilding in 1945; the new Cathedral of the Lord of Tabasco was finished in 1970 and consecrated by John Paul II on 11 May 1990. One source says a new Christ was brought from Toluca in 1944.
  • The odd detail: the cathedral was not simply pulled down: it took a sequence — locked (1928), burned (1930), used as a party office and a school, then razed — four separate desecrations on one site over six years.
  • People: Tomás Garrido Canabal — governor (1920–24, 1931–34) and master of Tabasco to 1935; Diego de Peredo — the Bishop of Yucatán who donated the Christ (1774); José de Jesús del Valle y Navarro — Tabasco's bishop who began the rebuilding (1945).

Villahermosa's name and the villages renamed — 1916 and the 1930s

The parish church of the Asunción, Tlapacoyan — 17 October 1931 (adds to M01)

  • Where: Tlapacoyan, Veracruz — the parish church of the Asunción de María Santísima and the municipal palace.
  • What happened: M01 has the reprisal on the town hall. Detail from the SciELO study: on 17 October 1931 the parish church was "profaned by unknowns, who burned the images of the temple," including more than ten paintings from the San Joaquín chapel of the El Jobo hacienda that had been left there for safekeeping. Local testimony says police from Xalapa entered the church, some on horseback, to remove and burn the saints; the government blamed Catholic activists. The crowd (200–300 by the official count) then surrounded the municipal palace, used firearms, machetes and burning chile smoke, and killed municipal officials, mostly agrarian leaders Tejeda's land programme had promoted; one survivor escaped. The parish stayed closed: in 1932 the town's civic board asked federal authorities to reopen it, citing the destruction of images and furniture, and in 1933 the request was denied on reports of illegal religious services. Two men, Miguel Mota Benavides and Baldomero Mota, were charged (case 132, Jalacingo court). The same study lists other Veracruz towns where "religious fetishes" (images) were destroyed in 1931: Otatitlán, Jáltipan, Tierra Blanca, Medellín, Huatusco.

  • People: Miguel Mota Benavides and Baldomero Mota — charged in the Jalacingo court; Adalberto Tejeda — governor; the "police from Xalapa" — the alleged burners.
  • Sources: https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S1665-44202018000200232 (read)

  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a deposited image: a saint that was sent to the parish for safekeeping and burned in someone else's church. The hacienda owner (or the estate's heirs) wants the accounting — who else deposited what? The parish stays closed to 1933+, i.e., a sealed church, not a demolished one: the perfect module set-piece.

Sonora's "de-fanaticising": about thirty churches shut, a bishop in the mountains — 1931–1937

  • Where: Sonora, notably Hermosillo (Plaza Zaragoza and its kiosk), Úres and Nacozari.
  • What happened: Rodolfo Elías Calles (son of ex-president Plutarco; governor 1931–35 ⟨fixed: V_M16⟩) ran a "de-fanaticising" campaign whose stated targets, in the historian Ignacio Almada's summary, included "closing the temples, expelling priests, burning saints, persecuting the bishop." He asked President Abelardo L. Rodríguez to apply the federal decree so as to close about thirty temples; the state budget gave the campaign 37,042 pesos of 1,420,000; a state law split Sonora into 16 districts with one priest each (each needing the governor's permission; fines of 50–100 pesos or up to eight days in jail); arrest warrants were issued for the priests and for Bishop Juan Navarrete y Guerrero, who stayed hidden in the Sonoran mountains until 1937. Catholic women ran the resistance: at Hermosillo a crowd of women gathered in the Plaza Zaragoza, a teacher named Antonia Figueroa told them "the government could not do more than God," and speakers at the kiosk said they must "recover the churches at all costs" (city report, quoted by Almada). At Úres women led by ex-teachers broke up a party meeting. Prayer went on in private homes. Román Yocupicio took the governorship on 4 January 1937; that year the churches were reopened by women crying "Viva la Virgen de Guadalupe, Viva Yocupicio, Viva Cristo Rey," and the bishop and all the priests returned to Hermosillo; in 1939 Yocupicio invited the bishop to lay the first stone of the University of Sonora.
  • The odd detail: the campaign's budget line — 37,042 pesos of a 1,420,000-peso state budget, about 2.6 percent — closed thirty churches cheaply.
  • People: Rodolfo Elías Calles — governor 1931–35 ⟨fixed: V_M16⟩; Juan Navarrete y Guerrero — the hidden bishop (arrived June 1919); Antonia Figueroa — the teacher at the Hermosillo plaza; Román Yocupicio — governor from 1937; Abelardo L. Rodríguez — president who issued the decree.
  • Sources: https://revistas.ues.edu.sv/index.php/conjsociologicas/article/download/1531/1468/4523 (Elizabeth Cejudo Ramos, "Acción colectiva y género: mujeres contra la campaña desfanatizadora en Sonora (1932–1936)" — downloaded and read locally) · cross-ref M08 (Calles's 1915 priest ban in Sonora)
  • Confidence: High on the numbers and events (an academic study quoting state archives and Almada 2009); Medium on the exact 1931–34 dating of each closure.
  • Divergence check: CEDED NORTH — DOES NOT HAPPEN AS RECORDED. Sonora is American in the setting; there is no Sonoran governor named Calles to close thirty churches. M08 already asks whether Sonoran churches shut in 1915–16 reopen under American law. This entry is the same question in the 1930s: the setting's Sonoran churches are either open under an occupier or closed by one.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the occupier's role: an American Protestant or Circle-adjacent official in Sonora closing "about thirty" churches by executive order would be an anti-Catholic move that the IHS could not ignore; or the opposite — the occupier keeps every church open and protects the bishop, and the IHS's northern base grows in an occupier's shadow. Chiapas under Grajales — Ocozocoautla, Tapachula's San Agustín, and the one licensed priest, 1932–1936

  • Where: Chiapas — Ocozocoautla de Espinosa, Copainalá, Tapilula, Jiquipilas, Chiapilla; Tapachula (the church of San Agustín); Ixtapa.
  • What happened: Governor Victórico R. Grajales (Dec 1932 – 1936) ran an "antialcohol and religious de-fanaticisation" campaign through the state's Department of Social Action; one summary says he closed all the churches in Chiapas and forced the priests underground before the federal school reform of 1934. A study of the Zoque region reports that in Ocozocoautla, in November 1934, the municipal authorities closed the parish church and several hermitages, burned the religious images and ransacked the temple on the pretext of making it a school; images were also destroyed at Chiapilla, Jiquipilas, Tapilula and Copainalá. In Tapachula the church of San Agustín had been seized by the schismatic ICAM in 1926, and the federal government closed it in June 1932; petitions from several groups to convert it into a school were denied ⟨fixed: V_M16⟩. Santa Mónica, Puebla — the convent that hid as a block of flats (found 18 or 20 May 1934)

PART E — The Caribbean and the Marines' hemisphere: earthquakes, hurricanes, decrees

Method note: in the islands the destroyers of churches in 1910–1940 are mostly nature (the 1918 San Fermín earthquake, the 1928, 1930, 1931 and 1932 hurricanes, the 1931 Managua quake), not anticlerical states. I searched for churches used as barracks by the Marines in Haiti, the Dominican Republic and Nicaragua and found none attested in the pages I read — listed under "Leads not chased." The one attested state campaign against religious practice in the islands is Haiti's against Vodou, and it is reported here with the period's framing marked.

The San Fermín earthquake — churches down across Puerto Rico's west, 11 October 1918

  • Where: Aguadilla (San Carlos Borromeo), Mayagüez (Nuestra Señora de la Candelaria cathedral), San Germán (San Germán de Auxerre), Humacao, Vega Baja (Santa María del Rosario), Arecibo (San Felipe Apóstol).
  • What happened: A magnitude-7.1 earthquake struck at 10:14 a.m. on 11 October 1918 off the northwest coast, with a tsunami; 76–118 died and property loss ran $4–29 million. Churches suffered badly: San Carlos Borromeo in Aguadilla was "either destroyed or partially damaged" — its south tower (added 1874) was rebuilt lower and the façade "somewhat changed"; the Mayagüez cathedral was severely damaged and its towers toppled; the San Germán church was badly affected; Humacao's original bell towers collapsed; at Vega Baja the main structure collapsed (Wikipedia's earthquake article) or the clock tower was heavily damaged (its church article) — the two pages disagree; the Arecibo cathedral was damaged. No page I read gives casualties inside churches.
  • The odd detail: at Aguadilla the south tower was rebuilt lower than before and the façade was "somewhat changed" — the church still shows the quake in its profile.
  • People: the Aguadilla, Mayagüez and Humacao parishes (no named priests on the pages read).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1918_San_Ferm%C3%ADn_earthquake (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iglesia_de_San_Carlos_Borromeo (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Santa_Mar%C3%ADa_del_Rosario_of_Vega_Baja (read)
  • Confidence: High on the earthquake; Medium on the individual church damage (Wikipedia pages, no engineering sources).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916? No — 1918, after the break, but a natural event: holds. Puerto Rico is American in both worlds; the setting's occupier is already in charge (M05).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a 1918 earthquake that took towers off a chain of coastal and inland parishes — an IHS survey of what each parish's repairs uncovered (a sealed crypt under a fallen tower, a bell recast). The quake and tsunami are natural; the GM decides what a cracked tower reveals.

The clock tower that fell twice — Vega Baja, 1918 and 13 September 1928

  • Where: Church of Santa María del Rosario, Vega Baja, Puerto Rico (built 1850–74; consecrated 1860; inaugurated 12 March 1870).
  • What happened: The church's original clock tower was heavily damaged by the 1918 earthquake and finally destroyed by the San Felipe Segundo hurricane on 13 September 1928 (312 dead across the island; the only Category-5 landfall on record in Puerto Rico). The hurricane also damaged churches, factories and haciendas across the east and Vieques.
  • The odd detail: the same tower took two hits, ten years apart, and never came back as it was.
  • People: none named.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Santa_Mar%C3%ADa_del_Rosario_of_Vega_Baja (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_the_1928_Okeechobee_hurricane_in_the_Caribbean (via search)

Santiago de Cuba's cathedral cracked — the earthquake of 3 February 1932

  • Where: Santiago de Cuba — the Cathedral of the Assumption, the churches of Dolores and San Francisco.
  • What happened: A magnitude-6.7 earthquake hit Santiago at about 1:15 a.m. on 3 February 1932 (one local page gives 1:12; the Spanish Wikipedia summary gives a 5 p.m. tremor — the sources conflict). The Cathedral's towers cracked and were thought likely to need demolition; the cathedral clock stopped at 1:14; the angel statue was damaged (local sources); the churches of Dolores and San Francisco were also badly damaged. About 80 percent of the city's buildings were affected; deaths are given as 8–14 (sources differ), injured about 300 (both Wikipedias; the local page says about 200; one source: nearly 2,000) ⟨fixed: V_M16⟩. The jail's prisoners were moved to the barracks. A local account says that on 10, 17 and 24 January Fathers Callejas and Cresencio Pajares told congregations of an approaching "test."
  • The odd detail: the priests' sermons before the quake — a period anecdote (not a verified prophecy) — and the stopped clock.
  • People: Fr. Cresencio Pajares and Fr. Callejas — the priests who warned of "a trial"; the victims Antonio Pagés, María Casas Suárez, Ofelia Rondón and her child (named by one local page).
  • Sources: https://santiagoenmi.wordpress.com/2010/12/20/el-terremoto-del-32/ (read) · https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terremoto_de_Cuba_de_1932 (read; differing figures) · https://www.cubanet.org/hace-91-anos-un-terremoto-casi-devasto-a-santiago-de-cuba/ (via search)
  • Confidence: High on the event, the damage to the cathedral and the two churches; Low-Medium on the clock and angel details and the priest's warnings (local anecdote).
  • Divergence check: natural event; holds. Cuba is under American occupation in the setting; Santiago's US military presence (Guantánamo) is nearby.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the cathedral clock stopped at 1:14 and the priests spoke of a "test" three Sundays earlier — a period detail for an investigator who notes the sermons in a diocesan bulletin. The occult layer: something did warn them; the IHS's "warning" file has a Santiago entry.

Haiti's "superstitious practices" decree-law — 5 September 1935 (and the Marines' confiscation of ritual objects)

  • Where: all Haiti — the state's law and the Marine Corps's and courts' practice under the 1915–34 occupation.
  • What happened: Haiti's government issued a decree-law on "superstitious practices" on 5 September 1935, repealed by Article 297 of the 1987 Constitution, which lists "the decree law of September 5, 1935 on superstitious beliefs" (a June 2012 amendment is reported to have removed Article 297 itself, not to have repealed the decree-law) ⟨fixed: V_M16⟩. Reviews of Kate Ramsey's The Spirits and the Law say Haitian governments, the Catholic Church and the occupying US forces all used anti-"superstition" laws against Vodou, and that during the occupation trials and "the confiscation and destruction of ritual objects" were part of the pattern. No page I read gives a named temple, date or place for a specific raid. The 1941–42 Church-led "rejeté" campaign (destroying temples and sacred objects) falls just outside the window and I could not confirm details.
  • The odd detail: the law's own term — "superstitious" — is the period's category, kept in the statute for 52 years (1935–1987) ⟨fixed: V_M16⟩.
  • People: Kate Ramsey — the historian (2011); the Haitian state, the Catholic Church and the US Marines as the three actors named by the reviews.
  • Sources: https://hemisphericinstitute.org/en/emisferica-101/10-1-book-reviews/the-spirits-and-the-law-voudou-and-power-in-haiti.html (read; a review, no specifics) · https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo10454972.html (via search) · https://pdba.georgetown.edu/Constitutions/Haiti/haiti1987.html (Art. 297, read — V_M16)

  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly. The occupation of Haiti (real: 1915–34) runs longer in the setting ("the occupier never leaves"); the 1935 decree-law (issued in the record under Sténio Vincent's government, after the real US withdrawal of 1934 — general knowledge, not on the pages read) would be issued under an American-supervised Haitian government. The confiscations by Marines are pre-1934 in the record and hold.

The hook is on the state side: a store-room of confiscated objects (the Marines' and courts' inventory) in Port-au-Prince that no one can return, that Vodou communities want back, and that an IHS or Secret Service inspector finds is mis-labelled. The Golden Circle's interest in the objects would be a theft, not a religion.

The cathedral whose iron frame stood — Managua, 31 March 1931
