M10 — The European Caribbean colonies the setting's America may have bought, 1900–1940

  • Lane: M10 — the British, French and Dutch Caribbean (and their mainland colonies and outliers): the real record of each, 1900–1940, its folklore (labelled and dated), and what changes if the flag changed.
  • Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent) · Brief: BRIEF.md in this folder + the Chicago sweep's BRIEF.md (entry format, sourcing rules, hard rules).
  • Setting sources read first: ALTERNATE_HISTORY.md §0 (status table), §8 (esp. §8.3–8.4 the fire sale, §8.6 still open), §9 (the Havana-centred circle), §29.3–29.4 (the Dutch correction, held by the ledger), §30.7–30.9 (who lost; Britain's empire after defeat); UMBRA_RULEBOOK.md §31 hemisphere row and Appendix 10 ("The Caribbean is not [ruled]").

How to read the divergence lines. The setting says America "bought [an empire] at a fire sale from the losers of a war it sat out" (§31) — but which colonies were bought, which leased and which taken is OPEN (§8.6, Appendix 10), and this file does not settle it. Three fixed points constrain the proposals below: (1) Britain and France are defeated, bankrupt reparation-payers (§30.7), so their colonies are for sale; (2) the Netherlands was neutral and "sold leases, concessions and distressed companies, not islands" (§29.4(6), delegated) — and §30.2 puts the Netherlands inside Germany's Mitteleuropa economic orbit, so the Dutch Caribbean is where Germany's reach touches America's lake; (3) before 1916 everything happened as recorded. Each entry's divergence line says pre-1916, happened, HOLDS (a colonial-era event that works under either flag) or BENDS (and how). Where an entry proposes a status for a colony it is labelled proposal — historical sense and it is only a proposal.

Overview. 43 entries across the British, French and Dutch Caribbean. The headline is that the setting's fire sale has a real paper trail: American naval writers named Bermuda and Trinidad as the price of the war debts in 1932; a senator ran an "Islands for War Debts Committee" in 1938–40 on a German agent's money; the 1940 destroyers-for-bases deal wrote the exact form of a 99-year lease, and the 1940 Act of Havana — signed in the Golden Circle's own city — wrote the legal form of taking a European colony "provisionally". Beneath that, each colony has a strong record of its own for the decade and its living memory: Pelée's 28,000 dead (1902) and its reawakening (1929–32); Kingston's earthquake and the day London apologised to American sailors (1907); Nassau and Saint-Pierre as Prohibition's warehouses (the Chicago link); the bagne and Dry Guillotine; Urbina's kidnapping of Curaçao's governor on an American ship (1929); a Chicago oil company owning Aruba's refinery (1929–32); the labour risings of 1935–38 and the colonial bans on Obeah, the Spiritual Baptists and Winti. The islands' own monsters — soucouyant, loogaroo, Ol' Higue, La Diablesse, the Rolling Calf, the chickcharney — are labelled and dated. Each colony gets a status proposal (historical sense) and the file ends with a summary table; §8.6 stays open.

Part A — The real record of buying, leasing and taking (the anchors §8.6 can stand on)

"Islands for war debts" — the real American idea, 1932 and 1938–1940

  • Where: Washington (the Senate; the US Naval Institute's Proceedings, Annapolis); London (the Foreign Office).
  • What happened: The idea the setting runs on was real and was argued aloud. In May 1932 the Naval Institute's Proceedings (vol. 58/5/351) ran "Some Naval Aspects of the War Debt Question", arguing that if the war debts were to be reduced again, Washington should remember that the debtors held "territorial assets" not indispensable to them — naming Bermuda and Trinidad in the Atlantic and the French Marquesas in the Pacific, valuable not for trade (only Trinidad's "moderate oil") but as bases. In 1939–40 ⟨fixed: V_M10 — the entry said "1938–40"; Lundeen floated seizing the British West Indies in October 1939 and the committee dates from 1939, when Viereck paid its treasurer Prescott Dennett; 1938 is the year the Foreign Office began weighing the colonies, per Whitham below⟩ Senator Ernest Lundeen (Farmer-Labor, Minnesota) founded and chaired the Make Europe Pay War Debts Committee, later called the Islands for War Debts Committee, which urged the seizure of the British West Indies to settle Britain's Great War debt. The German agent George Sylvester Viereck provided substantial financial support to Lundeen (Wikipedia; the Star Tribune says the German embassy could readily supply the cash), used his office and dictated speeches that Lundeen's staff franked out in huge numbers ⟨fixed: V_M10 — "by the million" is Viereck's whole franking scheme; one Lundeen speech went to 125,000 households⟩. Meanwhile, per the historian Charlie Whitham, the British Foreign Office from July 1938 was itself weighing the West Indian colonies as bargaining counters for American goodwill. Lundeen died on 31 August 1940 when Pennsylvania Central Airlines Flight 19 went down near Lovettsville, Virginia (25 dead) — with an FBI special agent, a second FBI employee and a Justice Department prosecutor aboard; Hoover denied they were watching him.
  • The odd detail: the man who campaigned loudest for taking Britain's islands was on a German agent's payroll — and died in a plane that also carried an FBI special agent, a second FBI employee and a Justice Department prosecutor ⟨fixed: V_M10 — "full of federal investigators" overstated three of 25 aboard⟩ two days before the destroyers-for-bases deal was signed.
  • People: Ernest Lundeen — senator, the committee's chair; George Sylvester Viereck — German propagandist, his paymaster; Charlie Whitham — historian ("Sore Thumbs and Beachcombers", Journal of Imperial and Commonwealth History 25:3, 1997).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Lundeen ; https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1932/may/some-naval-aspects-war-debt-question (search-summary only; the page refused a direct fetch) ; https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14794012.2013.814382 (abstract, citing Whitham 1997).
  • Confidence: High for Lundeen, the committee, Viereck and the crash (Wikipedia, cited). Medium for the 1932 article's wording (search summary, not a full read). Whitham's detail not read — Medium.
  • Divergence check: BENDS, and it is the key anchor. In the record this idea stayed a pamphlet because Britain was a creditor-ally with a fleet. In the setting Britain is a defeated reparation-payer to Germany with American dollar debts unpaid (§30.1) — the pamphlet becomes policy. The Viereck twist also inverts: here Germany is Britain's creditor and has reason to prefer that Britain pay Berlin in cash raised by selling islands to Washington.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Islands for debts" as the setting's public slogan of the fire sale — the phrase in every 1920s newspaper. The Golden Circle does not need to invent the policy; it only needs a Lundeen in every Congress. The dead-senator-in-a-plane-full-of-agents shape is ready for FBI Section 13.

Destroyers for bases — 2 September 1940 (the real "leased")

  • Where: Washington and London; the leased sites in Newfoundland, Bermuda (Great Sound and Castle Harbour), the eastern Bahamas, southern Jamaica, western St Lucia, west Trinidad (Gulf of Paria), Antigua, and British Guiana (within 50 miles of Georgetown).
  • What happened: On 2 September 1940 the United States gave Britain 50 old destroyers (3 Caldwell, 27 Wickes, 20 Clemson class) in exchange for 99-year, rent-free leases on base sites across the British Atlantic and Caribbean. The Bermuda and Newfoundland leases were given as outright gifts, "no destroyers … received in exchange"; the Caribbean sites were the price of the ships. Churchill had refused base-lease proposals in May 1940 "unless Britain received something immediate in return".
  • The odd detail: the real "lease" is exact to the setting's vocabulary — 99 years, rent-free, a foreign flag still over the island, an American base inside it.
  • People: Franklin Roosevelt; Winston Churchill.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destroyers-for-bases_deal
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the record's deal belongs to 1940 and a second war that the setting does not have. What survives is the form: a 99-year lease is the real template for the "leased" column of §8.6, and the list of sites is the real American naval planners' short list of what mattered in the British Caribbean.
  • Proposal — historical sense: where §8.6 needs "leased", these eight sites are the ones: a Trinidad base on the Gulf of Paria, a Jamaica base on the south coast, Antigua, St Lucia, Georgetown, the Bahamas, Bermuda. Each lease can have been signed around 1919–21, when a broke Britain needed dollars and before any sale.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a leased base is a small American town inside a British colony — a Marine, a chaplain, a bar, a local woman, an obeah case the provost marshal cannot file. Jurisdiction is the investigation.

The Act of Havana — 21–30 July 1940 (the real "taken", provisionally)

  • Where: Havana, Cuba — the centre of the Golden Circle's map (§9).
  • What happened: With France and the Netherlands fallen to Germany in May–June 1940, Cordell Hull called the foreign ministers of the American republics to Havana (the Second Meeting of Consultation, 21–30 July 1940). The Act of Havana provided that European colonies in the Americas in danger of changing hands would be put under a provisional inter-American administration ("Pan-American trusteeship") until the war ended, then made independent or returned; and that if the danger was urgent, any one American state could act alone before the machinery existed. It rested on the old American "No Transfer" principle: no colony in this hemisphere may pass from one European power to another.
  • The odd detail: the legal instrument for taking Europe's Caribbean colonies was signed in Havana.
  • People: Cordell Hull — Secretary of State, led the US delegation.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havana_Conference_(1940) ; https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/havana-meeting-1940 (search summary)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the 1940 trigger (a German occupation of France and the Netherlands) does not exist in the setting. But the doctrine does: No Transfer (1811) is pre-divergence, and in the setting the fear is sharper — a victorious Germany whose customs union takes in the Netherlands (§30.2) could claim Dutch colonial assets by the ledger. This is the real legal shape of "taken": a trusteeship to stop the Germans getting it.
  • Proposal — historical sense: if §8.6 wants its "one ugly exception", the Havana form fits: an American trusteeship declared over a colony that would not sell (the likeliest candidates are French Guiana or a Dutch island — see Parts F and G), justified by No Transfer against a German claim.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a 1920s "Havana Protocol" in the setting, signed in the Golden Circle's own city, used to take a colony — the Circle closing its circle with a Pan-American fig leaf.

Admiral Robert and the Bank of France's gold at Martinique — 1940–1943

  • Where: Fort-de-France, Martinique (the gold was held at Fort Desaix — UNVERIFIED in what I read); Guadeloupe; French Guiana.
  • What happened: Admiral Georges Robert, Vichy's high commissioner for the French Antilles, held 286 tons of Bank of France gold, landed from the cruiser Émile Bertin on 24 June 1940, together with the aircraft carrier Béarn (carrying 104 aircraft that had been bound for Canada), the cruiser Jeanne d'Arc and auxiliaries. He bargained with Washington — supplies for neutrality — while the US Navy watched and planned for seizure. In April 1943 the US cut off supplies; on 24 June 1943 pro-Free French crowds rose in Fort-de-France, the garrison at Balata mutinied on 29 June, and Robert left for the United States on 30 June.
  • The odd detail: a French empire's gold sat for three years on a volcanic island forty miles from the Pelée ruins, guarded by a carrier whose planes rotted on deck.
  • People: Adm. Georges Robert — Vichy commander; Henri Hoppenot — Free French successor (15 July 1943).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Robert_(admiral)
  • Confidence: High for figures and dates from the page; "planes rotted" is period colour, UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — no Second World War, no Vichy. But it is the real record of how close the United States came to taking a French Caribbean colony, and of what a broke France's Caribbean was worth in 1940: a vault.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): in the setting, France's reparations to Germany are paid in gold; a French colony is where gold waits between masters. A shipment that should have gone to Berlin and was "lost" in Fort-de-France is a heist, an audit and a haunting in one.

Part B — Jamaica

The Kingston earthquake and the American sailors — 14–19 January 1907 (living memory)

  • Where: Kingston, Jamaica; the harbour; Port Royal.
  • What happened: At about 3:30 p.m. on 14 January 1907 a magnitude-6.2 earthquake damaged every building in Kingston; fires burned three hours before anyone could check them, gutting the business and warehouse districts; about 1,000 died and a small tsunami followed (the sea drew back ~80 m at Annotto Bay). Among the dead: Sir James Fergusson, a former colonial governor, and the cricketer Bertie Verley. Rear-Admiral Charles H. Davis Jr. came from Guantánamo with the torpedo boat Whipple and the battleships Indiana and Missouri and landed men and supplies — with President Theodore Roosevelt's approval and without going through the governor. Governor Sir Alexander Swettenham refused American surgeons and asked the Americans to leave; American papers erupted on 21 January; Whitehall apologised to Washington, and Swettenham resigned, his career over (he died in Switzerland in 1933). A formal letter of 19 January from him thanking Davis survives in Foreign Relations.
  • The odd detail: the first time American sailors landed uninvited on a British Caribbean island in the new century, London apologised to Washington — and sacked its own governor.
  • People: Sir Alexander Swettenham — governor; Rear-Adm. Charles H. Davis Jr. — US squadron; Theodore Roosevelt — approved the landing; Sir James Fergusson — killed.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1907_Kingston_earthquake ; http://dustyheaps.blogspot.com/2014/05/the-kingston-incident.html ; https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1907p1/d439
  • Confidence: High for the quake and the incident's outline; the wording of Swettenham's rude letter (often retold with a sarcastic "burglary" comparison) UNVERIFIED — I did not read it.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened — and a precedent the setting can lean on: London had already once backed down to American sailors in Kingston.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Hunter Guild's founder approved the landing; a Guild man who went ashore with Davis's party in the ruins, and what the fires uncovered under the warehouses. For a 1930s Jamaican the quake is a parent's memory.

Alexander Bedward, the flying prophet of August Town — 1889–1930

  • Where: August Town (Augustown), on the Hope River, St Andrew, just outside Kingston; the Bellevue asylum.
  • What happened: Bedward (born 1848) led the Jamaica Native Baptist Free Church from 1889, baptising and healing in the Hope River; at his height perhaps 30,000 followed him. In 1895 he was tried for sedition for preaching that "there is a white wall and a black wall … the black wall has become bigger than the white," and was sectioned to the asylum, then freed by a lawyer. On 31 December 1920 he announced the Lord had called him to fly up to Heaven, with his followers to follow — and set the hour at 10 a.m., then 3 p.m., then 10 p.m. He stayed on the ground. In 1921 he marched on Kingston with some 800 followers, was arrested and committed to the asylum again; he died there on 8 November 1930.
  • The odd detail: three appointed hours for the ascension, and a crowd that waited through all three.
  • People: Alexander Bedward — Revivalist prophet; his followers ("Bedwardites").
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Bedward
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: HOLDS through 1920–21 (a colonial police matter under any flag). If Jamaica is American by then, the 1921 march meets American police and the asylum is an American institution — the period's American framing would read him as a Garvey-type agitator.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): three layers exactly: the Crown called it madness; his people called it prophecy deferred; and the files ask what answered in the Hope River. Respect: Revivalism is a living faith — the overlay is what came to the river, not the faith.

Marcus Garvey is deported home — 18 November – 10 December 1927

  • Where: Atlanta penitentiary → New Orleans (Esplanade Avenue wharf area — UNVERIFIED) → Cristóbal, Canal Zone → Kingston; then Kingston and St Andrew politics; London from 1935.
  • What happened: Garvey (born 17 August 1887, St Ann's Bay), founder of the UNIA and the Black Star Line, was convicted of mail fraud in 1923 and sent to Atlanta. His sentence was commuted on 18 November 1927 and he was deported: on 2 December 1927 he spoke a farewell from the deck of the United Fruit steamer SS Saramacca in New Orleans and sailed at 12:15 p.m. for Cristóbal and Jamaica. Kingston met him with bands and addresses at the Ward Theatre. In Jamaica he founded the People's Political Party (1929), sat briefly on the Kingston and St Andrew council, and was jailed for contempt the same year; he left for London in 1935 and died there on 10 June 1940.
  • The odd detail: America's most watched Black leader was shipped out on a fruit-company banana boat by way of the Canal Zone.
  • People: Marcus Garvey — UNIA; Calvin Coolidge — commuted the sentence (real record).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Garvey ; https://neworleanshistorical.org/items/show/1445 (search summary) ; https://blackquotidian.supdigital.org/bq/december-10-1927 (search summary)
  • Confidence: High for the dates of commutation and sailing (two independent pages, via search summary); High for the biography.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Coolidge is never president in the setting. In November 1927 the president is Charles Dawes (Wood died August 1927). So a Chicago banker signs Garvey's commutation and deportation. The Bureau case against Garvey (1919–23) ran in the years K. Edgar Hoover rose to Director (c. 1924). And if Jamaica is American by 1927, "deportation" is impossible — Garvey would be a colonial subject shipped home inside the same empire, or exiled elsewhere; if Jamaica is still British (proposal below), the record holds as written.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): FBI Section 13's oldest file on a Black leader is a Garvey file; what the Bureau feared was politics, what one agent noted was something else at the Liberty Hall meetings. Chicago link: UNIA was strong on the South Side (see R14).

Leonard Howell and the first Rastafari sedition trial — Dec 1932 – 1940

  • Where: Kingston and St Thomas parish (Seaforth, Trinityville — UNVERIFIED); prison at Spanish Town; Pinnacle, St Catherine (1940).
  • What happened: Leonard Percival Howell (born 16 June 1898, Clarendon) had sailed to Panama and New York, joined Garvey's UNIA in Harlem, was jailed, and was deported to Jamaica, arriving in December 1932. From January 1933 he preached that the crowning of Ras Tafari as Emperor Haile Selassie I (November 1930) was the sign for the African diaspora — the Messiah returned. On 13 March 1934 he was tried for sedition and sentenced to two years. In prison he wrote The Promised Key under the pen-name "G. G. Maragh" (Gangun Guru), calling the Pope "Satan the Devil". In 1940 he founded Pinnacle, a self-sustaining commune in St Catherine.
  • The odd detail: the Crown's charge against a man preaching a living emperor's divinity was "hatred toward the government" — the emperor himself was never asked.
  • People: Leonard Howell — Rastafari founder; Haile Selassie I — emperor of Ethiopia.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Howell
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS lightly — Haile Selassie's coronation (1930) is Ethiopian and unaffected by the war; Howell's deportation from the US (1932) and trial (1934) hold if Jamaica is British. Note: Italy is a loser in the setting (§30.5) — the 1935 Italian invasion of Ethiopia, which made Rastafari urgent, may not happen, or happens from a poorer, angrier Italy. Flag, don't settle.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Respect first — Rastafari is a living faith. The overlay is not the faith but the enemies it names: a Pope called Satan in 1934 is a line IHS would read in its own files, and would be wrong about.

Jamaica's Obeah Law — 1898, and the de Laurence books banned — 1940

  • Where: Jamaica (all parishes; the resident magistrates' courts).
  • What happened: The Obeah Law of 1898 made it a crime to practise obeah, to consult a practitioner, and to publish obeah material; it defined a practitioner as anyone who "uses, or pretends to use any occult means, or pretends to possess any supernatural power or knowledge" for gain or to frighten; possession of "instruments of obeah" was presumed practice. Penalties: up to 12 months' hard labour and flogging (up to 18 lashes; women exempt), six months and whipping for the client, £20 or (in default) up to six months for publishing ⟨fixed: V_M10 — the entry said "£50 or a year"; Obeah Histories' text of s. 10 says "not exceeding Twenty Pounds, or in default… not exceeding six months"⟩. It also folded Myalism into obeah by definition. In 1940, under wartime emergency controls, Jamaica banned a list of books — mostly political — that included the Chicago mail-order occult house of L. W. de Laurence, whose name had become island shorthand for obeah; the de Laurence ban outlived the others and still stands.
  • The odd detail: the grimoire the Crown feared most in Jamaica was printed on Chicago's Michigan Avenue (see R05) — a 1930s Chicago book in a Kingston courtroom.
  • People: L. W. de Laurence — Chicago occult publisher (d. Chicago 11 Sep 1936, per R05).
  • Sources: https://obeahhistories.org/1898-jamaica-law/ ; https://obeahhistories.org/daniel-young-trinidad-1931/ (the de Laurence context) ; search summary of the Jamaica Observer's "Obeah, de Laurence and Jamaican mysticism" (2023).
  • Confidence: High for the 1898 law; Medium for the 1940 list's details (search summary). (Also in R05: de Laurence.)
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (the law); the 1940 ban BENDS — it was a Second World War measure; in the setting a ban on de Laurence could come from whichever flag is over Kingston, and an American Jamaica would be banning an American publisher's books.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Chicago–Kingston book route — de Laurence's mail-order catalogue as the thread from a Michigan Avenue warehouse to an obeah trial in St Elizabeth. Three layers: the law says fraud; the people say power; the files say some of the books work.

Duppies and the Rolling Calf — folklore, first printed 1907 and 1929

  • Where: Jamaica, island-wide; roadsides, cotton trees, graveyards.
  • What happened (folklore, labelled): In Jamaican belief a duppy is a soul of the dead that can take many shapes. Walter Jekyll's Jamaican Song and Story (1907; the lines below are in Alice Werner's Introduction, pp. xlvii–xlix, ⟨fixed: V_M10⟩) records duppies as "the souls of the dead, capable of assuming various forms of men and other animals"; warns that whistling at night lets a duppy "catch your voice"; and names the monstrous forms — the Rolling Calf, the three-foot horse, long-bubby Susan — calling the Rolling Calf "the spirit of a person not good enough for heaven or bad enough for hell". Later tellings (Martha Beckwith, Black Roadways, 1929) give it fiery eyes and a dragging chain, make it the ghost of butchers, murderers and obeah-men, and say it fears the moon; obeah-men can set one on a man. Related figures: Old Hige (the Jamaican skin-shedding witch, cousin of the soucouyant) and River Mumma.
  • The odd detail: you hear a Rolling Calf before you see it — the chain on the road.
  • People: Walter Jekyll — collector (1907); Martha Warren Beckwith — folklorist (1929); Zora Neale Hurston — Tell My Horse (1938), Jamaica and Haiti.
  • Sources: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/35410/35410-h/35410-h.htm (Jekyll, read) ; https://abookofcreatures.com/2017/05/29/rolling-calf/ (search summary, citing Beckwith)
  • Confidence: High for Jekyll 1907 (read); Medium for Beckwith's specifics (secondary summary).
  • Divergence check: folklore — holds under any flag.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Rolling Calf case in an American-garrisoned Kingston: a butcher who supplied the base's mess, dead, and a Marine sentry who heard a chain on the parade ground. The setting's rule that a thing dismissed as superstition may be real.

The 1938 labour rebellion — Frome and Kingston, April–June 1938

  • Where: Frome sugar estate, Westmoreland (the West Indies Sugar Company, a Tate & Lyle subsidiary — UNVERIFIED in what I read); the Kingston waterfront; island-wide.
  • What happened: The largest of the British West Indian risings of 1934–39. A strike at the new Frome central factory spread in May–June 1938 into general strikes across the island; 46 people died across the disturbances. Alexander Bustamante was jailed; Norman Manley helped settle the strike. The two cousins' parties (Bustamante's union and JLP; Manley's PNP, 1938) came out of it. London answered all the risings with the West India Royal Commission (the Moyne Commission), which sat November 1938 – February 1939; its report was held back until 1945 so the enemy could not use it.
  • The odd detail: the empire suppressed its own report on the Caribbean for six years because the truth in it was useful to Germany.
  • People: Alexander Bustamante — labour leader, jailed (17 months per the page); Norman Manley — barrister, PNP founder; Lord Moyne — commission chair.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_West_Indian_labour_unrest_of_1934%E2%80%931939
  • Confidence: High for the outline and the death toll as the page gives it; Frome's ownership UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: HOLDS if Jamaica is still British — and the Depression that drove it is canon (caused differently). BENDS if Jamaica is American: the rebellion becomes a rising against an American sugar company and an American governor, and the commission is a Senate hearing.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "Germany as the patron of other people's revolutions" — in the setting Germany has every reason to fund a Jamaican union against a broke Britain or a new America. The Moyne report's suppression is a ready file: what did the commissioners write about that was never printed?

Jamaica — status proposal (historical sense, not a ruling): the largest and most populous British island and the hardest to sell — a colony of a million people with its own Legislative Council, a Kingston merchant class and the empire's Caribbean garrison. Proposal: still British, with an American naval lease on the south coast (Portland Bight / Old Harbour — the site the 1940 deal chose) from c. 1920. Keeping Jamaica British keeps Garvey's deportation (1927), Howell's trial (1934) and the 1938 rising as recorded, and makes Kingston the setting's "foreign port inside the American lake". The alternative (bought outright) is noted above entry by entry.

Part C — Trinidad and Tobago

Uriah Butler and the Fyzabad oil-field rising — 19 June 1937

  • Where: Fyzabad, south Trinidad (the junction now called Charlie King Junction); the oilfields (Roodal, Apex, Forest Reserve); then Port of Spain's railways, Sangre Grande and the sugar belt.
  • What happened: Tubal Uriah "Buzz" Butler (born St George's, Grenada, 21 January 1897; British West Indies Regiment 1914–18 in Egypt; a pipe-fitter at the Roodal oilfields from 1921, and a Spiritual Baptist preacher) led a strike that began 19 June 1937. Police tried to arrest him at a meeting in Fyzabad; Corporal Charlie King chased into a shop, was thrown from its window, broke his leg, and was doused with kerosene and set alight by the crowd. Sub-Inspector Bradburn was also killed; nine civilians died and fifty were wounded as the strike spread island-wide. Butler went into hiding; promised safe passage to testify before the inquiry, he was arrested when he came out and held from 9 September 1937 to May 1939 — then detained again for the whole of the Second World War.
  • The odd detail: the strike leader was a preacher of a faith that was illegal in Trinidad at the time (next entry).
  • People: T. U. B. Butler — labour leader and preacher; Cpl Charlie King — police, killed; Sub-Insp. Bradburn — police, killed.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tubal_Uriah_Butler ; https://nationaltrust.tt/home/locales-of-the-1937-labour-revolution/ (search summary)
  • Confidence: High for Butler and the date; the manner of King's death and the casualty figures from search summaries of the National Trust and press pages — Medium-High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Butler's war service (Egypt, 1914–18) holds, but in the setting it was service in a losing army. The oil is the question: Trinidad was the British Empire's most important oil colony, which is why it is exactly what the 1932 Proceedings named. If Trinidad is American, the oilfields are American-owned, and Fyzabad 1937 is a strike against an American company and an American police force.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a burned policeman at a crossroads that still bears his name — a revenant hook the island already set up. Germany as patron: who paid for the strike leaflets?

The Shouters Prohibition Ordinance — 16 November 1917 to 30 March 1951

  • Where: Trinidad and Tobago (the ordinance) — the Spiritual ("Shouter") Baptist churches of Port of Spain, the south and Tobago.
  • What happened: On 16 November 1917 the colonial government outlawed the Spiritual Baptist faith (passed 16 November; the Obeah Histories text gives "Commencement 28th November, 1917" ⟨fixed: V_M10⟩). The ordinance forbade, among other things, ringing bells, holding lit candles in public places and "shaking" of any sort; taking part in a Shouter service, lending a building for one, or obstructing the police carried a $240 fine. The stated reason was noise and "unseemly" worship — singing, bell-ringing, dancing, trembling, falling. It stood for 34 years, until 30 March 1951; since 1996 the date has been a national holiday, Spiritual Baptist Liberation Day. Obeah Histories documents prosecutions from 1918 ("Teacher Bailey").
  • The odd detail: a bell and a candle were contraband in a Christian colony.
  • People: "Teacher" Bailey — prosecuted 1918 (Obeah Histories); Uriah Butler — a Spiritual Baptist preacher.
  • Sources: https://obeahhistories.org/shouters-prohibition-ordinance/ (search summary) ; https://www.nalis.gov.tt/Research/Subject-Guide/Baptist-Liberation-Day (search summary)
  • Confidence: High (dates and provisions from two official/academic summaries).
  • Divergence check: BENDS on its date — November 1917 falls after the divergence. The ordinance was a local colonial act, not a war measure, so it can stand; if the island passed to America afterwards, the new owner inherits a law banning a Protestant church — which an American governor might drop (First Amendment habits) or keep (the period's racial fear).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a banned church meeting in a cane-field at night, a police raid, and something in the "mourning" room the police weren't looking for. Respect: the overlay is never the faith; it is what hunts the faithful when they cannot call the police.

Daniel Young, the Vincentian "obeah man" of Petit Bourg — 1931

  • Where: Petit Bourg, San Juan, Trinidad.
  • What happened: In a 1931 raid police arrested Daniel Young, a migrant from St Vincent, charged with "obtaining money by the assumption of supernatural powers" — the police used two decoys, Rufus Headley and a woman named Soomaria. They found him working by candlelight with a quatro and a guitar, and seized bottles of red liquid, a Bible, a de Laurence edition of The Sixth and Seventh Books of Moses, and letters from clients all over Trinidad — many of them oil-field workers. Over 46,000 migrants lived in Trinidad in the early 1930s, most in the oil industry.
  • The odd detail: his client list was an oil-field payroll.
  • People: Daniel Young — accused; Rufus Headley and Soomaria — police decoys.
  • Sources: https://obeahhistories.org/daniel-young-trinidad-1931/
  • Confidence: High for the facts given; the verdict and sentence were not stated on the page.
  • Divergence check: HOLDS under either flag (an ordinary colonial prosecution).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the letters are the gift: seize a practitioner's correspondence and you have a map of who in the oilfields was afraid of what in 1931. A Chicago book (de Laurence again) at the bottom of a Trinidad evidence box.

The Pitch Lake at La Brea — the hummingbird legend and an American monopoly (1887–1929)

  • Where: La Brea, south-west Trinidad.
  • What happened: The world's largest natural asphalt deposit: about 100 acres, a reported 76 m deep, some 10 million tons of bitumen. Walter Raleigh caulked his ships with it in 1595. In 1887 the American Amzi Barber — "the Asphalt King" — won a 42-year monopoly for his Barber Asphalt Paving Company; Trinidad pitch paved early streets in New York and Washington. Legend (Amerindian, labelled): the Chaima people ate a sacred hummingbird, and their winged god opened the earth and swallowed the village; the lake is the grave.
  • The odd detail: an American company held the concession on a British island's strangest natural feature for forty-two years before any flag changed — the "concession, not island" model the setting assigns to the Dutch, already operating in Trinidad.
  • People: Amzi L. Barber — the Asphalt King; Sir Walter Raleigh.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_Lake
  • Confidence: High for the facts; the legend's first attestation not established this session (later tellings attribute it to the Chaima — Medium).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (the concession) — and a real precedent for American economic ownership inside a British colony.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): things rise out of the Pitch Lake — tree-trunks, bones. Something from the swallowed village surfacing in 1929, the year the American monopoly ends.

The soucouyant, the Ol' Higue and the loogaroo — folklore of the southern islands

  • Where: Trinidad (soucouyant; lagahoo), Dominica, St Lucia, Grenada (soukouyan), Guadeloupe and Martinique, Haiti (lougarou), Guyana/Belize/Jamaica (Ol' Higue), Suriname (asema), the Bahamas and Barbados ("hag").
  • What happened (folklore, labelled): By day a reclusive old woman (sometimes a man); by night she strips off her skin, hides it in a mortar (or, in the loogaroo form, under a silk-cotton "Devil tree"), and flies as a ball of fire to suck the blood of sleepers. The loogaroo form has sold her soul to the Devil and must give him blood every night to keep her powers. The remedies: heap rice (or sand) at the door, and she must count it grain by grain until dawn catches her; find the skin and fill it with coarse salt, and she cannot put it back on. "Loogaroo" is from French loup-garou, werewolf; scholars see a European vampire tale fused with West African (Yoruba àjẹ́) witch belief.
  • The odd detail: she is caught by her own compulsion to count.
  • People: Elsie Clews Parsons — collected across the French and English Antilles in the 1920s; Folk-Lore of the Antilles, French and English (American Folk-Lore Society Memoirs 26, vol. 1, 1933).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soucouyant ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loogaroo ; https://archive.org/details/parsons-antilles-1 (catalogue record; text not read)
  • Confidence: High for the shape of the belief; the first attestation is not established — the Parsons volume (1933) is the safe dated source for the 1930s; earlier printings exist but I did not read them.
  • Divergence check: folklore — holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the setting has vampires in high society; the soucouyant is the island's poor vampire — an old woman at the edge of the estate. Cult versus population: the village that salts a neighbour's skin in 1934 may be right, or may be killing a widow.

La Diablesse / La Guiablesse — folklore, first printed by Lafcadio Hearn, 1890

  • Where: Martinique ("La Guiablesse"), Trinidad and Tobago, Grenada, St Lucia, Dominica.
  • What happened (folklore, labelled): A beautiful woman met on a lonely road at dusk — finely dressed, a wide hat or tall turban hiding the face, a long dress hiding that one foot is a cow's hoof (she walks with one foot on the road and the hoof in the grass); she smells of perfume and decay. She leads a man off into the forest or to a precipice and disappears; he is found mad, lost, or dead. Her origin, in later tellings: a woman who sold her soul to the Devil for beauty. Lafcadio Hearn gave her a chapter, "La Guiablesse", in Two Years in the French West Indies (Harper, New York, 1890), from his 1887–89 residence in St Pierre; in his telling, asked where she lives, she answers with a Creole proverb — "Zaffai cabritt pa zaffai lapin" (the goat's business is not the rabbit's).
  • The odd detail: Hearn's St Pierre, where he heard her story, was obliterated twelve years after he printed it (Part F).
  • People: Lafcadio Hearn — writer, later famous for Japanese ghost stories.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Diablesse ; https://www.gutenberg.org/files/6381/6381-h/6381-h.htm (chapter title confirmed; chapter text not read) ; https://tashqueedagg.wordpress.com/2013/05/22/lafcadio-hearn-martinique-sketches-1890/ (search summary)
  • Confidence: High for the legend's shape and Hearn's chapter; the proverb's wording is from a secondary summary — Medium.
  • Divergence check: folklore — holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Hearn as a real bridge — an American-published writer who recorded Martinique's ghosts and then Japan's. A copy of his 1890 book annotated in a 1930s Section 13 hand.

Trinidad — status proposal (historical sense, not a ruling): the British colony the Americans wanted most and said so (1932 Proceedings; 1940 Gulf of Paria base), and the one whose oil made it worth real money. Proposal: bought — the showpiece purchase, c. 1920–22, oil concessions and all; Tobago with it. If instead the owner wants the Americans to hold only a lease, the Gulf of Paria site is the real one.

Part D — Barbados

The Clement Payne deportation and the Barbados rising — 26–29 July 1937

  • Where: Bridgetown — the wharf, the Careenage; then the rural parishes.
  • What happened: Clement Osbourne Payne, a labour organiser who had spent his youth in Barbados, told immigration on arrival (spring 1937) that he was Barbadian; he had in fact been born in Trinidad. The authorities used the lie to deport him to Trinidad on 26 July 1937. His supporters went to the wharf that night; four days of rioting followed through Bridgetown and the countryside: 14 dead, 47 wounded, some 500 arrested. It led to Grantley Adams's Barbados Progressive League (1938) and is now Barbados's National Day of Significance.
  • The odd detail: the rising began not over wages but over a man's birthplace — a paperwork deportation.
  • People: Clement Payne — organiser (later a National Hero); Grantley Adams — lawyer, later premier.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clement_Payne (search summary) ; https://nationnews.com/2019/07/26/from-the-archives-day-bajans-struck-back/ (search summary) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_West_Indian_labour_unrest_of_1934%E2%80%931939
  • Confidence: High for date and toll (several sources agree on 14 dead; the labour-unrest page gives 14–22).
  • Divergence check: HOLDS if Barbados is British. Barbados is the oldest, most self-governing British colony (its own assembly since 1639) — the least saleable island in the empire.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): deportation-by-birthplace is a ready mechanism in an empire of changing flags: when islands change hands, who is a native of which? A Section 13 file on a man who has three birth certificates.

The Chase Vault — Christ Church, Oistins, 1808–1820 (legend in print through the 1930s)

  • Where: the churchyard of Christ Church Parish Church, Oistins, Christ Church, Barbados.
  • What happened (legend, labelled): A family vault in which, the story goes, the lead coffins were found thrown about each time it was opened for a burial — 1808, twice in 1812, 1816, 1819. The vault held Mrs Thomasina Goddard (buried 1807), Ann Maria Chase (2, buried 1808) and Dorcas Chase (12, buried 1812); Colonel Thomas Chase, the father, followed. In 1820 the governor, Lord Combermere, had the door — a stone six or seven men could barely move — cemented by masons and sand strewn on the floor; when it was reopened the coffins were again disturbed and there were no footprints. The bodies were buried elsewhere and the vault left empty and sealed. Modern checks find no support in the burial registers or the newspapers of the day.
  • The odd detail: the vault is still there, still empty — a haunting with the evidence removed.
  • People: Lord Combermere — governor; the Chase family.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chase_Vault
  • Confidence: High that the story exists and is unconfirmed by records; the events themselves — legend.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 legend — holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): three layers exactly — the period's reading (flood, earth tremor, slaves' revenge on a hated master), the legend, and the files. A 1930s investigator asked to reopen it for an American buyer of the churchyard land.

Barbados — status proposal (historical sense, not a ruling): still British. Three centuries of its own assembly, no strategic harbour Washington needed, and a planter class that would fight the sale in Parliament. In the setting it can be the island that refused to be sold — a point of pride and a place for British exiles from the sold islands.

Part E — The Bahamas, Bermuda and the British mainland colonies

Nassau, the rum capital of Prohibition — 1919–1933

  • Where: Nassau, New Providence — the docks, Market Street, the Lucerne Hotel (Frederick Street); West End (Grand Bahama); Bimini and Gun Cay, fifty miles off Florida.
  • What happened: Importing liquor into the Bahamas was legal, and after the colonial government raised its duties in 1919 Nassau became the warehouse of American Prohibition: 914 gallons of Scotch landed in 1919, 386,000 gallons in 1920. Schooners carried it north to "Rum Row" off New York and west to Florida; the Lucerne Hotel was bootleggers' headquarters and held an annual Bootleggers' Ball. The windfall dredged Nassau harbour, resurfaced roads, and paid for a sewer system and a 2,300-volt diesel generator. Gertrude "Cleo" Lythgoe (born Bowling Green, Ohio, 1888) arrived in 1922 as agent for a Scotch house, the only woman with a Bahamas wholesale liquor licence, working from a Market Street warehouse; Bill McCoy ran whisky north in the schooner Arethusa/Tomoka until the cutter Seneca took him on 15 November 1923. Washington asked London to stop the trade; Britain refused. The US pushed the territorial limit out to 12 miles on 21 April 1924.
  • The odd detail: American Prohibition built the modern Nassau — the sewer, the power plant and the harbour were all paid for in duty on whisky bound for Chicago's and New York's speakeasies.
  • People: Gertrude "Cleo" Lythgoe — "the Bahama Queen" (memoir 1964); Bill McCoy — rum-runner, "the real McCoy"; Winston Churchill — called Prohibition "an affront to the whole history of mankind".
  • Sources: https://whiskymag.com/articles/the-toast-of-nassau/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gertrude_Lythgoe ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rum-running
  • Confidence: High for the figures from the Whisky Magazine piece and the Wikipedia pages; the Churchill quotation as given there.
  • Divergence check: BENDS, sharply. Prohibition holds (domestic calendar; repeal Dec 1933). But if the Bahamas are American, Nassau cannot be the legal warehouse — American law would run there. So the rum trade itself is an argument for §8.6: either the Bahamas stay British through the 1920s (and Nassau's boom happens as recorded, with an American lease elsewhere in the islands), or the Americans buy them and the whisky moves to the next foreign port — Havana under occupation, Saint-Pierre, Belize, Curaçao.
  • Proposal — historical sense: the Bahamas stay British until 1933, a thorn in Washington's side, and are bought after repeal — the fire sale's late lot, when a Depression-broke Bay Street has lost its whisky money and the 1929 hurricane (next entry) has wrecked its sponge fleet. That keeps the Chicago link intact for the core book's decade.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Chicago link is direct — the Outfit's Scotch came up through Nassau. A crate that left the Lucerne Hotel's warehouse with something besides whisky in it; the Bootleggers' Ball as a masked gathering where the Golden Circle's Havana men meet the Outfit's buyers.

The great Bahamas hurricane — 22 September – 4 October 1929

  • Where: Nassau and Andros; the sponge grounds; Florida.
  • What happened: A storm that crawled over Nassau and Andros at 2–3 mph and held hurricane force there for about 48 hours (25–26 September 1929), with gusts of 164 mph at Nassau and a surge of 12+ feet reaching 20 miles inland on Andros. It destroyed 73% of Nassau's homes and businesses, left 5,000+ homeless, killed 142 in the Bahamas (35 on the schooner Pretoria off Fresh Creek) and 13 in the US; it wrecked about 70% of the sponging fleet and nearly all the sponge warehouses. A fungus later killed ~99% of the sponges and ended the colony's staple trade. The tanker Potomac logged 924 mb; the storm spawned some of the first verified hurricane tornadoes in Florida.
  • The odd detail: the storm ran south-west, the wrong way, and sat on the colony for two days — the kind of storm people say was sent.
  • People: the sponge fishermen of Andros; the Weather Bureau forecasters who lost it.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1929_Bahamas_hurricane
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: HOLDS (weather reads no treaties) — and it lands a month before the Crash; together with Prohibition's end it is why the Bahamas would be for sale in the 1930s.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Andros's pine forest is the chickcharney's country (next entry); a storm that sat on Andros for two days, and the obeah-men who said who had angered what.

The chickcharney of Andros, and Neville Chamberlain's sisal — folklore, and 1891–97

  • Where: the pine forests of Andros; Mastic Point (the Chamberlain sisal estate — place UNVERIFIED on the page read).
  • What happened (folklore, labelled): The chickcharney is a feathered, red-eyed creature with three toes, a long prehensile tail and a head that turns all the way round; it nests by binding the tops of two pines together, and blesses or curses a traveller by how he treats it. Zoologists tie the tale to the extinct Andros giant barn owl, Tyto pollens. The record: the young Neville Chamberlain spent the 1890s running his father Joseph's sisal plantation on Andros, which failed; the island's joke-legend says he cut down the chickcharnies' trees and they cursed him — down to Munich. Elsie Clews Parsons printed 115 tales from this one island in Folk-Tales of Andros Island, Bahamas (American Folk-Lore Society Memoirs 13, 1918).
  • The odd detail: a British prime minister's first failure was a haunted forest in the Bahamas.
  • People: Neville Chamberlain — sisal planter on Andros in his twenties; Elsie Clews Parsons — folklorist.

Bimini and the Compleat Angler — rum barges to Hemingway, 1924–1937

  • Where: Alice Town, North Bimini; the liquor barges anchored off Gun Cay.
  • What happened: Bimini, fifty miles from Florida, was the last stop for Nassau's liquor: sailing ships brought it from Nassau and transferred it to big barges anchored off Gun Cay as floating warehouses, where American speedboats loaded. After their first house burned on 18 November 1934, Henry and Helen Duncombe built the Compleat Angler hotel (12 rooms and a rowdy bar, finished 1935) out of the timber of a derelict liquor barge, the Dreamland, which had supplied American rum-runners since 1924. Ernest Hemingway stayed there 1935–37, fished from his boat Pilar, and worked on To Have and Have Not (1937) — his Prohibition-smuggling novel.
  • The odd detail: the famous bar was built of a bootleg barge's planks.
  • People: Ernest Hemingway — writer (an Oak Park, Illinois boy); Henry and Helen Duncombe — hoteliers.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compleat_Angler_Hotel (search summary) ; https://soundingsonline.com/voices/angler-burns-11-years-ago-bimini-lost-a-monument-to-hemingway/ (search summary)
  • Confidence: Medium-High (two concordant summaries; the hotel burned in 2006).
  • Divergence check: HOLDS if the Bahamas are British through the mid-1930s (proposal above). Hemingway's biography BENDS only at its war (the setting has no American Great War; his Italian ambulance service of 1918 was Red Cross, and Italy is a loser here — flag).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a hotel built of a ship's timbers carries the ship's dead. Hemingway as a famous visitor: the Chicago-born writer who knew the rum routes, drinking in a bar made of one.

Bermuda — the island that banned the motor car (1908–1946), and Woodrow Wilson's refuge

  • Where: Bermuda — Hamilton, St George's; the Bermuda Railway (1931).
  • What happened: In 1908 the Bermuda Assembly passed the Motor Car Act, banning cars; wealthy American winter visitors had petitioned for it — ⟨fixed: V_M10 — the entry had "Mark Twain and Woodrow Wilson among the 111 signers"; the sources read say the petition (111 visitors) was drafted by Woodrow Wilson, then president of Princeton, on the island in January 1908 and printed in the Royal Gazette on 1 February 1908, with Mark Twain, also on the island, urging it on (FHWA's Weingroff, quoting Hoffman and Forbes; bermudarailway.net); whether Twain signed is not stated⟩ — calling automobiles, in its own words, a danger to attract "the extravagant and sporting set who have made so many other places of pleasure entirely intolerable to persons of taste and cultivation". Bermuda lived on American tourists and was, in the 1932 Proceedings article (Part A), one of the two British Atlantic islands the Navy most wanted; the 1940 deal leased Great Sound and Castle Harbour as a gift.
  • The odd detail: the setting's removed president (§10, Wilson taken out in 1916 "as insane") had helped keep the car off Bermuda — his favourite holiday island.
  • People: Woodrow Wilson — petition drafter; in Bermuda January 1907 and from 20 January 1908 (FHWA), and again as President-elect (1912–13; not detailed); "1908–1910" was UNVERIFIED ⟨fixed: V_M10⟩; Mark Twain — on the island in January 1908.
  • Sources: https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/highwayhistory/wilson.pdf (added by V_M10; read) ; https://www.bermudarailway.net/railway/history/no-motors/ (added by V_M10; read) ; https://www.bermuda.com/do-you-know-about-getting-around-in-bermuda-from-horse-and-carriages-to-automobiles-and-trucks/ (search summary) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transport_in_Bermuda (search summary)
  • Confidence: Medium-High (petition and ban dates from two summaries; I did not read the petition).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (the ban, Wilson's visits); the railway (1931) HOLDS if Bermuda stays British.
  • Proposal — historical sense: Bermuda leased, not bought — a Royal Navy dockyard Britain would not part with, and a colony run by a rich white oligarchy that lived on American visitors and could live under an American naval lease. It was the first site named in 1932 and a gift in 1940.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Wilson's Bermuda — the place the Secret Service files say he went before 1916, and whom he met there. A Bermuda sanatorium where a man "who had a stroke" was said to recover. (Three layers: public, archive, speculation — §9.6.)

USS Cyclops sails from Barbados and vanishes — 4 March 1918

  • Where: from Bridgetown, Barbados, bound for Baltimore; lost somewhere in what later writers called the Bermuda Triangle.
  • What happened: The Navy collier Cyclops, overloaded with 10,800 long tons of manganese ore from Brazil for munitions, made an unscheduled stop at Barbados and sailed on 4 March 1918 with 306 crew and passengers; she was never seen again. Her captain, George Worley, had been born Johan Frederick Wichmann in Germany; crewmen described him chasing an ensign round the deck with a pistol and doing his rounds in long underwear and a derby hat. Aboard as a passenger was the US Consul-General in Rio, Alfred L. M. Gottschalk, said in a contemporary telegram to be as hated for pro-German sympathies as Worley. Theories: a U-boat (Germany denied it), structural failure, a shifted wet cargo; the Navy's historians favour a sudden storm.
  • The odd detail: a German-born captain and a pro-German consul on a ship carrying war metal — and no wreck, no signal, no wreckage.
  • People: Lt Cdr George Worley — captain; Alfred Gottschalk — consul-general.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Cyclops_(AC-4)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — March 1918 is after the break; America is not at war in the setting, so the manganese is commercial cargo (for a neutral America's own steel, or for sale to a belligerent). The vanishing can still happen; the "German sabotage" rumour gets louder in a world where Germany is winning.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the setting's founding sea-mystery for the Caribbean: 306 men gone between a British island and Baltimore, a German-born captain, and a file the Navy never closed. The Golden Circle's map is 1,200 miles around Havana; the ship vanished inside it.

The Belize hurricane on St George's Caye Day — 10 September 1931

President Hoover coordinated relief: a Marine Corps plane flew medical supplies from Managua — from the American occupation of Nicaragua — and USS Swan came from Honduras.
  • The odd detail: the relief came from the Marines next door — the American occupation was the nearest help a British colony had.
  • People: the Jesuits of St John's College (American Missouri Province — UNVERIFIED); the US consul (name not given on the page).

  • Divergence check: HOLDS — and the Managua Marines are the setting's own (Nicaragua occupied "since 1912" and never left). If British Honduras is American by 1931, the storm hits an American colony and the relief is domestic.

British Guiana — the Venezuela crisis (1895–99), Alcoa's bauxite (1916) and the lost constitution (1928)

  • Where: Georgetown; the Cuyuni basin (the disputed border); Mackenzie (later Linden) on the Demerara River; the interior gold and diamond fields.
  • What happened: In 1895 President Cleveland forced Britain to arbitrate Venezuela's claim to the Cuyuni basin under the Monroe Doctrine; the Paris tribunal (1898–99) gave Britain about 94% of the disputed land. From 1913 Alcoa's president Arthur Vining Davis sent the geologist George Bain Mackenzie to buy up bauxite land "under the guise of buying land for a citrus plantation"; in 1916 the Demerara Bauxite Company (DEMBA), an Alcoa subsidiary, built a mining town and a drying plant at Mackenzie. Part F — The French Caribbean: Martinique, Guadeloupe, French Guiana and Saint-Pierre

Mount Pelée destroys Saint-Pierre — 8 May 1902, 8:02 a.m. (living memory)

  • Where: Saint-Pierre, Martinique — then the island's commercial capital, "the Paris of the Antilles"; the roadstead.
  • What happened: After weeks of ash, tremors and warnings, Pelée erupted at about 8:00 a.m. on 8 May 1902 (Ascension Day); a pyroclastic surge reached the city at 8:02 and killed some 28,000–29,000 people in minutes — the deadliest eruption of the twentieth century. An election was due and the city had not been evacuated: Governor Louis Mouttet and his wife stayed and died. Ships in the roadstead burned — the Canadian liner Roraima was reduced to a burning wreck and the cable ship Grappler sank. People had taken the Soufrière's eruption on St Vincent the day before (Part E) as a sign that pressure had been relieved. On 20 May a second blast killed some 2,000 rescuers and salvagers. From October a lava spine — the "Tower of Pelée" — grew up to 15 m a day before collapsing in March 1903.

  • People: Gov. Louis Mouttet — died; Ludger Sylbaris and Léon Compère-Léandre — survivors (next entry).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1902_eruption_of_Mount_Pel%C3%A9e ; Hearn's description of the pre-1902 city, https://www.gutenberg.org/files/6381/6381-h/6381-h.htm ("the quaintest, queerest, and the prettiest withal, among West Indian cities").

Pelée wakes again — the 1929–1932 eruption and Frank Perret's hut

The 1928 hurricane: Guadeloupe first — 12–17 September 1928

The bagne of French Guiana and the Îles du Salut — 1852–1953

  • Where: Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni (the camp de la transportation, reception on the Maroni river border with Dutch Suriname); Cayenne; Kourou; the Îles du Salut — Île Royale (administration and hospital), Île Saint-Joseph (the solitary-confinement réclusion), and Île du Diable (political prisoners).
  • What happened: France transported more than 80,000 convicts to Guiana between 1852 and the last departures; mortality reached as high as 75% in the worst years. Under the doublage rule a convict who finished a sentence of under eight years had to stay in the colony as a libéré for as long again (life, for eight years or more) — free, penniless and unable to leave. Captain Alfred Dreyfus, falsely convicted of spying for Germany, was held on Devil's Island from 1895 to 1899. The journalist Albert Londres went in 1923 and his series in Le Petit Parisien (from 8 August 1923) made the bagne a national scandal; the Salvation Army's Charles Péan worked there from 1928. In 1938 the Daladier government, pushed by Guiana's deputy Gaston Monnerville, ended transportation; the last convicts went home in 1953.
  • The odd detail: the most famous prisoner of the bagne was sent there for betraying France to Germany — and was innocent.
  • People: Alfred Dreyfus — prisoner 1895–99; Albert Londres — reporter (1923); Charles Péan — Salvation Army; Gaston Monnerville — Guiana's deputy.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devil%27s_Island ; https://www.retronews.fr/long-format/2018/03/19/au-bagne-par-albert-londres-en-1923 (search summary)

The 1938 end of transportation depends on a French government with the will and money to reform; in a bankrupt reparation-paying France it could come earlier (a prison colony is a cost) or never (it is cheap). And the Dreyfus Affair reads differently in a world where Germany won — the French army's German-spy panic proved, in a sense, right about the enemy and wrong about the man.
  • Proposal — historical sense: French Guiana not sold — no American Congress will vote dollars for a penal colony, and France will not give up its dumping ground. The bagne is the setting's Château d'If for the Caribbean: who else did France send there that it did not want in Europe?

René Belbenoît escapes and writes Dry Guillotine — 1935–1938

  • Where: Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni → by open boat to Trinidad (17 days) → Colombia → Central America → Los Angeles (March 1937).
  • What happened: Belbenoît, born in Paris on 4 April 1899 and raised partly behind his uncle's café, the Rat Mort, was convicted of thefts and swindles (arrested 21 August 1921) and sentenced to eight years' hard labour with doublage; he landed at Saint-Laurent on 21 June 1923. Saint-Pierre and Miquelon — France's whisky warehouse off Newfoundland, 1920–1933

  • Where: Saint-Pierre, the French islands 20 km off Newfoundland's south coast; the Hôtel Robert.

Shell's Isla refinery at Curaçao — built 1918, the world's largest by 1930

Alcoa in Suriname — Moengo bauxite, from 1916

  • Where: Moengo, on the Cottica river, north-east Suriname.
  • What happened: In 1916 Alcoa set up the Surinaamsche Bauxiet Maatschappij (SBM) to mine bauxite at Moengo (one source dates first mining to 1920, mine construction to 1925). Together with Alcoa's Demerara Bauxite Company next door in British Guiana (same year — Part E), it made the two Guianas the aluminium mines of American industry before any flag changed. The Moengo company town was American-run in a Dutch colony.
  • The odd detail: the same American company, in the same year, took the bauxite of a British and a Dutch colony side by side.
  • People: Arthur Vining Davis — Alcoa (see Part E).
  • Sources: https://eitisuriname.gov.sr/en/about-suriname/history-of-mining-industry/bauxite-mining/ (search summary) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moengo (search summary)
  • Confidence: Medium-High (1916 founding agreed; the start of mining varies by source).
  • Divergence check: holds (1916 is the break year; the concession is Dutch-granted and a neutral's to give) — and it is the setting's Dutch model in action: America owning the concession, not the land.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a mining concession cut into Maroon and Indigenous forest — the Hunter Guild hired to keep the survey crews alive, and what the Maroon villages told them not to dig.

Winti outlawed — Suriname's "idolatry" ban, 1874–1971

  • Where: Suriname — Paramaribo and the plantations; the Maroon interior.
  • What happened: Winti is the Afro-Surinamese religion built from Akan, Fon and Kongo traditions: a creator (Anana Kedyaman Kedyanpon), the winti spirits of earth, water, forest and sky (the forest Ampuku, which can also be water spirits) that possess devotees and speak of past and future, and the ancestors. After emancipation (1863) the colony pushed conversion, and in 1874 its penal code made "idolatry" a crime — Winti was prosecuted under it, with fines and detention for public practice — until 1971. It survived in secret, taught out of sight of the government and the missions. (In 2014 Suriname recognised its first official Winti priest.)
  • The odd detail: for ninety-seven years a possession ceremony in Paramaribo was a police matter.
  • People: — (a religion, not a person).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winti ; https://www.surinameglobalgroup.com/en/erfgoed/winti (search summary)
  • Confidence: High for the dates; details of 1930s prosecutions not found.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (the law) — holds under a Dutch flag through the 1930s. Respect: a living religion; the overlay must never make it the villain.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the three layers again: the Dutch court calls it idolatry, the Moravian mission calls it the Devil, and the files note that the forest spirits named in one 1930s police report were right about the mine (previous entry).

Anton de Kom comes home, and the Oranjeplein shooting — January–May 1933

  • Where: Paramaribo — de Kom's house, where hundreds queued for advice; the Oranjeplein (now Onafhankelijkheidsplein).
  • What happened: Anton de Kom (born Paramaribo 1898, son of a formerly enslaved man) had lived in the Netherlands since 1921 and written for left-wing papers. He came home in January 1933 and opened his door to plantation workers — Creole and Javanese — who came in crowds for advice. He was arrested on 1 February 1933; on 7 February a crowd gathered on the Oranjeplein to demand his release and police opened fire, killing two and wounding 22. He was exiled to the Netherlands in May 1933 and wrote Wij slaven van Suriname (We Slaves of Suriname, 1934), published despite censorship. (He died in a German camp, Stalag X-B / Sandbostel, on 24 April 1945.)
  • The odd detail: a man was exiled for giving free advice from his front room.
  • People: Anton de Kom — writer and activist.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_de_Kom
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: HOLDS through 1934 under a Dutch flag; his 1945 death BENDS — there is no Nazi Germany and no occupation of the Netherlands in the setting. His communist affiliations in the 1920s BEND too (no Soviet Union — his politics would be labour-socialist without Moscow).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "cult versus population" inverted: the crowd on the Oranjeplein was the population, the colonial police the fear. A Section 13 agent in Paramaribo in February 1933 who was not there for de Kom.
