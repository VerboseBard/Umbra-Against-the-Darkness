M08 — The cities and communities of the ceded north, 1900–1940

  • Lane: M08 — who lives in the land the treaty handed to America: Monterrey, Saltillo, the Laguna, Chihuahua, Parral, Hermosillo, Guaymas, Nogales, Cananea, Nacozari, the Mormon colonies, the Mennonites, the Yaqui / Mayo / Rarámuri, the Chinese of the north, and the American mining and ranching barons.
  • Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (research agent) · Brief: BRIEF.md in this folder + the Chicago sweep's BRIEF.md (entry format, sourcing rules).
  • Setting sources read first: ALTERNATE_HISTORY.md §0, §3, §7.4 (the northern-resources table), §8, §29.3–29.4; PEOPLE_OF_THE_TIMELINE.md (Hearst's Babícora; "who governs the ceded north" — open decision 4); M01 and M06 in this folder (skimmed so as not to repeat: Torreón 1911, Bierce, Santa Isabel, Columbus, Carrizal, the Yaqui campaign of 1926–27, Villa's death at Parral, the Escobar rising and Naco bombing, the 1931 Sonora expulsion, Maldonado, the Hearst forgeries — entries here that overlap say (also in M01) and go wider or deeper).

How to read the divergence lines. The setting's treaty (1917) cedes Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila and Nuevo León. Before March 1916 everything happened as recorded. From the Columbus raid on, the Southern War replaces the record; a ceded city's later real history (the Cristero era, the 1930s Cárdenas land reform, the 1938 expropriation) can only happen there as occupation-era events under American law — who governs is still open (PEOPLE open decision 4: a military governor or a civil one; the Insular Cases' category). Every entry says what the cession does to the people named.

Overview (37 entries). The ceded north, on the real record, is not a wasteland with minerals under it: it is a set of finished societies the setting's treaty inherits on 1917's paperwork — a steel city (Monterrey) whose bosses backed Huerta and got their brewery back through diplomats, a copper town (Cananea) whose 1906 strike already had Americans crossing the line to shoot Mexicans, a Chihuahuan estate system (Terrazas–Creel, Hearst's Babícora) that outsizes many U.S. states, and — under all of them — peoples the record barely counts: the Yaqui (never officially surrendered), the Mayo, the Rarámuri, the Chinese of Sonora (6,078 in 1919, 92 in 1940), Okinawan and Japanese coal miners in Coahuila, Mormon colonists from Utah in Chihuahua, Low-German Mennonites from Manitoba. What surprised me most: (1) the north's religion is the richest hook — a folk healer of Nuevo León whom President Calles visited in 1928 (Niño Fidencio), a Sonoran girl whose name was a rebellion (Santa Teresa de Cabora), a saint's reclining statue that would not be moved north (Magdalena), and Calles's first anticlerical experiment was run in Sonora in 1915–16, the state the treaty cedes; (2) the north's people were already crossing the line — Yaqui refugees to Tucson, Chinese trucked to Nogales, Federals marched from Ojinaga to Fort Bliss, Mormon women and children by train to El Paso in 1912 — the treaty erases the line those people were crossing; (3) almost every "oddity" is also a hook for the open question "who governs the ceded north": Enrique Creel (American-blooded Chihuahuan banker, dead 1931), Kosterlitzky (a Russian-born colonel who ended a Bureau agent), and Hearst (a press lord who owns a county) are three different answers. Respect note: the Yaqui, Mayo, Rarámuri, Mormon, Mennonite, Chinese and Fidencista communities are real and, for most, living; the villains of this file are leagues, mobs and the occupation's own paperwork, never the peoples. Part A — Nuevo León: Monterrey, the steel city (and its saint)

The Fundidora — Latin America's first steel mill, incorporated 5 May 1900

  • Where: Monterrey, Nuevo León — the works east of the old centre (now Parque Fundidora).
  • What happened: The Compañía Fundidora de Fierro y Acero de Monterrey was constituted on 5 May 1900, the first integrated iron-and-steel enterprise in Latin America. Vicente Ferrara (the driving spirit), Eugenio Kelly (a US banker), León Signoret (French) and Antonio Basagoiti (Spanish) formalised it; the first board also seated Isaac Garza, José A. Muguerza, Francisco G. Sada, Valentín Rivero y Gajá, Adolfo Zambrano and an Ernesto Madero, with local shareholders in dozens. Wikipedia lists the Milmo, Madero and Garza-Sada families and the Guggenheims among the capital. It made iron, steel, coke and slag: rails, wire rod, structural steel, train wheels. It ran for 86 years (nationalised 1977, bankrupt 9 May 1986, 7,000 workers at the end).
  • The odd detail: four of the men who founded the brewery ten years earlier (see next entry) were on the steelworks' founding roster — beer money built the steel town.
  • People: Vicente Ferrara — promoter; León Signoret — French financier; Eugenio Kelly — American banker; Isaac Garza, José A. Muguerza, Francisco G. Sada — the Monterrey elite; Ernesto Madero — a director (kinship to the Revolution's Madero family plausible, UNVERIFIED that he is the future finance minister).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monterrey_Foundry · https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundidora_de_Fierro_y_Acero_de_Monterrey (via search) · https://www.hcnl.gob.mx/archivo/2013/05/fundidora-de-monterrey.php (via search)
  • Confidence: High on date and founders; Medium on the board list (search summary of the Spanish article).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. CEDED NORTH (Nuevo León) — ALTERNATE_HISTORY §7.4 names the Fundidora ("the only integrated steel mill in Latin America") as one of the war's prizes. After 1917 the owners are a French–Spanish–American–Mexican consortium under a new flag; who now owns the mill is a treaty question.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Monterrey is the Mexican South Chicago: a steel town with a night shift, a brewery, a foreign board and a workers' welfare society. Its bosses are the natural first collaborators of the occupier (compare Peláez in M01). A Chicago steel or union man sent south finds his own city's rules already in place.

The brewery, the glassworks and the Garza-Sada — 8 November 1890 – 1918

  • Where: Monterrey, Nuevo León (Cervecería Cuauhtémoc; Vidriera Monterrey).
  • What happened: The Cervecería Cuauhtémoc was founded on 8 November 1890 by Isaac Garza, José A. Muguerza, Francisco Sada, Joseph M. Schnaider and others, with 150,000 pesos and the Carta Blanca label; its first barrel came in 1893 and it reportedly won first prize at "the Chicago and Paris world fairs." A cardboard works (c. 1900) and a glass-bottle company, Vidrios y Cristales de Monterrey (1909), made the group self-supplying; the working day was cut from twelve to nine hours in 1907 and the Sociedad Cuauhtémoc (1918) gave workers' families medical care and schooling. In the Revolution the founders backed Huerta; Carranza seized the brewery, the families fled to Texas, and they got it back "through the intervention of U.S. and Russian diplomats" (Wikipedia's wording).
  • The odd detail: the Monterrey magnates' route back to their own brewery ran through a Russian diplomat — in a world where, in the setting, Russia never went red and Germany won, whichever embassy that was is a live question. (Source's phrase is unverified; Medium.)
  • People: Isaac Garza — founder; José A. Muguerza — founder; Francisco Sada — founder (the Sada line becomes the Garza-Sada dynasty; Eugenio Garza Sada b. 1892, killed 1973 — outside the window).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuauht%C3%A9moc_Moctezuma_Brewery · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenio_Garza_Sada (via search)
  • Confidence: Medium-High (a single encyclopaedia page for the diplomats and the "Chicago" prize; check the 1893 Columbian Exposition award list before printing).
  • Divergence check: 1890–1913 happened. CEDED NORTH. The 1913 flight to Texas and the return "by diplomats" would, in the setting, be followed by a more decisive episode: the 1917 treaty puts these families under an American governor who needs them.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "no one is clean": paternalist Catholic-liberal industrialists who cut the working day and build worker schools, and who backed the man who murdered Madero. A Chicago brewer of the Prohibition era (repeal Dec 1933) meets a Monterrey brewer; the Golden Circle's map wants both.

The flood that took a quarter of Monterrey — 27–28 August 1909

  • Where: Monterrey, Nuevo León — the Santa Catarina river, the south-bank barrios (San Luisito).
  • What happened: A hurricane that made landfall in Tamaulipas on 27 August 1909 dropped about 17.5 inches (440 mm) of rain in 40 hours; the Santa Catarina, normally 150 yards wide, swelled to three-quarters of a mile and ran at about 20 mph carrying houses. A reservoir dam burst overnight and flooded San Luisito. The 1911 Britannica says the flood swept away about a quarter of the city and drowned 1,200–1,400; Wikipedia's hurricane article has 800 dead in south Monterrey and ~4,000 across the storm's track (some sources higher). Relief did not arrive until 31 August. The dead were cremated and mass-buried; the river bed turned to quicksand-like silt that hampered body recovery; workers rebuilt the railways by mid-September.
  • The odd detail: the steelworks and smelters along the river were reportedly wrecked in the same night — the founding disaster of the industrial city came nine years after its founding.
  • People: —
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1909_Monterrey_hurricane · https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Monterrey (via search) · https://mexicanhinterlands.org/stories/barrio-san-luisito-and-flood-1909 (via search, not read)
  • Confidence: High on the event and the meteorology; Medium on the toll (sources range 800 to 5,000).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. CEDED NORTH — a disaster of living memory (twenty-seven years before a 1936 module) and a physical scar on the city the war later prizes.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): mass graves in a riverbed that later got built over; four blocks that were never rebuilt as they were. A 1930s survivor, a Monterrey-born Chicago steelworker, remembers which barrio went under and who was seen walking out of it.

El Niño Fidencio at Espinazo — 1925 – 19 October 1938

  • Where: Espinazo, Nuevo León (a hacienda and railway halt north of Monterrey).
  • What happened: José de Jesús Fidencio Constantino Síntora (born 17 October 1898) began healing the sick at Espinazo around 1925; by 1927 more than 4,000 people from every class came to his sessions. On 8 February 1928 President Plutarco Elías Calles himself came for a healing (records say Calles had nodular leprosy, unknown to the public). Fidencio became famous for operations without anaesthetic and without pain, threw objects at the crowds from beside a pepper tree and submerged followers in a sulphur-rich mud pool. Doctors opposed him and Nuevo León charged him with illegally practising medicine; authorities called him a fraud. He died on 19 October 1938, two days after his 40th birthday. The Fidencista Christian Church venerates him today; the Catholic Church has never recognised him.
  • The odd detail: the president who shot the priests and closed the churches went in person to a Nuevo León peasant healer, on the same trains the pilgrims used.
  • People: Niño Fidencio — folk healer (a living tradition: report, don't monster); Plutarco Elías Calles — president who consulted him.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni%C3%B1o_Fidencio
  • Confidence: High on the dates and the Calles visit; the leprosy diagnosis is Wikipedia's "records indicate" (Medium).
  • Divergence check: the healing pre-1916 and after: BENDS — Espinazo is in CEDED NORTH, so 1925–38 there happens under the American flag. Calles is a Sonoran (also ceded); whether he would be governing the rump state and could travel to Espinazo across the treaty line is an open question (M01 Part E, question 2). The medical prosecution then belongs to American authority, not Nuevo León's.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): three layers: the period says a medical fraud; the folk say a healer of lepers; the files say something in the mud pool. The GM's cleanest use is the pilgrims' road: thousands of sick people walking north onto a healing site the occupier cannot police, and a president who came in secret. The IHS would have a file; the Church never recognised him — do not make him or his followers the villain.

Part B — Sonora's copper: Cananea, Nacozari and the men who ran them

Cananea, 1 June 1906 — the strike the Arizona Rangers crossed the line for

  • Where: Cananea, Sonora — the Cananea Consolidated Copper Company's lumberyard and the town, ~40 miles below Naco/Bisbee, Arizona.
  • What happened: On 1 June 1906 Mexican miners struck over piecework and an unequal wage: American workers earned about $5 a day, Mexicans a maximum of $3. The company employed ~5,360 Mexicans and 2,200 Americans in a town of about 23,000 that Col. William C. Greene controlled outright (food, banking, water, land). The walkout began in the lumberyard and ended in gunfire; Wikipedia gives ~33 workers dead and 22 wounded (other sources: "more than 20 miners and six company men"). Greene telegraphed Arizona and 275 volunteers from Bisbee, Douglas and Naco under Capt. Thomas H. Rynning of the Arizona Rangers crossed into Mexico, against the orders of Arizona's governor; Sonora's Governor Rafael Izábal accepted them and sent forty Rurales from Hermosillo; Col. Emilio Kosterlitzky (next entry) made the Americans surrender their weapons before he took control. Leaders of the Partido Liberal Mexicano — Juan José Ríos, Manuel M. Diéguez, Esteban Baca Calderón — were blamed and sentenced to up to fifteen years, released May 1911 after Díaz fell. The strike ended 6 June.
  • The odd detail: the first time American armed men crossed the line to put down a Mexican strike [UNVERIFIED — V_M08: "first" is not in any source read; Wikipedia confirms only that the 275 crossed "against the orders" of Arizona's Governor Kibbey at Greene's telegraphed plea] was at the invitation of a Sonoran governor, and it was the Russian-born Mexican colonel who disarmed them. The affair helped convince Mexico that Díaz was selling the country to foreigners.
  • People: William Cornell Greene — owner; Thomas H. Rynning — Rangers captain; Rafael Izábal — governor of Sonora; Emilio Kosterlitzky — Rurales commander; Ríos / Diéguez / Baca Calderón — PLM strike leaders.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cananea_strike · https://bisbeehistory.substack.com/p/the-cananea-strike-of-1906-the-birth (via search) · https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/cananea-strike (via search)
  • Confidence: High on date, wages, Rynning and the leaders; Medium on the death toll (sources range from ~26 to 33+).
  • Divergence check: 1906, happened. CEDED NORTH (Sonora) — ALTERNATE_HISTORY §7.4 names Cananea's copper (Greene/Anaconda) as the first prize. After 1917 there is no line to cross: the Bisbee vigilantes and the Rurales are under one flag, and 1906 becomes the precedent for how the occupation deals with strikers.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): copper is the war metal (shell casings, cartridge brass, wire). A Cananea widow's son in a Chicago packinghouse; a PLM organiser's file in Section 13's basement; and an Arizona Rangers roll that lists the 275. Three layers: the period called it a riot; the miners called it a massacre; the files ask who paid for the telegram.

Colonel Greene, the copper king and his rancho — 1899 – 5 August 1911

  • Where: Cananea, Sonora; the San Rafael Ranch near Lochiel, Arizona; Chihuahua and Sonora ranch lands.
  • What happened: William Cornell Greene (born 26 August 1852, Duck Creek, Wisconsin; staked the site of Fargo as a boy) founded the Greene Consolidated Copper Company on 15 September 1899 after finding copper at Cananea; by 1905 the mines made over 70 million pounds a year and he was reckoned one of the world's richest men. In 1903 he bought the San Rafael Ranch on the Arizona line (held by his family to 1998) and ran the Greene Cattle Company and the Cananea Cattle Company; his Sierra Madre Land and Lumber Company fed the mines. The Panic of 1904 and copper's price broke him: he sold the copper company in 1906 to Thomas F. Cole, John D. Ryan and Amalgamated Copper (later Anaconda — the "Greene/Anaconda" of the setting's table), and by 1908 his lumber and cattle empire had collapsed. He died on 5 August 1911 of pneumonia brought on by an accident (a carriage accident, per a search summary), was reburied in Cananea's cemetery in 1956, and after Greene the mine cut foreign workers to about 13% of its workforce.
  • The odd detail: the man whose 1906 telegram brought the Rangers across the line is buried in the town whose strikers they shot — reburied there, with the Mexican miners, fifty years later.
  • People: William C. Greene — the Colonel; Thomas F. Cole, John D. Ryan — the Amalgamated men who bought him out.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Cornell_Greene · https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/19768190/william-cornell-greene (via search)
  • Confidence: High on dates and the copper company; Medium on the carriage accident and on the extent of the Chihuahua timber and cattle lands (not confirmed — the brief mentions "the Greene cattle and copper empire").
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened; death 1911, happened. The Greene Cattle Company's Sonora and Chihuahua ranges are CEDED NORTH — in the setting American-owned land under American law. The Anaconda takeover (1906) is the property chain the treaty inherits.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a dead baron's cattle empire sold in pieces to men of the Golden Circle's map; ranch ledgers with "Sierra Madre lumber" entries for a road nobody built. Greene's San Rafael Ranch, right on the old line, is a base for a Section 13 outpost that needs to be on both sides at once.

Jesús García and the burning dynamite train, Nacozari — 7 November 1907

  • Where: Nacozari, Sonora, on the Moctezuma Copper Company's line to Douglas, Arizona.
  • What happened: Jesús García Corona (born Hermosillo 13 November 1881; a railway water-boy at 17 who rose to fireman and engineer) was resting in Nacozari on 7 November 1907 at about 3 p.m. when hay on the roof of a dynamite car caught fire from a failing locomotive smokebox. He drove the train in reverse, downhill, at full steam, six kilometres out of town before it exploded. He died; his body was found some 20 feet from the engine with, the story says, his hand still on the throttle. The town was renamed Nacozari de García, the American Red Cross declared him a "Hero of Humanity" ⟨fixed: V_M08 — was "the American Cross of Honor", not found in Wikipedia or any page read⟩, and 7 November is the Día del Ferrocarrilero. The corrido "Máquina 501" commemorates him; the town's link to the world was the Douglas line.
  • The odd detail: the national hero of Mexican railway workers died working for an American copper company on a line that ran to Arizona — and the corrido remembers the engine's number.
  • People: Jesús García Corona — engineer; the Moctezuma Copper Company (Phelps Dodge, per ALTERNATE_HISTORY §7.4).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jes%C3%BAs_Garc%C3%ADa · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nacozari_de_Garc%C3%ADa (via search) · https://www.mexconnect.com/articles/1234-did-you-know-the-hero-of-nacozari/ (via search)
  • Confidence: High on the date, drive and the honours; death toll and dynamite tonnage not confirmed (do not print numbers without a source).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. CEDED NORTH. In the setting the hero of Mexican railwaymen is a hero of the American north now: a Sonoran railway union, an American company, and a corrido that both sides sing.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a 1930s Nacozari with a monument to a man who drove death away from a town — and a coal-tender in the yard that smells of burnt dynamite every 7 November. A ghost train is folklore-cheap; "what was really in that car?" is better.

The Mexican Cossack — Emilio Kosterlitzky, Rurales commander to Bureau agent — 1853 – 1928

  • Where: Sonora (Nogales, Cananea, Hermosillo); Los Angeles.
  • What happened: Born in Moscow on 16 November 1853 to a Cossack father and German mother, he deserted the Russian navy at Venezuela in 1871, made his way to Sonora and joined the Mexican army. He spoke nine languages (Russian, Polish, Spanish, French, Italian, English, German, Danish, Swedish), commanded the Gendarmería Fiscal (1885) and the Sonoran Rurales, was nicknamed the "Mexican Cossack" by American troops in the Apache wars, put down the 1896 "Teresitas" rising at Nogales, was at Cananea in 1906, and in 1913, with 400 men, surrendered to the American garrison at Nogales, Arizona rather than yield to Obregón's 2,000. He went to Los Angeles, became a postal translator, was recruited into the US Bureau of Investigation (1917), and was appointed Special Agent on 1 May 1922, handling border cases and Mexican informants until he resigned on 4 September 1926. He died in Los Angeles on 2 March 1928.
  • The odd detail: a Russian-born Mexican colonel who once made 275 Arizona volunteers give up their weapons at Cananea ends as a federal agent liaising with the Mexican informants he used to command.
  • People: Emilio Kosterlitzky — Rurales colonel and later Bureau agent; Álvaro Obregón — the general who forced his 1913 surrender; Emil Lewis Holmdahl — the American mercenary he fought in 1910 (the same Holmdahl accused in the theft of Villa's head, M01).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emilio_Kosterlitzky · https://www.fbi.gov/image-repository/history/emilio-kosterlitzky.jpeg/view (via search) · https://read.dukeupress.edu/hahr/article/52/2/304/151888/Emilio-Kosterlitzky-Eagle-of-Sonora-and-the (via search, not read)
  • Confidence: High on the outline; the "posed as a German physician in WWI" claim is in Wikipedia but conflicts with the setting anyway (see below) and is not repeated.
  • Divergence check: to 1913 happened. BENDS after 1916: in the setting the north is American from 1917 and there is no "border" for a Bureau liaison, so the "agent for border cases" job has no border — but the man is the ideal pre-treaty local expert for a new occupation. And there is no US war in which to pose as a German.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a colonel of the old regime who speaks nine languages and knows where the Sonoran Rurales buried their dead. He would be K. Edgar Hoover's ideal source in the north; the Secret Service would prefer him quiet. His file is where the two agencies' rivalry began in Sonora.

Part C — Chihuahua: the Mormon colonies, the Mennonites and the great estates

The Mormon colonies of Chihuahua and the 1912 exodus — 1885 – 1912

  • Where: the Casas Grandes river valley, Chihuahua (Colonia Díaz, Juárez, Dublán, Pacheco, García, Chuichupa, Cave Valley); also Sonora (Oaxaca, Morelos, San José).
  • What happened: The first colonists left Snowflake, Arizona, on 9 February 1885 — polygamists escaping the Edmunds Act, settling in Mexico on land bought for them. Colonia Díaz and Juárez (1885), Pacheco (1887), Dublán (1888, on 73,000 acres bought by George M. Brown from Lewis Huller; six weeks after the first plough there were 350 people; laid out with wide streets like Salt Lake City), Oaxaca (Sonora, 1892), García and Chuichupa (1894), Morelos (1900), San José (1905). In 1912, with Pascual Orozco's rebels camped near and extorting, the Mexican government said it would no longer protect them; on 28 July 1912 residents of Dublán received expulsion orders and "within two days almost all had fled" — women and children rode the train from Pearson (now Mata Ortiz) to Douglas and El Paso, where Fort Bliss lent tents and a lumber shed on Magoffin Avenue held families. Fewer than half returned; only five of ten colonies were resettled; the mountain colonies were abandoned. George W. Romney (father of Mitt Romney) and Marion G. Romney were born in the colonies.
  • The odd detail: Colonia Dublán became the Punitive Expedition's headquarters in 1916 — a Mormon village of wide streets, abandoned and looted in 1912, became the supply depot of the first American army in Mexico.
  • People: George M. Brown — Dublán's founder; the Romney family — colony-born; Pascual Orozco — the rebel general whose men camped near.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonia_Dubl%C3%A1n · https://www.lascolonias.org/a-brief-history-of-the-mormon-colonies/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mormon_colonies_in_Mexico
  • Confidence: High on dates and Dublán; Medium on colony head-counts (not confirmed this session; I did not find a reliable 1912 population).
  • Divergence check: 1885–1912 happened. CEDED NORTH (Chihuahua; Sonora colonies too). In the setting the exodus of 1912 is followed by a return under the American flag: the colonists who fled a Mexican revolution come back to a Chihuahua American by 1917, and Dublán's 1916 role is exactly as recorded. The colonies were founded as a refuge from American anti-polygamy law; in an America that owns their land the reason for being in Mexico disappears — they become the empire's first settled American communities in the north (how the church's own later reforms fall is not something I confirmed).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): these are Americans who live in Mexico before it is American — a community with its own scripture, its own quiet, and its own view of what the Casas Grandes ruins nearby mean. Report accurately (a living church); use as a population, not a cult. Ask the owner whether Latter-day Saints appear in the setting at all.

The Mennonites arrive from Canada — March 1922

  • Where: the Bustillos valley near Cuauhtémoc, Chihuahua (Manitoba and Swift Current colonies); Nuevo Ideal, Durango.
  • What happened: About 6,000 conservative Mennonites left Manitoba and Saskatchewan after the provinces required public schools to fly the Union flag and enforced attendance; the first train left Plum Coulee, Manitoba, on 1 March 1922. The Santa de Cabora and Tomóchic — 1889 – 29 October 1892

  • Where: the Cabora ranch, Sonora (Teresa Urrea's father's rancho); the village of Tomóchic in the Guerrero district, Chihuahua; Navojoa, Sonora; exile at Nogales, Arizona.
  • What happened: Teresa Urrea (born 15 October 1873 at Ocoroni, Sinaloa; her mother a fourteen-year-old Indigenous ranch worker, her father Tomás Urrea) fell gravely ill in autumn 1889, had visions, and on recovery claimed the Virgin had given her the power to heal. About 1,200 people camped near Cabora for cures; the Mayo and Yaqui, pressed to give up their lands, venerated her as "La Santa de Cabora." The Chihuahua mountain village of Tomóchic — in drought, and under an army that wanted its lands — declared that God alone was its authority under leader Cruz Chávez, and fought the federal troops from 7 December 1891; the army razed the village in October 1892. About 300 villagers were dead by year's end; six survivors, including Chávez and his brother, were lined up and shot on the morning of 29 October 1892. In May 1892 the Mayo sacked Navojoa crying "Viva la Santa de Cabora" and the government exiled Teresa and her father to Nogales, Arizona. She died of tuberculosis on 11 January 1906 in Clifton, Arizona.

Batopilas and "Boss" Shepherd — silver in the canyon, 1879 – 1921

  • Where: Batopilas, Chihuahua — deep in the Sierra Madre / Copper Canyon country, among the Tarahumara.
  • What happened: The Spanish town dates from a 1708 claim (Tarahumara "Bachotigori," place of enclosed waters). The railway that Brownies built — Stilwell's Kansas City, Mexico and Orient, 1900 – 1961

  • Where: Kansas City → Wichita → Alpine (Texas) → Ojinaga → Chihuahua → the sierra (to Topolobampo on the Pacific); the Chihuahua–Ojinaga section and the Temósachic line in the sierra.

  • Where: Chihuahua (train from Mexico City), Bocoyna, Sisoguichi, Norogachi — the Sierra Tarahumara.

The Yaqui republic and its end — Cajeme to Tetabiate, 1872 – 1901 (also in M01: Mazocoba and the 1926–27 campaign)

  • Where: the Río Yaqui valley and the Sierra del Bacatete, Sonora; Guaymas (the embarkation port); Cócorit.
  • What happened: José María Leyva "Cajemé" ("he who does not drink water"; born Hermosillo 1835 or 1837 — sources differ) was schooled at Guaymas in Spanish and English, served the Mexican army, and from 1872 turned his office as alcalde mayor into a unified Yaqui republic of eight pueblos with taxes, river tolls, schools and an army; he was beaten at El Añil (May 1886), betrayed and taken near Guaymas in April 1887 and shot at Cócorit / Chumampaco in April 1887 (dates 12 and 23 April both appear) "attempting escape." Juan Maldonado "Tetabiate" (born Hermosillo 28 August 1857) led a fourteen-year guerrilla war until he was killed in the Bacatete on 9 July 1901. After the battle of Mazocoba (18 January 1900) — about 397 Yaqui confirmed dead, some 1,000 captured — Díaz's "extermination and deportation" policy shipped an estimated 8,000–15,000 Yaquis (of ~30,000) between 1902 and 1908 to Yucatán henequen fields and Oaxaca cane; the port of Guaymas was the embarkation point, and one report (Indigenous Mexico, via search) claims 200 prisoners were thrown from a gunboat off Guaymas in 1892 (Low-Medium confidence). A Yaqui rising ended in defeat in 1929; in 1937 Cárdenas allotted about 500,000 hectares of ejidos to the Yaquis. The NPS notes the Yoeme are "the only Native American tribe that has never officially surrendered."
  • The odd detail: Cajemé, the great Yaqui leader, was mission-educated and spoke English — he had gone to the California gold fields as a boy of fourteen.
  • People: Cajemé — the republic's founder; Tetabiate — his successor, killed 1901; Gen. Lorenzo Torres — Mazocoba's Mexican commander; Kosterlitzky — credited (Wikipedia) with running the deportations [UNVERIFIED — V_M08: Wikipedia's Kosterlitzky article does not mention Yaqui deportations].

Waehma: the Yaqui Easter in Sonora and Tucson — the Pascua refuge, 1900s – today

  • Where: the eight Yaqui pueblos (Sonora); Old Pascua Village, Tucson; South Tucson, Marana, Guadalupe (near Phoenix).
  • What happened: "Thousands of Yaquis walked from Sonora to Arizona" as refugees from Díaz's deportations and the Revolution, founding Pascua (Tucson), Barrio Libre, Marana and Guadalupe; Old Pascua was set on land a benefactor gave. The Lenten cycle (Waehma) runs from Ash Wednesday to Easter Sunday: the Fariseos / Chapayekas (leather-helmet masks with painted faces), an all-night Deer Dance by the maso (little-brother deer) with pascolas and matachines, and on Holy Saturday a battle in which flowers are thrown to drive off the masked Fariseos. Dancers make their promise (a manda) in dreams. Five societies carry out the 40 days. The NPS notes photography, video, audio recording and alcohol are strictly prohibited at Yoeme ceremonies — respect that in any table use.

  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): methane, a shift change, 200 bodies; a 1930s miner remembers who was not in the shaft that morning. The occult layer is a "cult versus population" question: what did the company do with the Japanese dead's funeral rites?

Álamos, the silver town that stayed up — Sonora, 1683 – 1940

  • Where: Álamos, southern Sonora (the Promontorios and Aduana mines; the Mayo country).
  • What happened: A silver vein found in 1683 near La Aduana produced a town of once ~30,000; the price fell in the 1890s and the mines played out by 1915. John Hays Hammond ran the Promontorio and Minas Nuevas mines. In the Revolution an Orozquista column reached Álamos on 21 August 1912 (650 defenders; "over 100 dead men on the ground" at La Aurora) and on 12 May 1915 500 Villistas attacked it — Major Mendoza, with 30 troops and 50 citizens, lost a quarter of them and then, ordering surrender, killed himself. The railway from Navojoa reached Álamos in 1908 and was disbanded in 1931; the town fell to about 1,000 people by 1933 and its mansions turned to ruins — but "Álamos does not become a ghost town." The actress María Félix was born there on 8 April 1914. Álamos lies in Mayo country.
  • The odd detail: the famous Mexican film star was born in a silver town that had already died of the Revolution.
  • People: John Hays Hammond — American mining engineer; Major Mendoza — the 1915 defender; María Félix — born Álamos 1914; the Mayo (Yoreme) — the surrounding people (fariseos; see Waehma entry, the Mayo also joined the 1892 Navojoa rising, "Viva la Santa de Cabora").
  • Sources: https://alamos-sonora-mexico.com/alamos-1900-1949/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayo_people (via search)
  • Confidence: Medium-High (one local-history site; matches other summaries on decline).
  • Divergence check: to 1915 happened. BENDS from 1916–17: CEDED NORTH — Hammond, an American, is the natural mining-engineer figure of an American Sonora; a "quiet backwater" of ruined mansions is a place the occupier forgot.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a ghost-adjacent colonial town with mansions "turned to ruins," a mayor who killed himself rather than surrender, and Mayo neighbours whose Lent society keeps silence for forty days. Mind the third layer: the ruins are real, the ghosts are the period's favourite tourist folklore.

Part G — Three capitals: Chihuahua, Hermosillo, Saltillo

Chihuahua city under Villa — the bank, the Dodge and the Quinta Luz, 1913 – 1923

  • Where: Chihuahua city — the Government Palace, the Bank of the State of Chihuahua, La Quinta Luz.
  • What happened: The city had 19,520 people in 1895 and 45,595 in early 1931; it had had a telephone since 1881, a railway since 16 September 1882, electric trams from August 1908 and the Chuvíscar dam (inaugurated 16 September 1908). Francisco Villa arrived on 8 December 1913 to take the provisional governorship and that month decreed the Bank of the State of Chihuahua, guaranteed by the estates he had confiscated (see the Terrazas–Creel entry); it closed for good in 1915. Carranza came from Juárez in 1914 and was cheered from the Government Palace balcony. Villa's widow María Luz Corral de Villa kept his Quinta Luz as a museum; the Museo Histórico de la Revolución preserves Villa's bullet-riddled Dodge (the car he was shot in at Parral in 1923, M01). Miguel Hidalgo was shot in the former Loreto convent's courtyard on 30 July 1811.
  • The odd detail: the state bank that the rebel governor opened in 1913, with a landlord's cattle as collateral, lasted about as long as the collateral did — two years.
  • People: Francisco "Pancho" Villa — provisional governor; María Luz Corral — his widow and the museum's keeper; Venustiano Carranza — visitor 1914.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chihuahua_City
  • Confidence: High on dates and the Dodge; the museum's provenance claim ("the" car) is Wikipedia's, and the car's identity is often disputed (Medium).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. CEDED NORTH — after 1917 Chihuahua city is an American-held state capital; the Museo de la Revolución's Dodge is the object that answers M01's first question: was Villa killed at Parral in 1923? If the setting rules a different fate, the Dodge is a forgery or a different car. It is also a physical clue a player can touch.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a museum in the widow's house holds the car; the car has more holes than the record accounts for, and the widow is in the guest book. A Section 13 file on the Dodge: was Villa in it at all?

Hermosillo, "the revolutionary capital" — Carranza's refuge and Diéguez, 1913–1915

  • Where: Hermosillo, Sonora — the Government Palace, the Cerro de la Campana and its 1909 lookout (El Caracol), the Cathedral of the Assumption (finished in the early 20th century).
  • What happened: Hermosillo had about 14,000 people at the start of the 20th century. After Madero's murder, Carranza (governor of Coahuila) sought refuge in Hermosillo and there launched the Constitutionalist movement — the city's claim to be "the revolutionary capital of the country." Villa-loyal forces were expelled from the city by Gen. Manuel M. Diéguez — the same Diéguez who had been a Partido Liberal Mexicano organiser sentenced to fifteen years after Cananea (1906). From the late 19th century to the 1920s Chinese immigrants ran shoe and clothing factories in Hermosillo before the leagues (1925 Liga Nacionalista Pro-Raza) drove many out.
  • The odd detail: the striker who went to prison for Cananea in 1906 became the general who drove Villa out of the state capital.
  • People: Venustiano Carranza — First Chief; Manuel M. Diéguez — PLM strike leader and Constitutionalist general; the Chinese shoemakers of Hermosillo — the community the leagues targeted.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermosillo · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cananea_strike
  • Confidence: High on the outline; exact dates of Carranza's stay and Diéguez's action not confirmed (Wikipedia gives none).
  • Divergence check: 1913–15 happened. CEDED NORTH — the "revolutionary capital" is the seat of an American governor by 1917; the setting has to decide whether "Hermosillo, seat of the Constitutionalists" gets to be that (a Carranza-in-exile government-in-waiting).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the capital of a movement that won and then lost its own state. A cathedral finished "at the beginning of the 20th century" over a Neogothic-Neoclassical plan is a dedicated building for a module: the IHS restored the church the revolution's governors banished the priests from (Calles, 1915).

Saltillo — the city three armies occupied, 1913–1916

  • Where: Saltillo, Coahuila — the Ateneo Fuente and its Paraninfo; the Cathedral of Santiago (1746–1810).
  • What happened: Saltillo modernised early (railway 1880; telegraph, telephone and street lighting 1890) and in the Revolution "was occupied in separate events by the forces of Victoriano Huerta, Francisco Villa, and then by those of Venustiano Carranza"; hundreds of peasants were conscripted, and many families, including aristocratic ones, fled to Texas. The Ateneo Fuente's Paraninfo carries murals by the Catalan painter Salvador Tarazona; the Antonio Narro Agrarian University was founded in 1923. The city's identity is the sarape, a striped garment tied to its Tlaxcaltec settlers.
  • The odd detail: the capital of the state that produced the Revolution had to be taken three times by three armies, and the people who fled did so to Texas.
  • People: Huerta, Villa, Carranza — the three occupiers.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saltillo · https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/saltillo-coahuila-mexico (early history only — stops before 1900)
  • Confidence: Medium (Wikipedia only; I found no detailed Saltillo case for 1900–40 — see Leads).
  • Divergence check: the occupation years 1913–15 pre-1916, happened. CEDED NORTH. The 1923 Narro university would, in the setting, be an American institution in an American Coahuila; the Texan aristocrats who fled to Texas in 1914 return in 1917 as owners of the new empire's estates.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): thin on the record, so the room is wide: a capital that did not decide anything and has been left to it. A sarape as a period detail: pattern-woven, Tlaxcaltec, worn by both sides.

Part H — Divergence questions this lane raises (for the owner; nothing settled here; M01 Part E already lists ten — these are new)

  1. Who governs the ceded north — and is it one governor or four? (PEOPLE open decision 4.) The record supplies three types of candidate: a local banker-diplomat with an American father (Enrique Creel, d. 1931 — Terrazas–Creel entry), a frontier enforcer (Kosterlitzky — a colonel of the old regime who already answered to Washington), and a press-and-cattle baron (Hearst). Four states with four different histories (Sonora's Yaqui and copper, Chihuahua's estates and Mormons, Coahuila's coal and the Madero family, Nuevo León's steel) argue for four administrations, not one.
  2. What are the people, legally? Puerto Rico's Jones Act (March 1917) is the model the rulebook already uses (§8.3); the ceded north's inhabitants (no census figure read this session) have to be citizens, nationals, or something the Insular Cases' language invents. The Chinese of Sonora (6,078 in 1919) and the Japanese and Okinawan miners of Coahuila (about 11,000 contracted 1901–07) fall under the Exclusion regime in ways nobody has decided; the Yaqui (never officially surrendered) are not a "tribe" American law has seen in Sonora at all.
  3. Do the Mexican land titles survive? Villa's 1913 confiscations (Terrazas), Hearst's Babícora, Greene's ranches, the Madero and Garza-Sada holdings and the Mennonites' 1922 purchases all rest on Mexican law. Article 27 (M01) cannot bind them; the treaty must either recognise them or void them. The Mennonites' and Mormons' exemptions from military service were granted by Mexican presidents — who honours them?
  4. The Church in the ceded north. Calles's priest ban ran in Sonora in 1915–16; Chihuahua's one-priest law (1926) and Maldonado's murder (1937) cannot happen (M01). Do the Sonoran churches shut in 1915 stay shut into 1917, then reopen under American protection? That is where the IHS would set its first northern base — and where the Magdalena pilgrimage and Niño Fidencio's pilgrims fit (the only two mass devotions in the ceded north that the occupier cannot police).
  5. The Yaqui after 1917. The 1926–27 campaign becomes American (M01); the 1929 rising and the 1937 land grant of ~500,000 hectares to the Yaquis (a Cárdenas act) have no obvious setting equivalent. Either the occupation's second war is a Yaqui war, or the setting gives the Yaqui their land as a matter of policy (irrigated Yaqui Valley wheat: §29.3).
  6. Does the Sonoran dynasty rule anything? (M01 Q2.) This lane adds: Calles's 1915 Sonora and Diéguez's expulsion of Villa from Hermosillo are both pre-treaty events by men who later lose their state.
  7. Does the Japanese labour in Coahuila matter to Berlin? With Japan leaning German (§30.6), a Japanese-descended mining population in an American-held coalfield is exactly the kind of thing a victorious Germany's consul counts.
  8. The Torreón claim. China's 30-million-peso claim (M01) and the Wah Yick bank's museum: does America assume Mexico's liabilities in the ceded states?
