M02 — Mexican folklore, folk religion and the uncanny, as attested before and during the 1930s

  • Lane: M02 — La Llorona, the nahual, brujas and the lechuza, curanderismo and the famous healers (Niño Fidencio, Teresa Urrea, Don Pedrito Jaramillo), Día de Muertos and Posada's calaveras, Guadalupe and the Cristero devotion, folk saints and the roots of Santa Muerte, the Yaqui ceremonies, Aztec survivals, the period's haunted places (Mexico City, Guanajuato), Spiritualism — each with its first attestation and the treaty-line check.

Respect rule, applied throughout. Curanderismo, Catholic folk devotion, Guadalupe, the Yaqui ceremonial year and the Día de Muertos are living religion and living practice — reported as such, with the period's own (often racist or anticlerical) framing marked as the source's. Folklore is labelled folklore, with the earliest attestation found. Hooks put the setting's monsters beside these traditions, never inside them: the population is ordinary, the cult is small, and a healer is not a witch because an American newspaper said so.

Treaty-line tags (as in M01): CEDED NORTH (Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila, Nuevo León/Monterrey — American after the 1917 treaty) · TAMPICO ENCLAVE · VERACRUZ (Mexican flag, American customs receivership) · UNCEDED MEXICO (Mexico City, Guanajuato, Jalisco, Michoacán, Oaxaca…) · US SIDE (Texas, Arizona, New Mexico — American before 1848).

Overview (40 entries). The oldest layer has the best paper: La Llorona's ancestor is Sahagún's sixth omen (a woman heard weeping for her children before the Conquest), and the nahual has an Inquisition file from 1536 (Martín Ocelotl, the jaguar-man whose prison ship vanished), a 1629 priest's manual that preserved the spells it meant to kill, a bishop who burned a nagualist treasure in 1691, and an 1894 American scholar (Brinton) who read the whole thing as a pan-Indian occult conspiracy — the period's own wrong reading, made for a federal memo. The healers are the richest living-practice material, and two of the three sit on the setting's American side: Teresa Urrea (Sonora → Arizona; rebels hit customs houses in her name in 1896) and Niño Fidencio of Espinazo, Nuevo León — ceded, visited by Calles for about three hours on 8 Feb 1928 ⟨fixed: V_M02 — the WRSP profile says six; Agostoni 2018, from the press, says three⟩, his patron a German Spiritist hacendado. The best places: the mummies of Guanajuato (dug up for unpaid grave rent since 1865, shown for a fee), La Pascualita (a bride in a Chihuahua shop window from 25 March 1930 [UNVERIFIED — V_M02: popular-source date, no primary record] — ceded north), and Don Juan Manuel's street in the capital. Chicago surfaces three times: the University of Chicago published the period's key studies (Redfield 1930, Parsons 1936, Spicer 1940), and the scholar jailed for the 1932 theft of the Carvajal crypto-Jewish diaries had taught at Northwestern ⟨fixed: V_M02 — confirmed by LARB, no longer "per search"⟩. Flags: Santa Muerte has no public cult in the period (1797, then silence to the 1940s); the chupacabra is 1995; Malverde is unverified; the lechuza has no pre-1940 dated text found; Catemaco's witch festival is post-1970. Surprise for the setting: Pedro Linares's 1936 fever dream of self-naming creatures is the lane's one clean fae-shaped event.

Part A — The weeping woman and the shapeshifter (the oldest layer, with first attestations)

The sixth omen: a woman heard weeping in the night — c. 1550s–1570s (recorded); set before 1519 (FOLKLORE, first attestation of La Llorona's ancestor)

  • Where: Tenochtitlan / Mexico City (UNCEDED MEXICO); recorded at the Franciscan college of Tlatelolco.
  • What happened: Fray Bernardino de Sahagún's Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España (compiled c. 1540–1585; the Nahuatl–Spanish Florentine Codex) lists ten omens said to have foretold the Conquest. The sixth: "many times a woman would be heard going along weeping and shouting" at night — crying for her children, asking where she could take them. Sahagún also describes the goddess Cihuacóatl ("Snake Woman") appearing in white, walking at night weeping and wailing — an evil omen. Scholars read this as the ancestor of La Llorona; the pre-Hispanic Chocacíhuatl ("the crying woman", a spirit of women who died in childbirth) is the other root. Many popular sources give "1550, Mexico City" as La Llorona's first sighting — that date is Sahagún's era, not a dated police-blotter event (treat "1550" as folklore's own date).
  • The odd detail: the first attestation is not a ghost story but a prophecy of conquest — the weeping woman is heard before the invader comes. In a Mexico that lost its north in 1917, she has a second invasion to weep for.
  • People: Bernardino de Sahagún — Franciscan ethnographer (d. 1590); Cihuacóatl — Mexica mother-goddess of childbirth and war.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Llorona · (search summary of Mexicolore's Llorona article: https://www.mexicolore.co.uk/aztecs/home/la-llorona-and-the-days-of-the-dead-in-mexico-1)
  • Confidence: High that the omen and Cihuacóatl passages exist in Sahagún; the Cihuacóatl→Llorona line is a scholarly reading, not a documented chain.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, recorded. The legend is everywhere, both sides of the treaty line.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): three layers — the period calls her a nursemaid's bogey; the folklore says she is heard before disaster; the setting could let her be heard in Chihuahua in the winter of 1916, before the column came south. She is not the monster; she is the warning.

La Llorona in print and on screen — a 19th-century sonnet; the 1933 film (FOLKLORE; first literary and film attestations)

  • Where: Mexico City (UNCEDED MEXICO); the southwestern US (US SIDE) in oral tradition.
  • What happened: A published 19th-century sonnet by the Mexican poet Manuel Carpio (1791–1860) makes La Llorona the ghost of a murdered woman named Rosalía — not a child-killer; the infanticide version (the woman who drowned her children for a lover) is the one that hardened later. The story reached film in 1933 with La Llorona, shot in Mexico — among the first Mexican sound-era horror films (director not confirmed this session: see Leads). The folk song "La Llorona" (Isthmus of Tehuantepec) was popularised by Andrés Henestrosa in 1941 — after the period. Versions are recorded across Guatemala, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Costa Rica and the American Southwest.
  • The odd detail: the oldest literary Llorona in this search is a murder victim, not a murderer — the monster was a victim first.
  • People: Manuel Carpio — physician-poet; Andrés Henestrosa — Zapotec writer (1906–2008).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Llorona
  • Confidence: Medium — the Carpio sonnet and 1933 film are from Wikipedia's summary; the film's director and cast UNVERIFIED this session.
  • Divergence check: the 1933 film holds (a Mexico City production; the capital is unceded). In the setting a 1933 horror picture about a weeping ghost plays in the occupied north too — in Spanish, in American-run cinemas.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a 1933 Mexico City film crew shoots its Llorona at a real canal or river at night and hears a second voice on the sound disc. A Chicago Mexican-quarter cinema (Pilsen, South Chicago) screens the print; the voice comes with it.

Martín Ocelotl, the jaguar-man before the Inquisition — tried autumn 1536, sentenced 10 February 1537

  • Where: Chinantla (Puebla) and Texcoco / Mexico City (UNCEDED MEXICO).
  • What happened: Martín Ocelotl ("Jaguar"), born c. 1496 to a merchant father and a well-known priestess mother, baptised in 1525, was a tlaciuhqui and ticitl (diviner-healer) who predicted rain and famine. In autumn 1536 he was tried before Bishop Juan de Zumárraga's Apostolic Inquisition; witnesses said he could turn himself into a jaguar ("tiger") and a cat — and scholars say he identified the Spaniards with the tzitzimime, the star-demons expected to descend at the world's end [UNVERIFIED — V_M02: not on the Wikipedia page read]. His house was searched and ritual objects seized (jade, gold disks, codices, per a secondary source [UNVERIFIED — V_M02: not on the Wikipedia page read]). Sentenced 10 February 1537 to public humiliation and imprisonment in Seville, he was put on a ship for Spain — and the ship disappeared. No one knows what became of him.
  • The odd detail: the first documented nahualli in the Inquisition's files is lost at sea on the way to prison — the record simply stops.
  • People: Martín Ocelotl — Nahua diviner, "a nahual"; Juan de Zumárraga — first bishop of Mexico, inquisitor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mart%C3%ADn_Ocelotl · (search summary: https://mexicounexplained.com/martin-ocelotl-life-and-trial-of-the-jaguar-shaman/)
  • Confidence: High on the trial, dates and the lost ship (Wikipedia; standard in the literature, e.g. Don, Bonfires of Culture); Medium on the seized objects list.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (by four centuries). UNCEDED MEXICO.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): IHS's archive holds a copy of the 1537 sentence, and a margin note from a Seville official: the ship never came in. The setting's werewolf analogue has a paper trail older than any European werewolf trial the players know — but the man himself was a healer and a priestess's son; the files that call him a monster are the Inquisition's. Keep the two apart.

Ruiz de Alarcón's Treatise on the Heathen Superstitions — written 1629

  • Where: Atenango and the Guerrero–Morelos parishes; the manuscript now in the Museo Nacional de Antropología, Mexico City (UNCEDED MEXICO).
  • What happened: Hernando Ruiz de Alarcón (brother of the playwright Juan Ruiz de Alarcón), a parish priest acting with inquisitorial commission, wrote a manual in 1629 to help churchmen root out surviving Nahua religion. He recorded the incantations in Nahuatl with Spanish glosses — for curing, love, hunting, travel, sleep — and offered an etymology of nahualli: likely "a person wrapped up or disguised under the appearance of such an animal, as they commonly believe." The only surviving manuscript copy is in the national museum. (English translation: Andrews & Hassig, University of Oklahoma Press, 1984.)
  • The odd detail: a book written to destroy the spells is the reason the spells survive — the only copy sits in a Mexico City museum case.
  • People: Hernando Ruiz de Alarcón — priest-extirpator (c. 1574–1646).
  • Sources: https://www.oupress.com/9780806120317/treatise-on-the-heathen-superstitions/ (via search) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagual
  • Confidence: High on date, purpose, the Nahuatl incantations and the etymology quote (publisher's summary via search; not the full text).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916. The manuscript is in the capital — UNCEDED MEXICO.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a grimoire-in-reverse. A Kabbalah contracted by the Church (the setting's arrangement) is paid to read the Nahuatl the priests could not pronounce. Which incantations Ruiz de Alarcón left out is the lead.

Bishop Núñez de la Vega burns the nagualists' treasure — 1691 (burned); 1702 (published)

  • Where: Huehuetán, Soconusco, Chiapas (UNCEDED MEXICO, far south).
  • What happened: Francisco Núñez de la Vega, Dominican Bishop of Chiapas and Soconusco from 1687, devoted his ninth Pastoral Letter to nagualismo and printed it in his Constituciones Diœcesanas (Rome, 1702). He found at Tlazoaloyan a hidden "dark house" said to have been built by the culture-hero Votan "by blowing with his breath", kept by a noble lady and guardians (tlapiane); he seized its lidded jars, a stone carved with ancient heroes, green chalchihuites and other objects and had them "publicly burned in the plaza of Huehuetan" (1691). He described the nagualists' calendar books in "a secret cypher" used to fix each newborn's fate, and an initiation in which the disciple lies on an ant-hill while the master calls a black, white and red snake that "enters the body with a leap".
  • The odd detail: the ant-hill initiation — ants entering at the finger-joints, the snake passing through the body. A bishop printed it in Rome.
  • People: Francisco Núñez de la Vega — bishop, extirpator (in office 1687–1706); Votan — Tzeltal culture hero.
  • Sources: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/26426/26426-h/26426-h.htm (Brinton's translation of the passages)
  • Confidence: High for what the bishop wrote (as translated by Brinton, 1894); the events are his account.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916. UNCEDED MEXICO.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Church burned the objects and kept the book of the burning. Cult versus population: the bishop's letter treats a whole region as a conspiracy; the setting's version is one lineage of nagualistas the bishop never found, and a population he punished in their place.

Brinton's Nagualism — read to the American Philosophical Society, 5 January 1894

  • Where: Philadelphia (US SIDE); about Mexico and Central America.
  • What happened: Daniel Garrison Brinton, the University of Pennsylvania's Americanist, read Nagualism: A Study in Native American Folk-lore and History to the American Philosophical Society on 5 January 1894. He argued nagualism was "a cult … which united many and diverse tribes of Mexico and Central America into organized opposition against the government and the religion … introduced from Europe" — a secret pan-Indian society "bound together by strange faculties and an occult learning." He tied it to the Zapotec rising of 1550 (a priest claiming to be Quetzalcoatl), the 1661 Tehuantepec revolt (Don Pascual's summons to "the thirteen governors"), and the 1692 Mexico City riot, after which the authorities banned the piochtli scalp-lock. From Herrera he took the rule that "when such an animal dies, so does the Indian to whom it was assigned."
  • The odd detail: the period's leading American scholar framed Mexican Indigenous religion as an occult secret society plotting against the state — the same shape the setting gives the Golden Circle.
  • People: Daniel G. Brinton — Penn anthropologist (1837–1899).
  • Sources: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/26426/26426-h/26426-h.htm
  • Confidence: High on what Brinton wrote; his "organised secret society" thesis is the period's reading, not established history (modern scholars treat nagualism as a belief complex, not an organisation).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916. A book a 1930s federal analyst could pull from any university library.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the period's own category. A Section 13 or Secret Service memo on the occupied north quotes Brinton to argue the Sonoran resistance is a nagual conspiracy — so the occupier's intelligence is wrong in the most useful way: it hunts shapeshifters among farmers while the thing that actually walks the Sierra is not Mexican at all.

"Lots of brujas around": Bourke's Rio Grande — recorded 1891–1893, printed 1894

  • Where: Fort Ringgold, Rio Grande City, Texas (US SIDE) and the Tamaulipas bank opposite.
  • What happened: Army Captain John G. Bourke, stationed on the lower Rio Grande 1891–93, published "Popular Medicine, Customs, and Superstitions of the Rio Grande" in the Journal of American Folklore (vol. 7, no. 25, Apr.–Jun. 1894). His main informant, María Antonia Cavazo de Garza, called herself a curandera though neighbours called her a bruja; she told him "there were lots of 'brujas' (witches) around, who took delight in doing harm to you personally, or in spreading sickness among your cattle." He records a washerwoman-witch who "filled this man full of worms" when he would not pay her five dollars; the cure for mal de ojo (the evil eye) — the culprit spits water into the child's mouth, or an egg is passed in the sign of the cross; mustard crosses on the wall against witches; the prayer "Cuatro esquinas tiene mi casa, cuatro ángeles que la adoran"; a coyote crossing your path as a sign of sudden death; and a love-charm of jack-rabbit eyes ground into a cigarette.
  • The odd detail: the curandera/bruja line is drawn by the neighbours, not the woman — the same person is healer to herself and witch to the street.
  • People: John Gregory Bourke — cavalry officer and ethnologist (1846–1896); María Antonia Cavazo de Garza — his informant.
  • Sources: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Popular_medicine,_customs_and_superstitions_of_the_Rio_Grande
  • Confidence: High (primary text, public domain). Note Bourke's framing is an Army officer's, condescending by design.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916. US SIDE — Texas was American already; the practices cross the river both ways.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Bourke is exactly the kind of officer the Secret Service would have read in 1916; his informant's granddaughter is a 1930s curandera in Laredo whom the Bureau has confused with a witch. Three layers: the Army's condescension, the living practice, and whatever is really putting worms in men.

Part B — The healers: curanderismo and the folk saints (living practice)

Teresa Urrea, "La Santa de Cabora" — 1873–1906

  • Where: Born Ocoroni, Sinaloa (UNCEDED); healed at the Cabora ranch, Sonora (CEDED NORTH); exiled to Nogales, El Paso, Clifton, Arizona (US SIDE).
  • What happened: Born 15 October 1873 to a 14-year-old Tehueco (Cahita) mother, Cayetana Chávez, and the hacendado Don Tomás Urrea. In 1889 she fell into convulsions, ate only earth mixed with her saliva, then a trance of "three months and eighteen days" — and woke a healer, saying the Virgin had given her the don. Thousands of pilgrims came to Cabora; she charged nothing and healed with touch, saliva-and-earth poultices and herbs. Rebels invoked her: the Mayo shouted "¡Viva la Santa de Cabora!" attacking Navojoa (May 1892), and the Tarahumara/mestizo village of Tomóchic (Chihuahua) rose in her name and was razed in October 1892 (~300 dead). Porfirio Díaz deported her and her father in May 1892 (19 May by her own statement; the WRSP page says June) ⟨fixed: V_M02⟩. From El Paso (1896) the Spiritist Lauro Aguirre published her as "Juana de Arco Mexicana"; in August–September 1896 raiders called Teresistas hit the customs houses at Nogales (12 Aug), Ojinaga (17 Aug) and Palomas in her name. She toured US cities 1900–02 (St. Louis, San Francisco, New York), was advertised as a Spiritualist medium, and died of tuberculosis in Clifton, Arizona, 11 January 1906, aged 32 ⟨fixed: V_M02 — born 15 Oct 1873; several sources say 33⟩.
  • The odd detail: the rebels who attacked the customs houses in her name — in the setting, the customs house at Veracruz is exactly what America holds. The Teresistas already had the target.
  • People: Teresa Urrea — healer, symbol of Indigenous and anti-Díaz dissent; Tomás Urrea — her father; Lauro Aguirre — El Paso Spiritist and revolutionary publisher; Gen. Refugio González — "the Mexican Kardec", who wrote her up in La Ilustración Espírita.
  • Sources: https://wrldrels.org/2021/07/05/teresa-urrea-la-santa-de-cabora/ · (search: https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/urrea-teresa)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Cabora (Sonora) and Tomóchic (Chihuahua) are CEDED NORTH after 1917 — the saint's country becomes American, as her exile (Arizona) already was. Living memory for any 1930s northerner over 45.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): respectfully — she is a healer and a symbol, not a monster. The hook is the people who used her name: a 1930s guerrilla band in the occupied Sonoran foothills calls itself Teresistas again, and the occupation's files do not know whether they are Mayo farmers, Spiritists, or something wearing her memory. IHS would read her as a possible true mystic; the Bureau would read her as a flag.

Don Pedrito Jaramillo, the healer of Los Olmos — 1881–1907; his biography printed 1934

  • Where: Los Olmos ranch, near Falfurrias, Brooks County, Texas (US SIDE); born near Guadalajara, Jalisco (UNCEDED).
  • What happened: Born to Tarascan (Purépecha) parents near Guadalajara, Pedro Jaramillo, a poor labourer, cured a nose affliction by burying his face in the mud at a pool for three days; a voice woke him and told him he had received the gift of healing from God. He came to South Texas in 1881, settled at Los Olmos, and for 26 years prescribed "the first thing that he thought of" — Catholic faith and simple remedies — charging nothing, walking or riding a donkey to patients between the Nueces and the Rio Grande. Letters came by mail with fifty-cent and dollar donations; more than $5,000 in fifty-cent pieces was found in his house at his death on 3 July 1907. His grave became a shrine visited by hundreds a year. Ruth Dodson's Don Pedrito Jaramillo: Curandero was published (in Spanish) in 1934.
  • The odd detail: $5,000 in half-dollars under a healer's roof who never charged — and he gave the remedies away as well.
  • People: Pedro Jaramillo — curandero (d. 1907); Viola Ruth Dodson — Texas folklorist, his 1934 biographer.
  • Sources: https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/jaramillo-pedro
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (life); 1934 book holds — domestic calendar. US SIDE.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): his shrine near Falfurrias is on the road between the King Ranch country and the border; in the 1930s pilgrims still leave notes. A Section 13 agent reading Dodson's 1934 book finds one prescription that is not a remedy at all — a warning, dated.

El Niño Fidencio of Espinazo — arrived 1921 or 1923, healing from 1925 ⟨fixed: V_M02⟩; President Calles's visit of about three hours ⟨fixed: V_M02⟩, 8 February 1928; died 19 October 1938

  • Where: Espinazo, Mina municipality, Nuevo León (CEDED NORTH — the Monterrey state), a railway stop in the desert near the Coahuila line.
  • What happened: José Fidencio Constantino Síntora, born 17 October 1898 in Yuriria, Guanajuato, came to Espinazo in 1921 or 1923 and began healing there in 1925 (Agostoni 2018; the WRSP profile gives 1925 as the year he came) ⟨fixed: V_M02⟩ to join his friend Enrique López de la Fuente, administrator of a hacienda owned by a German immigrant, Teodoro von Wernich (confirmed by Agostoni: "empresario alemán y espiritista"), who (per the WRSP profile) "may have introduced Fidencio to Spiritism." After a 1927 vision under a pepper tree (pirul), he healed without charge: tumours removed with pieces of glass, paralysis treated with a swing, cleansings in mud and in his own bathwater and in the pool called the Charco, fruit thrown into the crowd as mass healing. On 8 February 1928 President Plutarco Elías Calles — the anticlerical strongman of the Cristero War — spent about three hours in Espinazo ⟨fixed: V_M02 — Agostoni 2018, from the press; WRSP says six hours⟩ with him (a skin disease; later said to be leprosy) and reportedly pulled him into his private train. The New York Times ran "Cures by Healer Stir Mexico City" (22 Feb 1928). Shanties grew to hundreds, then thousands; Espinazo got a post office and telegraph. Nuevo León prosecuted him for illegal medicine (from 18 Feb 1929). He died 19 October 1938, days before 40; believers hold that doctors' autopsy incision in his throat drew warm blood — proof he was alive, in trance, when they cut. His mediums (cajitas, materias) still channel him; pilgrimages each March and October.
  • The odd detail: the man who shut Mexico's churches sought out a folk healer in a boxcar town — and the healer's patron was a German hacendado who taught Spiritism.
  • People: Niño Fidencio — curandero, folk saint; Plutarco Elías Calles — president (a Sonoran); Teodoro von Wernich — German hacienda owner; Enrique López de la Fuente — his friend.
  • Sources: https://wrldrels.org/2016/10/08/el-nino-fidencio/ · (search: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni%C3%B1o_Fidencio)
  • Confidence: High on the 8 Feb 1928 visit, the 22 Feb 1928 New York Times piece and the 19 Oct 1938 death; the visit's length (three vs six hours) and his arrival year (1921/23 vs 1925) vary by source ⟨fixed: V_M02⟩ (Agostoni 2018 also records that the 1929 illegal-medicine case was dismissed); the leprosy is a later disclosure (Medium); the von Wernich–Spiritism link is the source's "may have".
  • Divergence check: BENDS — Espinazo is in CEDED NORTH (Nuevo León). In the setting a Mexican president's visit is a state visit into American territory, or it is not Calles who comes (M01 asks whether the Sonoran dynasty rules the rump state at all). Either way the healer's fame falls under an American public-health regime — Nuevo León's 1929 prosecution becomes an American medical-licensing case, and his 1938 death, a death on American soil with an American autopsy.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Germany as patron in miniature — the German landowner, the Spiritist books. Santa Muerte in the Inquisition files — 1793 (Querétaro) and 1797 (San Luis de la Paz)

  • Where: a Querétaro parish (1793) and San Luis de la Paz, Guanajuato (1797) — UNCEDED MEXICO.

Part C — The dead: Día de Muertos and the calaveras

José Guadalupe Posada's calaveras and the Catrina — c. 1880–1913; died 20 January 1913

  • Where: Mexico City — the press of Antonio Vanegas Arroyo (UNCEDED MEXICO); Posada born in Aguascalientes (UNCEDED).

The Mummies of Guanajuato — first exhumed 1865; shown for a fee by the late 1800s

  • Where: the Panteón de Santa Paula, Guanajuato city (UNCEDED MEXICO — the Bajío, Cristero-adjacent country).

  • Where: the Zócalo, Mexico City (UNCEDED MEXICO) — then the patio of the Royal and Pontifical University; the Sun Stone set into the west tower of the Metropolitan Cathedral.

Part D — Faith: Guadalupe, the Cristero devotion, and the Yaqui ceremonial year (living religion)

The Virgin of Guadalupe — 1531 by tradition; first in print 1648–49; the bomb under the image, 14 November 1921

  • Where: the Basilica at Tepeyac, Villa de Guadalupe, north of Mexico City (UNCEDED MEXICO).
  • What happened: By tradition the Virgin appeared to Juan Diego on 9–12 December 1531 and left her image on his cloak (tilma).   • People: Juan Diego — the seer (canonised 2002); Miguel Sánchez and Luis Laso de la Vega — first printed sources; Luciano Pérez Carpio — the 1921 bomber (per Catholic sources).

Part F — The Inquisition's afterlife, the Chicago connection, and the period's anthropologists

The Carvajal diaries vanish from the national archive — 1932 (a Jewish mystic's book, burned 1596)

  • Where: the Archivo General de la Nación, Mexico City (UNCEDED); the family's history runs through the Nuevo Reino de León (Monterrey — CEDED NORTH).
  • What happened: Luis de Carvajal the Younger (c. 1567–1596), nephew of Luis de Carvajal y de la Cueva, governor of the Nuevo Reino de León, was a secret Jew (Hebrew name Joseph Lumbroso) who wrote, in a tiny book about 4 × 3 inches, an autobiographical meditation for his siblings with prayers, the Ten Commandments and Maimonides' Thirteen Principles. The Inquisition burned him at the stake on 8 December 1596 in Mexico City's plaza. His manuscripts sat in the national archive until 1932, when they "disappeared without a trace." An archive historian writing his own Carvajal book (Alfonso Toro, per the LARB account) accused a visiting Yiddish-speaking scholar, Jacob Nachbin (also spelled Nachlin) — who spent three months in jail and was released for lack of evidence; some scholars suspect the accuser. The booklet surfaced at Bloomsbury Auctions, London, in 2015 (catalogued as an anonymous 17th–18th-century work) and at Swann Galleries, New York, 21 June 2016 (lot 387); collector Leonard Milberg bought and returned it to Mexico.
  • The odd detail: Nachbin had taught at Northwestern University in Evanston, Illinois (and at the University of New Mexico at Las Vegas) — the 1932 suspect in the theft of the Americas' oldest Jewish memoir was a Chicago-area professor ⟨fixed: V_M02 — confirmed on the LARB page, https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/the-thief-on-the-rediscovery-of-luis-de-carvajal-the-youngers-memoir/; the three months in jail and release for lack of evidence are on the Mexico News Daily page cited above⟩.
  • People: Luis de Carvajal el Mozo — crypto-Jewish mystic, burned 1596; Luis de Carvajal y de la Cueva — governor of Nuevo León (d. in prison 1590); Jacob Nachbin — Polish-born scholar (b. 1896), accused 1932; Alfonso Toro — archive historian and accuser.
  • Sources: https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/the-thief-on-the-rediscovery-of-luis-de-carvajal-the-youngers-memoir/ · https://mexiconewsdaily.com/mexico-living/story-of-first-jewish-writings-in-americas/ · (search: https://paw.princeton.edu/article/leonard-milberg-53-helps-return-16th-century-texts-rightful-home)
  • Confidence: High on Carvajal, the 1596 burning, the 1932 disappearance, the jailing, the 2015–16 reappearance; Medium on Toro as accuser (LARB); the Northwestern link is now confirmed (LARB) ⟨fixed: V_M02⟩.
  • Divergence check: the 1932 theft holds (the capital's archive is unceded). The Carvajals' Nuevo León is CEDED NORTH — the governor's old province is American ground in the setting.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): made for the Kabbalahs. A 1932 theft of a burned mystic's prayer book, a Chicago-connected suspect, an archivist accuser, and a book that stays lost until long after the decade. Three layers: the period says a scholars' feud; the folklore of the Jewish Americas says the book was rescued; the setting can say which Kabbalah has it in 1936, and what the Inquisition's copy-clerks never saw in the margins.

The Palace of the Inquisition becomes the Medical School — built 1732–36; medical school 1854–1956

  • Where: north side of the Plaza de Santo Domingo, Mexico City (UNCEDED).
  • What happened: Designed by Pedro de Arrieta and built 1732–1736 as the seat of the Holy Office's tribunal, it served the Inquisition until the tribunal was abolished in 1820. Offered at auction in 1838 without a buyer, it passed through the archbishopric, lottery offices, a primary school and a barracks, and in 1854 was sold to the professors of the National School of Medicine, which taught there — anatomy included — until 1956, when it moved to University City. It is now the Museum of Mexican Medicine (1980).
  • The odd detail: for the entire 1930s, Mexico's doctors learned to cut the dead inside the building where the Inquisition had held its prisoners.
  • People: Pedro de Arrieta — architect.
  • Sources: (search) https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacio_de_la_Escuela_de_Medicina · https://libros.facmed.unam.mx/index.php/2024/01/23/el-edificio-de-la-inquisicion-un-lugar-para-la-medicina-cientifica-la-escuela-nacional-de-medicina-1854/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_reportedly_haunted_locations_in_Mexico
  • Confidence: High on the dates (multiple search summaries; the UNAM page confirms the 1854 sale); dissection rooms are inference from a medical school (Medium). Ghost reports are modern and generic.
  • Divergence check: holds; UNCEDED.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a 1930s medical student finds a sealed cell below the anatomy theatre; the Inquisition's files on Martín Ocelotl's successors were never all moved. IHS wants the room before the Faculty does.

Elsie Clews Parsons, Mitla, Town of the Souls — fieldwork 1929–33; University of Chicago Press, 1936

  • Where: Mitla, Oaxaca (UNCEDED) — Zapotec town beside the great ruins.
  • What happened: The American anthropologist Elsie Clews Parsons (1875–1941) spent three field seasons in Mitla and published Mitla, Town of the Souls with the University of Chicago Press in 1936. She records that the old Zapotec name Lyobaa means "Place of Tombs", and that "the souls, las ánimas, of all Zapotecan people, from valley and mountain come to Mitla"; that "witches and spirits as well as robbers are abroad at night"; and she asks in her preface whether "the beliefs in transformation into animals, in witch assembly, in acquiring property by witchcraft" are Indian or Spanish. Her best informant was a curandera, "la difunta Isidora".
  • The odd detail: a whole town understood by its own people as the destination of every Zapotec soul — and a Chicago press printed it in 1936.
  • People: Elsie Clews Parsons — anthropologist, Columbia/New School; Isidora — Mitla curandera (her informant).
  • Sources: https://archive.org/stream/parsons-mitla-town-of-the-souls/parsons-mitla-town-of-the-souls_djvu.txt · (search: https://www.britannica.com/topic/Mitla-Town-of-the-Souls)
  • Confidence: High on the quotations (from the full text via the archive; page refs p. 2, p. 12, p. x).
  • Divergence check: holds; Oaxaca is UNCEDED.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Chicago book about a town where the dead go. Ghosts are one of the setting's five threats — Mitla is the place where, by its own people's account, they all arrive. An American faction arriving "to study" is the intrusion; the Zapotec town is the host.

Robert Redfield's Tepoztlán — fieldwork November 1926 – July 1927; University of Chicago Press, 1930

  • Where: Tepoztlán, Morelos (UNCEDED) — a Nahuatl-speaking town under the cliff temple of the Tepozteco.
  • What happened: Robert Redfield, who joined the University of Chicago faculty in 1927, did fieldwork in Tepoztlán from November 1926 to July 1927 and published Tepoztlán, a Mexican Village: A Study of Folk Life (1930) — the founding text of his "folk–urban continuum". He records the ruined temple above the town, "known as the 'house of Tepozteco'", and "a dance-drama [that] commemorates the valorous defense of Tepoztlan by Tepoztecatl", the pre-Hispanic deity-hero (linked to the pulque god Ome Tochtli); his Chapter IX is "Magic and Medicine"; his photographs include a household santo and the leaping chinelos of carnival.
  • The odd detail: the University of Chicago's founding study of "folk" Mexico was written during the Cristero War, in a town whose old god still had his house on the cliff.
  • People: Robert Redfield — University of Chicago anthropologist (1897–1958); Tepoztécatl — the town's god-hero.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/stream/tepoztlanamexica007150mbp/tepoztlanamexica007150mbp_djvu.txt · (search: http://scihi.org/robert-redfield-folk-urban-continuum/)
  • Confidence: High on dates and quotes (full text); the nahual/witch passages were not reached this session.
  • Divergence check: holds; Morelos is UNCEDED (Zapata's country).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Chicago book's own link south — Redfield, Parsons (Chicago Press) and Spicer (a Chicago PhD) mean the University of Chicago is the period's American clearing-house for Mexican folk religion. A Chicago module can start in a Hyde Park seminar room with field notes that mention the Tepozteco's house on the cliff.

Part G — Folk saints and devotions of the north and centre

The Santo Niño de Atocha of Plateros — the Child who walks at night (devotion from the 18th century)

  • Where: Plateros, near Fresnillo, Zacatecas (UNCEDED — Zacatecas is not among the ceded states; the silver belt runs into it) — a Poor Clares shrine.
  • What happened: The Spanish legend: in Moorish-ruled Atocha, when only children under twelve could bring food to prisoners, an unknown child dressed as a pilgrim fed the prisoners at night. The devotion came to the Zacatecas mines; at Plateros a local mine-owner's imported statue of Our Lady of Atocha (a church dated 1789 in popular sources) was eclipsed by the Child she holds. Miners' lore says the Child came to men trapped underground, gave them water and showed them the way out; the sisters say they find the Infant's shoes dusty in the morning "from being out all night caring for pilgrims" — devotees bring him new shoes. The shrine's walls are covered with painted tin ex-voto retablos.
  • The odd detail: worn-out shoes as a miracle — the saint's shoes are replaced because he walks.
  • People: the Poor Clares of Plateros (keepers).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Infant_of_Atocha · (search: https://mexicounexplained.com/the-santo-nino-and-the-miracles-at-plateros/)
  • Confidence: High on the devotion and legends as legends; Medium on "1554" and "1789" (popular sources).
  • Divergence check: holds; Zacatecas UNCEDED — but the devotion's miners work both sides of the line (and Chimayó, New Mexico has its own Niño, US SIDE).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): living devotion — the Child is not a monster. The hook: an American mining company in the ceded north (Chihuahua silver) loses a shift in a collapse, and the survivors — Mexican miners — describe a child with a lamp. The company's report calls it hysteria; IHS calls it something else; the Bureau wants to know why the company sealed the gallery afterward.

Juan Soldado, the executed soldier who became a saint — 13–17 February 1938

  • Where: Tijuana, Baja California (status in the setting OPEN — Baja is not named among the ceded states, though Mexicali's American-owned cotton lands are named in ALTERNATE_HISTORY §7.4).
  • What happened: On 13 February 1938 eight-year-old Olga Camacho Martínez disappeared; her body was found mutilated. A mob, possibly including her family, set fire to the police station and the city hall and kept the firemen back. A 24-year-old private, Juan Castillo Morales, was court-martialled and on 17 February 1938 shot under the ley fuga (told to run, then shot "escaping"). Almost at once people doubted his guilt; before 1938 was out newspapers reported prayers at his grave, blood seeping from it and ghostly voices, and miracles. He became Juan Soldado, patron of migrants and the wrongly accused, at the Puerta Blanca cemetery (Panteón Número Uno).
  • The odd detail: a man executed for a child's murder was being prayed to within the year, on the principle that those who die unjustly sit closest to God.
  • People: Juan Castillo Morales — the executed soldier; Olga Camacho — the victim; Paul J. Vanderwood — historian (Juan Soldado: Rapist, Murderer, Martyr, Saint, Duke UP, 2004).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Soldado · (search: https://dukeupress.edu/juan-soldado)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: real 1938 event on the border; BENDS only if Baja is American (open). Tijuana in 1938 is the American drinkers' town across from San Diego either way.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): handle with care — a murdered child and a real devotion. Three layers: the period saw a lynch mob and a ley fuga; the folklore saw an innocent martyr; the setting may leave guilt unresolved and put its layer on who (or what) actually took Olga, which neither the mob nor the saint's devotees ever asked.

Jesús Malverde, the bandit-saint of Culiacán — "hanged 3 May 1909" (LEGEND; existence unverified)

  • Where: Culiacán, Sinaloa (UNCEDED).
  • What happened: Legend makes Jesús Malverde (b. "1870") a Robin Hood bandit under Governor Francisco Cañedo, hanged on 3 May 1909 and left unburied to rot — people threw stones on the spot until a cairn rose, then a chapel. His existence is not historically verified; Sam Quinones suggests he fuses the real bandits Heraclio Bernal (1855–1888) and Felipe Bachomo (1883–1916). The chapel dates from the 1970s; his association with drug traffickers is later still.
  • The odd detail: a saint's shrine that began as a pile of stones thrown on an unburied body.
  • People: Jesús Malverde — legendary; Heraclio Bernal, Felipe Bachomo — real bandits.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jes%C3%BAs_Malverde
  • Confidence: Low on the man; FLAG: no 1930s attestation of the devotion found; the "narco-saint" is late 20th century — do not use that layer in the period.
  • Divergence check: UNCEDED; a 1909 legend would be local living memory in 1930s Sinaloa if it existed then.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): only as a rumour in Culiacán's rock pile — a period GM can use "stones thrown on the unburied" without the modern cult.

Pedro Linares's fever-dream monsters — the alebrijes, 1936; and the Holy Saturday Judases

  • Where: Mexico City (the La Merced / cartonero trade) — UNCEDED.
  • What happened: Pedro Linares López (29 June 1906 – 25 January 1992) was a cartonero who made piñatas, carnival masks and the papier-mâché Judas figures burned (often with firecrackers) on Holy Saturday, and figures for Diego Rivera and Frida Kahlo. In 1936, aged 30, he fell into high fever and unconsciousness from peritonitis and dreamed of a strange forest full of polychrome creatures — a winged donkey, a lion with a dog's head, a rooster with bull's horns — that shouted one word: "¡Alebrijes!" On recovering he built them in paper. The form became famous only with Judith Bronowski's 1975 film.
  • The odd detail: the monsters named themselves — the word alebrije exists because the dream-creatures shouted it.
  • People: Pedro Linares — cartonero; Diego Rivera, Frida Kahlo — customers.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_Linares · (search: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alebrije)
  • Confidence: High on the 1936 illness and dream as Linares told it (his own account).
  • Divergence check: holds; UNCEDED capital.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the fae — the one threat the lane otherwise lacks. A 1936 fever, a forest that is not in Mexico, creatures that name themselves: the setting can read it as a man who went somewhere and came back, and whose paper figures (sold to Rivera's circle) are descriptions. Linares himself stays an artisan, never a villain.

The lechuza, the owl-witch of the north (FOLKLORE; no pre-1940 print attestation found this session)

  • Where: South Texas and northern Mexico (US SIDE and CEDED NORTH) — the Rio Grande Valley, Robstown, Alice.
  • What happened: The legend: a woman — often falsely accused of witchcraft when a child disappears — makes a pact with the Devil and returns as a huge owl with a human face, preying on men walking alone at night (drunk, lecherous or abusive men in modern tellings). The Spanish lechuza is the barn owl; owls as death-omens and witches' forms are widespread in Mexican lore (Bourke, 1894, records the witches and animal death-omens of the Rio Grande but not the owl by name). The best-documented dated episode is late: a 1975 wave of sightings near Robstown, Texas of a bird 2–6 feet tall with a human face.
  • The odd detail: in the modern telling the witch was innocent first — the owl is what false accusation made.
  • People: Rachel González-Martin — UT Austin folklorist (modern commentator).
  • Sources: https://texasstandard.org/stories/la-lechuza-legend-cautionary-tale-story-revenge/ · https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Popular_medicine,_customs_and_superstitions_of_the_Rio_Grande
  • Confidence: Medium that the owl-witch belief is old (general sources); Low on any dated pre-1940 attestation — FLAG and see Leads (Texas Folk-Lore Society volumes, 1920s–30s).
  • Divergence check: both sides of the line; unchanged by the occupation.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the period frame for anything that kills livestock or men at night in the ceded north (instead of the anachronistic chupacabra). Keep the tradition's own moral: the accusers made her.

Tomóchic, the village that rose for a saint — December 1891 – October 1892 (living memory in the north)

  • Where: Tomóchic, Sierra Tarahumara, Chihuahua (CEDED NORTH).
  • What happened: Villagers of Tomóchic, devotees of Teresa Urrea (above) under the leader Cruz Chávez, refused the Díaz government's authority in the name of God and the Santa de Cabora and defeated the first troops sent against them. In October 1892 the federal army razed the village; about 300 villagers were dead by the end of that year. The young officer Heriberto Frías published the novel Tomóchic (1893–94) exposing the army's conduct and was punished for it.
  • The odd detail: the village fought in the name of a healer who was not there — she had been deported that June.
  • People: Cruz Chávez — village leader; Teresa Urrea — invoked, absent; Heriberto Frías — soldier-novelist.
  • Sources: https://wrldrels.org/2021/07/05/teresa-urrea-la-santa-de-cabora/ · (search summary: https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/urrea-teresa) — M01 lists Tomóchic as a lead.
  • Confidence: High on dates/deaths as given; Cruz Chávez and Frías from general knowledge — the Wikipedia Tomóchic page returned 404 this session (Medium).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916; CEDED NORTH — in the setting a 1930s Chihuahuan's grandfather's massacre is on American soil now.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the razed village's church site as a place the occupation's surveyors will not map. Cult versus population: Tomóchic was a population, not a cult; the setting's thing in the Sierra is older than either army.
