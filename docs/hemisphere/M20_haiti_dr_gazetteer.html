M20 — A GM's gazetteer of Port-au-Prince, Cap-Haïtien, the Plateau and Artibonite, Santo Domingo and the Dominican sugar coast, 1915–1940

  • Lane: M20 — the places an investigator team would walk through in occupied Haiti and the occupied Dominican Republic. Format follows M11 (### <Place> — <where>). Events at these places (the Banque Nationale's gold, Sam's last day, Fort Rivière, the corvée, Péralte's door, Batraville, Hasco's zombies, Felix-Mentor, San Zenón, the Parsley Massacre, Papá Liborio, the gavilleros) are in M04 and are only cross-referenced here.
  • Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (research agent) · Brief: BRIEF.md in this folder + the Chicago sweep's BRIEF.md.
  • Setting sources: the brief's compressed canon and M04's divergence notes (Haiti and the DR are both occupied before the 1916 break, so the landings "happened"; after it the occupations never end; Audit 151 rules out a Haitian withdrawal; the DR's 1924 withdrawal, the 1930 Trujillo coup and the 1937 border massacre are open questions for the owner, M04 Part G).

Overview

39 place entries. The occupation left a map in print, and the books give it: in 1919–20 the Marine barracks and the Gendarmerie caserne face each other below the National Palace, the Marine brigade headquarters sits above the Champ de Mars (Kuser), and Seabrook puts the Brigade in the old palace of the murdered President Sam, next to the French legation where he was dragged out in 1915 — the killing ground and the headquarters are one block. The harbour is entered on two range lights, one on the cathedral's tower and one on Fort National (Kuser); in 1920 only two buildings in Port-au-Prince have window-glass ("one might perhaps say in all Haiti"), the cathedral and the unfinished palace (Franck) ⟨fixed: V_M20: Franck hedges the country-wide claim⟩. In the north, Franck reports (1920) that the occupation forces "are threatening to tear down" Sans-Souci's walls to build barracks, and that Péralte was given five fake funerals in five places so that the real grave would not become a shrine. The Hotel Oloffson — Wikipedia says — was a Marine hospital for the whole occupation and became a hotel only in 1935, which the setting's never-ending occupation breaks (a ready-made divergence). In the Dominican Republic, the wrecked USS Memphis was still sitting on the rocks beside Santo Domingo's seaside promenade in early 1920 (Franck), the Alcázar of Columbus was a slum-ringed ruin until its 1955–57 restoration, Monte Cristi's iron clock tower produced an American bill for $16,000 (Franck's anecdote), and La Romana was one American company's estate of about a quarter-million acres with 7,500–9,000 workers, "a bare hundred" of them Americans, mostly Haitians and Puerto Ricans (Franck) — the mill Wikipedia says Trujillo never took. Respect: this file describes towns and buildings, not religions. Vodou, Liborismo and Catholic folk devotion are not the "cult" of any entry; the setting's monsters are an overlay on the record, and the real dead (the 1937 victims, the corvée dead, the caco dead) are never scenery.

Part 1 — Port-au-Prince

Port-au-Prince: the city and its harbour — the Gulf of Gonâve, west of the Cul-de-Sac plain

  • What it was in the occupation years: A capital of "between 80,000 and 90,000 inhabitants" (the 1920 guide's figure) lying "at the bottom of a deep gulf" facing west under mountains of about 2,800 feet. Ships came in by alignment with two huge red range lights, "one on the top of the Cathedral and the other on Fort National"; a launch brought the customs officials alongside, passports were taken and kept overnight, and the dock was "at the end of a long pier jutting out from the land" (Kuser). The streets "throughout most of the town" had been put down under contract with an American firm in 1914, before the occupation, and were "of excellent quality" (Kuser); Franck (1920) found them "excellently paved with asphalt", "scores of automobiles", a small old-fashioned railway through the heart of the town, an electric-light service over the whole city, barefoot street-sweepers in palm-leaf hats. The old French street plan was wide but with poor sidewalks; the houses were mostly wooden, "built as open as possible to every breeze". The 1920 guide says an American colony of "several hundred" (not counting the forces of occupation) lived largely at La Coupe in the upper town, because the city "hangs around the nineties".
  • Why an investigator goes there: Everything the occupation is — the Brigade, the Gendarmerie, the Financial Adviser, the Legation quarter, the customs — is in one small city that can be walked in an afternoon.
  • The odd detail: The harbour is entered on a line between a church and a fort — the two lights are the cathedral's north tower and Fort National (Kuser; the cathedral's Wikipedia page independently says its north tower's cupola was the front light of a pair, via fetch summary).
  • Sources: Kuser 1921 (ch. 1, the arrival); Franck 1920 (Haiti chapters); 1920 West Indies guide (Port-au-Prince section); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Our_Lady_of_the_Assumption,_Port-au-Prince (via fetch summary); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port-au-Prince (via fetch summary — says the occupied city "garrisoned by American Marines and Haitian gendarmes, was attacked twice by caco rebels", 1919 and 1920)
  • Confidence: High for what the books print (a 1919–20 snapshot, with Franck's line copied into the guide); the population figure is the guide's.
  • Divergence check: holds — a 1919–20 city; the occupation never ends in the setting, so the American paving contract, colony and garrison persist.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A port that steers by a church and a fort. A pilot who says the two lights "have not agreed for a month" is a GM's way of telling players the Church and the Army are no longer pointing at the same channel.

The National Palace — Champ de Mars, north side; "the fourth palace on the same site"

  • What it was in the occupation years: Designed by the Paris-trained Haitian architect Georges Baussan (1874–1958), budget $350,000, work begun May 1914; the unfinished building was, per Wikipedia's fetch summary, "partially destroyed by a mob" after Sam's death in 1915, and finished in 1920 by U.S. naval engineers: "white-painted reinforced concrete", two storeys, central domed pavilion with matching end pavilions. Kuser (1919–20) says it is "the fourth palace to be built on the same site, one of the others having been set on fire and destroyed, and the other two ruined through explosions" — the presidents "killed by means of their own powder", because a president "had been unable to trust anyone with the keeping of the national supply of ammunition and was forced to keep it in his own palace". One wing was already occupied by the President and his family. Franck: "unfinished, snow-white", "larger and more sumptuous than our own White House", one of the two glazed buildings in Port-au-Prince ("perhaps … in all Haiti") ⟨fixed: V_M20⟩. Seabrook (1929): a "great white glaring stairway" up past sentries, the palace "more permanently and safely inhabited by his Excellency Louis Borno" than his predecessors'. Wikipedia: the 2010 quake wrecked it; demolished 2012.
  • Why an investigator goes there: The seat of the client president. In 1920 it was still a construction site with a president camped in one wing, so a GM can put plaster, scaffolding and American engineers in the halls.
  • The odd detail: The previous palace exploded with a president inside (8 August 1912 — Wikipedia, via fetch summary: President Leconte). The new palace was built by the Americans around a burnt-out shell of a lynch mob's work.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Palace_(Haiti) (via fetch summary); Kuser 1921; Franck 1920; Seabrook 1929 (ch. on the palace)
  • Confidence: High on Baussan, dates, dimensions-as-described; the "partially destroyed by a mob in 1915" is the fetch summary's wording — check.
  • Divergence check: the building holds (1914–20; pre-break conception; the Navy that finished it in 1920 is Marshall's, not Wilson's). BENDS for who lives in it after 1930 (M04 Part G: Vincent as a client president under a permanent High Commissioner, or direct rule) — open.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A palace whose predecessors kept the powder magazine in the president's own house. The Americans built a palace that has, on paper, no magazine — and a GM decides what is kept in the Marines' own strongroom beneath it.

The Champ de Mars — the parade ground between the palace and the old Sam palace

  • What it was in the occupation years: "A laid-out park with a bronze of Dessalines, the 'Founder of Haitian Independence,' in the center; and at the end a grandstand" (Kuser); Franck, in the sun: "vast sun-scorched", bare, with "a Napoleonic statue of Dessalines, his telltale complexion disguised by the kindly bronze" and "a modest and deeply tanned Venus of Melos", with a legend that it had once been wooded until a former president had it cleared of assassins' hiding-places. By 1929 Seabrook found "a bandstand, lawns, monuments, drill grounds, and boulevards", and every Easter the square filled with straw-stuffed effigies of Judas and Pilate's soldiers that the crowds, machetes in hand, tore up (a Catholic folk custom, in Seabrook's telling, where "both Americans and upper-class Haitians" watched). The Club Port-au-Prince (a young men's club) stood on the square "next to Brigade Headquarters" (Kuser). The Wikipedia page for the square returned nothing.
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is the one open space in the capital, so a parade, a march, a funeral or a riot must pass through it; the palace, the Brigade and the legation face it.
  • The odd detail: Seabrook's own juxtaposition: the Easter crowd dragging effigies through the square in 1928 is "the same identical crowd" that in 1915 tore Sam apart at the legation and "dragged his mangled torso at the end of a rope through this same Champs de Mars". (His reading; the Easter rite itself is a Catholic custom, not "voodoo".)
  • Sources: Kuser 1921; Franck 1920; Seabrook 1929 (chs. on the Brigade and "The Soul of Haiti")
  • Confidence: High for the books; the Easter effigies are Seabrook's eyewitness.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A square built to have no hiding-places, used once a year to destroy a man made of straw. A murderer's gift to a GM: the effigy that is heavier than straw. (The rite is Catholic folk practice; the killer is the villain.)

The old Sam palace, the French legation and the Marine headquarters — the block beside the Champ de Mars

  • What it was in the occupation years: Two witnesses, two placings. Kuser (1919–20): "On the lower side of the palace are the marine barracks and the gendarme caserne, opposite one another, and above the Champ de Mars is the marine brigade headquarters." Seabrook (1929): "The Brigade was installed precisely in the old Guillaume Sam palace before whose gates, only ten years before, a black woman had been biting chunks out of that defunct president's bleeding heart", a "three-minute walk across the tranquil Champs de Mars" from the new palace; and, describing the Easter crowd, "the palace of the late Guillaume Sam; beside it stood the French legation in its walled garden". Kuser's own rented house on Avenue Christophe "had at one time been used as the Presidential palace" (the capital's presidential houses were interchangeable).
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is where a Marine officer decides whether a Haitian is a bandit. The provost's files, the brigade commander (Col. Russell, later High Commissioner), the Gendarmerie chief's office and the legation that could not protect its guest are within one block.
  • The odd detail: The legation and the headquarters are neighbours: the wall Sam was thrown over in July 1915 (M04) is the wall of the compound where the Americans afterwards sat. Kuser and Seabrook do not agree exactly on which building held the Brigade; both agree it is beside the Champ de Mars.
  • Sources: Kuser 1921; Seabrook 1929; M04 (Sam's last day)
  • Confidence: Medium-High (two eyewitnesses, one place-name difference; they fit if the old Sam palace stands "above" the square).
  • Divergence check: holds — a 1915–20 fact; in the setting the Brigade never withdraws.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A headquarters in a dead president's house, next to the legation where he begged for sanctuary. A witness hidden in the French legation's garden in 1936 is hidden beside the Marines' own office: the cheapest and most dangerous refuge in the city.

Fort National and the long pier — the harbour front

  • What it was in the occupation years: Fort National carried the second red range light for the channel (Kuser); the dock lay at the end of "a long pier". In the real record the Germans left in Haiti were held at Fort National under American guard after Haiti entered the war in 1918 (M04, from Wikipedia — UNVERIFIED here). Franck saw even "along the waterfront the principles of sanitation are enforced". The Marines' route past the slaughterhouse on the waterfront was the caco column's road on 15 January 1920 (next-but-one entry).
  • Why an investigator goes there: The wharf is where cargo, mail, cable and people come in; the fort is where the Marines put people they have not decided about.
  • The odd detail: The harbour's second light is a prison-fort — the entrance to the capital is a place where people were held.
  • Sources: Kuser 1921; Franck 1920; M04 (German colony)
  • Confidence: Medium-Low: a range light and a passing mention; nothing on the fort's interior.
  • Divergence check: holds; BENDS for the German prisoners — America never fights Germany, so nobody is interned in 1918 (M04).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A German merchant house on the pier with an American guard at its own dock: the Circle's or Berlin's warehouse, watched by the wrong navy.

The cathedral and its square — Notre-Dame de l'Assomption, facing the harbour

  • What it was in the occupation years: Begun 1884, finished 1914, formally dedicated 13 December 1928 (via fetch summary); its north tower carried the front range light. Franck, 1920: "large and rather imposing", "light yellow both outside and within, flooded with the aggressive tropical sunshine" that left it none of the "dim and mystic light" of a church; one of the two glazed buildings in Port-au-Prince ("perhaps … in all Haiti") ⟨fixed: V_M20⟩; it "looks down upon the blue harbor across a great open square unadorned with a single sprig of vegetation". Kuser's photograph captions show "the open market just below the cathedral". The 2010 quake killed Archbishop Miot in the ruins (via fetch summary; out of window).
  • Why an investigator goes there: The archdiocese (the Church's Haitian seat), the confessors of the elite, the market on its steps, and the place where the harbour light can be read.
  • The odd detail: The building was in daily use for a decade before it was formally dedicated (1914 → 1928); the Church's own Haitian mission ran alongside the occupation.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Our_Lady_of_the_Assumption,_Port-au-Prince (via fetch summary); Franck 1920; Kuser 1921
  • Confidence: High for dates and description; the fetch gave no architect and no murals.
  • Divergence check: holds. The IHS (Catholic) presence is a setting given; the 1939–42 anti-superstition campaign (M04) BENDS — under the setting's permanent occupation it would be run beneath the occupation's eye.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers: the Church's line on Vodou, the IHS's, and what the cathedral's archive knows. The IHS's Port-au-Prince desk lives in the ecclesiastical quarter behind the cathedral; the harbour-light tower is where it watches the ships.

The museum of the Petit Séminaire — Port-au-Prince

  • What it was in the occupation years: One sentence of the 1920 guide: "In the museum of the Petit Séminaire, at Port-au-Prince, one may see objects which were used not only in the practice of the black art of voodooism, but in cannibal ceremonies which are the outgrowth of the former." (The "cannibal" claim is the guide's period slander; the guide offers no evidence, and Niles (1926) — quoting Haitian hosts — calls the 1860s "cannibalism" charge a humiliation and says Haitians "do not eat people raw".) I found no other source and could not confirm which school's museum this was (the Petit Séminaire Collège Saint-Martial of the Holy Ghost Fathers is my guess — UNVERIFIED).
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is a Church-held collection of ritual objects. The IHS's reference library on Haitian religion would sit exactly here.
  • The odd detail: The objects in a Catholic museum were, in 1920, already treated as evidence of sorcery. In 1939–42 the anti-superstition campaign (M04) made more.
  • Sources: 1920 West Indies guide (Port-au-Prince section)
  • Confidence: Low: single sentence, single guide.
  • Divergence check: holds; the collection predates 1916.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Respect: these are sacred objects of a living religion, held by a Church museum. A manbo or oungan has a real claim on them and is the expert; the "cannibal" label is a source's slur. The hook is a curator IHS priest who is quietly returning items.

The Iron Market and the open markets — the Marché en Fer and the cathedral-square market

  • What it was in the occupation years: The Iron Market (Marché en Fer; also Marché Hyppolite, Marché Vallières) is, in Wikipedia's fetch summary, a metal building made in Paris "originally intended for a railway station in Cairo", bought by President Florvil Hyppolite and brought to Haiti in 1891: two iron-framed halls of 2,000 m² each, joined by a gate with four domed towers and a clock, painted red with green accents; "burned several times". (The popular Eiffel attribution is not in the article — UNVERIFIED.) The 1920 guide calls Port-au-Prince's market-places "large and well worth visiting", where country people come in "with fruits and vegetables, sometimes with meats" ("the meats are poor, the fruits delicious"; "mutton" usually goat); Kuser's photograph captions show a separate open market under the cathedral.
  • Why an investigator goes there: Everything moves through the market: news, goods, rumours, women's credit networks, ritual supplies. A market is the fastest place to find a person who would not be found at an address.
  • The odd detail: A building bought for a station in a different continent and re-used as a market — perhaps the image of the receivership city: foreign iron, local trade.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March%C3%A9_en_Fer (via fetch summary); 1920 West Indies guide; Kuser 1921 (captions)
  • Confidence: Medium (one fetch summary on the building; the market's 1915–40 life not read).
  • Divergence check: holds (1891; the fires and the 2008/2010 events are out of window).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The market women are the real intelligence service of any Haitian city: a GM can make a marchande an ally who knows which Marine's cook buys what. A Circle agent who wants to move something invisibly puts it in a sack of charcoal.

The Grand Cimetière — Port-au-Prince (PLACE NOT SOURCED — see Leads)

  • What it was in the occupation years: I found no source that describes this cemetery in 1915–40; Wikipedia has no article under the titles tried. What the sources I read do give: in Seabrook's transcription of a rite, "Baron Samedi, ruler of the cemetery", is invoked; and M04's Vodou entry records that the Gede are "the family of death and the dead". The annual feast of the Gede on 1–2 November is widely reported but UNVERIFIED this session; so are the cemetery's founding date, its tombs and its Gede crosses.
  • Why an investigator goes there: Every death, every burial, every exhumation order, every missing corpse in the capital ends here. A police or Marine file that says "buried" means this ground.
  • The odd detail: None sourced. (A GM should not import the modern tourist descriptions — the painted tombs and the crosses of Baron Samedi — without a period source.)
  • Sources: Seabrook 1929 (rite text); M04 (Gede basics)
  • Confidence: Low. A named gap, not a description.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The cemetery is where the zombi accusation (M04: an accusation about who profits from whose labour) meets the paper record: a burial register with a name whose grave the family says is empty. Respect: the Gede are a living family of lwa; the villain is whoever forges the register.

The Hotel Oloffson — Port-au-Prince (street address not given by my source) ("verify the 1930s role" — done against one source only)

  • What it was in the occupation years: Per Wikipedia (via fetch summary): a Haitian-style gingerbread mansion built in 1887, the Sam family's private house (commissioned by Demosthenes Simon Sam, son of President Tirésias Simon Sam), by a French architect named Lefèvre; "leased by the Sam family to the Marine Corps as a military hospital for the duration of the occupation from 1915 to 1934"; and converted to a hotel in 1935 by Werner Gustav Oloffson, "a Swedish sea captain from Germany", with his wife Margot and sons Olaf and Egon (the "Greenwich Village of the Tropics" fame, Graham Greene's The Comedians, 1966, and the arson of 6 July 2025 are outside the window). None of my period books names the Oloffson (zero hits in six texts), so this is one source.
  • Why an investigator goes there: In 1915–34 a Marine hospital in a president's family house; from 1935 the capital's most talked-about hotel, kept by a German-born Swede.
  • The odd detail: The house that treated the Marines who fought the cacos was a hotel within a year of the occupation ending — a chronology the setting cannot keep.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotel_Oloffson (via fetch summary)
  • Confidence: Medium (no period corroboration; "Swedish sea captain from Germany" as the summary words it). ⟨fixed: V_M20: two modern sources agree — Wikipedia and Haiti's tourism site https://visithaiti.com/history-heritage/visit-the-oloffson-hotel/ both say a US military hospital until the occupation ended in 1934 and a hotel from 1935 under Werner Gustav Oloffson; "from Germany" is Wikipedia only (Visit Haiti says "Swedish sea captain"); Wikipedia says the Sams "leased" it, Visit Haiti says it was "seized"; the address is 60 Avenue Christophe (Wikipedia infobox)⟩
  • Divergence check: BENDS. With no 1934 withdrawal, a Marine hospital "for the duration" never ends, so the house never becomes a hotel in 1935 — unless the owner rules the hospital wing and a captain's guest-house coexist. If a Germany that won the Great War puts a "sea captain from Germany" in the capital's best house, it becomes the setting's first "Germany contests the hemisphere" address (§8.6, open).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The hospital ward records — the Marines' fever cases, "nervous" cases, the men who came back from the hills — are a document any faction wants, kept in a house built for the family of a murdered president. And a Swedish-German captain who arrives in 1935 and asks for the top floor.

Hotels and clubs of the capital — the port hotel, the Champ de Mars hotel, the Cercle Bellevue and the Club Port-au-Prince

  • What it was in the occupation years: The 1920 guide: "no very good hotel in the city. There is one near the port and one on the verge of the city, at the Champ de Mars, which is old and ramshackle, but its meals are — or were — excellent." Kuser: two clubs — the Cercle Bellevue ("the more representative", "a beautiful building in the upper part of town", members including "certain Americans" as well as Haitians) and the Port-au-Prince, "a young men's club" on the Champ de Mars beside Brigade Headquarters. The brief's Hotel Excelsior appears in none of the six period texts I searched — UNVERIFIED; I did not find a source for the name in Haiti.
  • Why an investigator goes there: Where the elite and the officers meet without a Marine present at the door.
  • The odd detail: The old ramshackle hotel sits at the Champ de Mars, on the parade ground beside the palace: a guest looks out on the Brigade.
  • Sources: 1920 West Indies guide; Kuser 1921
  • Confidence: Medium (1919–20 only).
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The club that admits "certain Americans" is where the colour line and the class line are drawn in one membership list; a Section 13 agent can read the list as an org chart.

The town's edge on 15 January 1920 — the Hasco Road, the slaughterhouse and Belair hill

  • What it was in the occupation years: Kuser was in the town for the caco raid at 4 a.m. on 15 January 1920 ("3,000 cacos are marching into town by the Hasco Road"): about a quarter-mile from town the column divided: one along the water front by the slaughterhouse, the other two inland "around Belleair hill, by the radio station". Marines "opened fire with Brownings and machine guns"; the cacos fought from corners and windows and fired some of the native "cailles" in the poor section. By daybreak "over 150 were captured or killed", three Marines wounded, one fatally; survivors were driven "into a closed valley back of Belleair" and machine-gunned. Solomen Janvier was found asleep in his own house and boasted every attack on the capital had been his. Numbers were 3,000, then 1,500 ("as claimed by the men at Hasco"), then Kuser's own guess of about 300 actual fighters; the market rumour next morning said 2,000,000 cacos and 1,000,000 dead. M04 (from Wikipedia) has this as Batraville's Bel-Air raid, "over 300 killed or captured". The two figures differ.
  • Why an investigator goes there: It fixes three places (Hasco Road, the slaughterhouse, Belair hill with a radio station) that a night-time cordon or an ambush would use.
  • The odd detail: A radio station on the hill above the poor quarter in 1920 — the Marines' wireless was on the caco route.
  • Sources: Kuser 1921 (ch. "Cacos"); M04 (Batraville entry)
  • Confidence: High for the eyewitness; the casualty count differs from M04's — flag.
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The hill with the radio station is where a GM puts a listening post — and a place from which someone can see the town burn. The "2,000,000 cacos" rumour is a period model of how a rumour becomes a legend overnight.

Hasco — the Haitian-American Sugar Company, eastern suburbs, at the head of the Cul-de-Sac plain

  • What it was in the occupation years: Seabrook (1929): "an immense factory plant, dominated by a huge chimney, with clanging machinery, steam whistles, freight cars. It is like a chunk of Hoboken. It lies in the eastern suburbs of Port-au-Prince, and beyond it stretch the cane fields of the Cul-de-Sac. Hasco makes rum when the sugar market is off, pays low wages, twenty or thirty cents a day, and gives steady work." Kuser calls it "the sugar plant of the American-Haitian Sugar Company" on the road the cacos used. In Seabrook's book the roads out of it lead to Croix-des-Bouquets (Saturday-night bamboche, Sunday cockfights) and the mountains near the Dominican border, where the "zombies" of the 1918 story came from (M04: the Hasco zombies — folklore, as Seabrook's informant told it).
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is the occupation's economy in one place: an American company, a payroll, a labour supply of peasants pulled off the land.
  • The odd detail: Seabrook chose it because it is "the last name anybody would think of connecting with either sorcery or superstition" — the setting's most modern address is the one the zombi legend attaches to.
  • Sources: Seabrook 1929 (ch. "Dead Men Working in the Cane Fields"); Kuser 1921; M04 (Hasco zombies)
  • Confidence: High that it existed as described; the 1918 events are folklore.
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era; in the setting the company's American ownership is unaffected.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Hasco payroll of 1918 that lists workers who never gave names. Respect: the zombi is a Haitian belief about being made a slave; the villain is the ledger-keeper, not Vodou.

Pétion-Ville and La Coupe — the hill suburbs above the Cul-de-Sac, about 1,200 ft

  • What it was in the occupation years: Wikipedia (via fetch summary): founded 1831 by President Boyer and named for Alexandre Pétion; a residential and touristic hill town; the Club de Pétion-Ville was built "in the 1930s" (nine-hole golf, tennis, a pool); the Cabane Choucoune dance club was built in 1940 by Max Ewald. The 1920 guide: La Coupe, the upper-town district of wealthy natives and most of the American colony, "at an altitude of 1,200 feet", cooler, "some fine residences … embowered among palms, breadfruit, mangoes", a natural bath under lofty trees. Kuser rode from Pétion-Ville up mountain trails with mules and gendarme guides. Seabrook lived in a "dilapidated but beautiful garden of several acres" and "faded pink" house on a lane running toward Pétion-Ville, remembers a Marine officer's villa there where they played bridge, and names Dr Price-Mars as "of Petionville"; the market "behind the church" is where he bargained for gifts (M04 for Price-Mars).
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is where the elite and the Americans sleep, out of the capital's heat and (they believe) its rumours.
  • The odd detail: The Pétion-Ville club was built in the 1930s — a golf course made by the occupation era's money, on the ridge above the cane plain.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%A9tion-Ville (via fetch summary); 1920 West Indies guide; Kuser 1921; Seabrook 1929
  • Confidence: High for the books; Medium for the 1930s items.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Jean Price-Mars (M04) lives up here: the Haitian expert the Americans never ask. An investigator who takes the road up La Coupe with a bottle for the ethnologist is a scene.

Bizoton — the road south along the bay (a village, a landing, a famous trial, a diving base)

  • What it was in the occupation years: Niles (1926): Bizoton-les-Bains, "a tiny village on the road out of Port-au-Prince, along the north shore of the southern peninsula", small wooden houses with corrugated-iron roofs among thatched cottages; the poet Oswald Durand once lived there; gas-girls patrol the streets selling kerosene and the peasants light torches "out along the Bizoton Road"; it lies with Carrefour beyond. Niles also mentions "the famous cannibalism case at Bizoton" tried under President Geffrard — a sensational 19th-century prosecution (the killing was in December 1863 and the eight condemned were executed on 13 February 1864; historians dispute the case — no trial transcripts survive and the confessions were obtained under torture: https://www.executedtoday.com/2014/02/13/1864-bizoton-affair-executions/ and https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-trial-that-gave-vodou-a-bad-name-83801276/) ⟨fixed: V_M20⟩ that Haitian hosts told her had been used to slander their country. M04 places the 28 July 1915 landing "at Bizoton/Port-au-Prince". William Beebe's Beneath Tropic Seas (1928) photographs a "Haitian Shore at Bizoton and the Coral Reefs": his 1927 diving expedition worked from here (Beebe not read beyond the captions).
  • Why an investigator goes there: A short ride out the shore road from the palace — the Marines' landing beach, a seaside resort of a few houses, and a bay of coral.
  • The odd detail: One small village holds the most notorious "Vodou" trial in the foreign press, the Marines' first landing beach and a scientific diving base — three layers of one place.
  • Sources: Niles 1926 (ch. on Oswald Durand and the Emperor's statue); Beebe 1928 (captions only: https://archive.org/details/beneathtropicsea00beeb); M04 (the landing)
  • Confidence: Medium: Bizoton's existence and the case's mention are Niles's; the trial's date and the landing site are not confirmed here.
  • Divergence check: the landing (1915) and the case (1860s) hold; the 1927 expedition is unaffected.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers of truth: the trial as foreign press told it, as Haitians remember it (a smear), and what a GM decides was really buried at Bizoton. Respect: the 1860s "cannibalism" was used to justify contempt of Haiti; the hook is the accuser, not the accused religion.

Part 2 — Cap-Haïtien and the north

Cap-Haïtien — "the Cape", the capital of the north, on its open bay

  • What it was in the occupation years: Founded 1670 as Cap-Français by a dozen colonists under Bertrand d'Ogeron; capital of French Saint-Domingue 1711–1770; nearly destroyed by the 7 May 1842 earthquake (est. magnitude 8.1; "about 10,000 dead" is the Cap-Haïtien article's figure, but the earthquake's own Wikipedia article gives 5,300 — the sources conflict ⟨fixed: V_M20⟩); the site of the Battle of Vertières, 18 November 1803 (Wikipedia via fetch summary). Wikipedia adds one occupation fact: its north–south streets were renamed with single letters by the Americans. Franck (1920): "the second city of Haiti, sometimes called the 'capital of the North'", once "the Paris of America", now with "old stone ruins" stretching out to the lighthouse, wide streets still stone-paved with French names, a cinema, business houses "in most cases owned by foreigners", the acrid smell of raw coffee spread on canvas in the squares and old women "thigh-deep in coffee" in the warehouses, a large market, a "blue vaulted cathedral", "American marines are everywhere in evidence", and a short railway (about twenty miles) to Grande-Rivière and Bahon. Franck also describes election day under the Marines: each voter dips his right forefinger in nitrate of silver (some district commanders use iodine) so that no man votes twice.
  • Why an investigator goes there: The north's capital and port — the gateway to the Citadelle, Milot, Fort Rivière and the caco country; the place FDR came in July 1934 (M04).
  • The odd detail: The election was policed by a black fingernail — a stain that stays on the hand until election day is over, and which the mixed-race elite ("gens de couleur") tried to cheat by dipping the wrong finger (Franck's telling, with his condescension).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cap-Ha%C3%AFtien (via fetch summary); Franck 1920 (ch. VI–VII)
  • Confidence: High for the books (1919–20); Medium for the Wikipedia items.
  • Divergence check: holds; FDR's 5 July 1934 visit (M04) BENDS — he comes as a president inspecting a colony, not to sign a withdrawal.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A city ruined by a quake and a revolution and still wearing its French names; a GM's ghost town inside a working port. The silver-nitrate stain is a ready-made clue: someone has no black nail.

The bay, the reef and Petite Anse — the Cap's coast

  • What it was in the occupation years: Franck: the open bay "in which the winds frequently play havoc"; on its outer reef "the flagship of Columbus came to grief on Christmas eve, 1492"; the wreckage was "brought ashore near the present fisher village of Petit Anse, and here was erected the first European fort in the New World" (the guide's claim; the exact site of La Navidad is a scholarly question — UNVERIFIED here). The road from the capital came down through the Plaisance valley; the ford of the Limbé could delay motorists for a week.
  • Why an investigator goes there: A fisher village at the mouth of the bay is where a boat would put in unseen; Columbus's fort is the kind of "first" that a relic-hunting faction would dig for.
  • The odd detail: The Cape's "first fort in the New World" is a wreck-fort: the Santa María's timbers made the walls.
  • Sources: Franck 1920
  • Confidence: Medium-Low (one book; the archaeology is not read).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916; holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The IHS's oldest Caribbean story is a wreck and a fort. A digger on the Petite Anse shore in 1936 finds where the ship's timbers were burnt.

Grande-Rivière-du-Nord and Fort Capois — the caco heartland, terminus of the northern railway

  • What it was in the occupation years: Hanneken's own diary entry, as Franck prints it: "Nov. 1, 1919. — Killed Charlemagne Peralte, Commander-in-Chief of the bandits… Brought Charlemagne's body to Grande Riviere, arriving 9 a.m. Went to Cap Haitien with the body. Received orders to proceed to Fort Capois next morning. Went to Grande Riviere via handcar, arriving 9 p.m…. via handcar to Bahon, arriving midnight." Franck, writing in early 1920: "The public display of his body at Grande Riviere and Cap Haitien produced an effect that will not soon be forgotten"; his followers "credit him with supernatural powers", captured men still answer "Mais, c'est Charlemagne", so "it was found necessary to give him five fake funerals in as many different places, as a blind, and to bury his body secretly in the out-of-the-way spot, lest his grave become a shrine of pilgrimage for future cacos." (M04 has the door photograph, Fort Capois near Sainte-Suzanne and the disputed grave near Caracol; Price-Mars was born at Grande-Rivière-du-Nord.)
  • Why an investigator goes there: The northern railway's end and the caco country's centre — where the body was shown, and the first of five "funerals".
  • The odd detail: The five fake funerals — a 1920 eyewitness source confirms the Marines' own decoy-graves plan, so the real grave is a genuine hole in the record.
  • Sources: Franck 1920 (the Hanneken diary and the funerals); M04 (Péralte, Péralte's bones)
  • Confidence: High (a period source printing the diary).
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era. M04's "state funeral 1934/35" BENDS (does not happen); Franck's five decoys make the setting's unmarked grave plausible.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Five decoy funerals for one man; four graves are empty and one is not, and a Marine sergeant in 1937 (M04) remembers which. Every faction wants the bones for its own reason; the Haitians want them for the right one. (Respect: Péralte is a national hero.)

Milot and the palace of Sans-Souci — the road south from the Cape

  • What it was in the occupation years: Franck (1920): from the Cape a wide trail "once the 'royal highway' between Christophe's main palace and Cap Haitien", then Milot, "a small town lined up on each side of the road like people awaiting a procession of royalty", and behind it "a great crumbling ruin which had about it something suggestive of Versailles": five storeys of native brick and plaster with the roof gone, wild oranges growing in the lower halls, two pairs of gate-posts "nearly twenty feet high", the caïmite tree under which the king judged his subjects, a small chapel used as the village church. Then: "Our forces of occupation are threatening to tear down the walls, which are soon likely to fall of themselves, to clear away the vegetation, and to build barracks of the materials that remain." (The palace was wrecked by the 1842 quake — M04.)
  • Why an investigator goes there: A palace ruin on the road to a fortress is a ready-made day-trip and a ready-made cover for an assembly.
  • The odd detail: The occupation's engineers considered quarrying a king's palace for barracks — a threat of 1920 that the setting's never-ending occupation can carry out.
  • Sources: Franck 1920 (ch. VII); M04 (Citadelle and Sans-Souci)
  • Confidence: High for Franck's report (one visitor's statement of a threat, not proof the walls were used).
  • Divergence check: the ruin is pre-1916. BENDS open: whether the barracks were built is a setting choice.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Marines' quarry: what the demolition crews find under the palace floor when the walls come down. (M04's Citadelle hook — the quicklime courtyard.)

The Citadelle Laferrière — Bonnet à l'Évêque peak above Milot

  • What it was in the occupation years: Franck's climb (1920): a trail "wholly uninhabited" on its last two-thirds, "the mighty stone walls, twenty feet thick in many cases", "an endless labyrinth of gloomy chambers, dungeon-like", cannon of Spain, of Queen Elizabeth, of English and French origin (the tradition of 365 guns), pyramids of cannon-balls, "stone basins always full of clear running water, the source of which no man has ever been able to discover", at the top a courtyard with "the mammoth stone vault in which Christophe lies buried", and the long stone platform from which his condemned were thrown. "The captain" who led the party had sent a group of prisoners in "thin striped garments" ahead with the picnic lunch. (M04 has the dates, dimensions, the quicklime and silver-bullet legends and the ruin of Sans-Souci.)
  • Why an investigator goes there: A fortress on a mountain, visible from the Cape, with a tomb and a spring nobody can explain.
  • The odd detail: The springs whose source "no man has ever been able to discover" (Franck's report, 1920) — a period puzzle to hang a secret on; and gendarmerie convicts carrying the Marines' picnic to the place where Christophe's own prisoners were thrown.
  • Sources: Franck 1920 (ch. VII); M04
  • Confidence: High for Franck's description; the "unknown source" is his claim.
  • Divergence check: holds; it sits inside the occupied north for the whole 1930s (M04).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A GM's Citadelle: the water that never fails, the vault nobody opens, the Marine detail that comes to clear "the old powder magazine".

Fort Rivière — a ruined fort near Saint-Raphaël, about 20 miles south of the Cap

  • What it was in the occupation years: The last stronghold of the First Caco War: the Marines' attack of 17 November 1915 (Butler's drain, the 50+ dead cacos, the fort dynamited, some 60 dwellings destroyed) is in M04. As a place: an old French fort on a mountaintop; after the dynamiting, a shell. (M04 hook: "a fort blown up and its site never rebuilt: what the dynamite opened".)
  • Why an investigator goes there: The fort is a set-piece location for any Butler-era flashback and a site where the occupier deliberately destroyed a place.
  • The odd detail: The Marines dynamited it because it worked as a fortress — the only Haitian fort they took by hand and then blew up rather than garrison.
  • Sources: M04 (from https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Fort_Rivi%C3%A8re)
  • Confidence: High for the event; not re-read this session.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916 (Nov 1915), happened.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Ruin as a mission site: what a Marine who crawled through that drain saw on the other side, and why Butler never described the interior.

Part 3 — Mirebalais, the Plateau and the Artibonite

Mirebalais — the Marine post on the Artibonite road, about 60 km north-east of Port-au-Prince

  • What it was in the occupation years: A town "established in 1702", on National Road 3 (Wikipedia via fetch summary; the summary gives Batraville's death as 9 March 1920, while M04 has 20 May 1920 — flag: dates conflict; V_M20: 20 May 1920 is right, per Wikipedia's Batraville article, some sources say 19 May ⟨fixed: V_M20⟩). Seabrook's book puts "the Marine Camp in Mirebalais" under Lt-Col. Little in spring 1920 (the Muth ambush, below) and a U.S. Marine Corps provost court hearing at Mirebalais into the 1920 case of Cadeus Bellegarde, whom Seabrook calls "a papaloi turned criminal", "feared, bitterly hated, and finally denounced by the fervent Voodoo peasants themselves"; the evidence was then "turned over to the Haitian courts on a ruling … of the Judge Advocate General that it was outside military jurisdiction" (Seabrook's phrasing and his hearsay: "I am informed"). Seabrook raises the case in a passage on alleged ritual killing that he admits he never witnessed; nothing here confirms the charges. Batraville (M04) was born and buried at Mirebalais.
  • Why an investigator goes there: A garrison town at the mouth of the plateau, where a provost court decided which sins the Marines could punish.
  • The odd detail: A provost court that declined jurisdiction over a "sorcery" murder — so a Haitian court and a Marine court both had a claim on the same body.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirebalais (via fetch summary); Seabrook 1929 (appendix and the Muth passage); M04 (Batraville)
  • Confidence: Medium-Low for the Bellegarde case (Seabrook's hearsay; "papaloi turned criminal" is his label; I found no court record). The Marine Camp at Mirebalais is well attested by the Muth account.
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A murder that the Marines will not try, the Haitians cannot reach, and the IHS wants: a jurisdiction gap for a Section 13 agent. Respect: a criminal who happened to be a priest of the religion does not make the religion the crime.

Lascahobas (Las Cahobas) and the trail to Barrière Roche — the ambush country east of Mirebalais

  • What it was in the occupation years: In Seabrook's telling of the Muth ambush (spring 1920; M04): the wounded Marines "fought steadily until close to Las Cahobas", where they got word to Lt-Col. Little at Mirebalais "some twenty miles away"; twenty-four patrols went out, took four hours to reach the mountain-top and "fought a guerilla warfare all the way up"; "at the very peak they found what was left of the Sergeant" (the account names Muth and a Sergeant; the rank is unresolved — M04 already flags it); the bodies of ten "bandits" were found on the trail. M04 has Batraville's death at Barrière Roche, near Lascahobas.
  • Why an investigator goes there: The route where the occupation lost its patrol and gained its "voodoo" story (M04's entry: the Muth mutilation account is prisoners' testimony recorded by Marines).
  • The odd detail: The Marines' own grievance — "It was all right to kill Muth… That was war. But why did they mutilate his body?" — is, in Seabrook's telling, the Marines' standing grievance about the hills.
  • Sources: Seabrook 1929 (appendix, "From the Author's Notebook"); M04 (Batraville)
  • Confidence: Medium: Seabrook's retelling; M04 flags the prisoner-testimony problem.
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A mountain trail patrolled in a month of four-hour climbs: the GM's "Marines' file on the hills". The village's own memory of what happened at the peak is the second layer nobody has recorded.

Hinche — the Central Plateau's Marine post and Péralte's home town

  • What it was in the occupation years: Franck (1920): "the birthplace of the departed Charlemagne"; "half a hundred marines playing baseball and basketball on the plain about their camp at the edge of town" against a town that fits Franck's template for "all Haitian towns": "a big, brown, bare, dusty central place, with a tiny band-stand with steps painted in the national colors and surmounted by a single royal palm-tree". Péralte's family "long predominated" in Hinche, and he was sentenced to five years' hard labour there (M04). Niles (1926) has the new Las Cahobas–Hinche road pushed through "brigand-Cacos country". M04 has the 19 caco prisoners executed at Hinche in January 1919 under a Captain Lavoie, and Barnett's confidential letter of 1919.
  • Why an investigator goes there: The pivot of the caco war; a town where a Marine post and a martyr's family sat side by side for a decade.
  • The odd detail: Baseball and basketball beside the parade ground of a town whose prisoners were shot: the occupation's ordinary face.
  • Sources: Franck 1920 (ch. VII); Niles 1926 (the Hinche road); M04 (Péralte, Barnett)
  • Confidence: Medium-High (Franck's eyewitness; executions per M04).
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A town where a Marine captain's execution list is "recorded" only by a gendarme's memory. The Barnett letter's copy is a document a Chicago newspaper family kept (M04).

Gonaïves, Ennery and the Artibonite — the coast, the plain and the hills behind

  • What it was in the occupation years: Gonaïves is "Haiti's city of independence": Dessalines read the Act of Independence on 1 January 1804 at the town's Place d'Armes, drafted by Boisrond Tonnerre (Wikipedia via fetch summary, which gave nothing on 1915–40). Kuser's 1920 car from Gonaïves "escaped the caco band by a miracle" (bullet-holed on arrival at Port-au-Prince). M04: Ennery, in the hills north of Gonaïves, is where the woman identified as Felicia Felix-Mentor appeared on 24 October 1936; she was taken to the government hospital at Gonaïves, and Mars X-rayed her at the Central Hospital. (No source read here on the Artibonite plain's geography or on the port's occupation life.)
  • Why an investigator goes there: The road-and-hospital chain of the Felix-Mentor case; a town whose founding act — an independence proclaimed against a white army — is on every schoolroom wall of an occupied country.
  • The odd detail: The town of the 1804 declaration is the town of the 1936 "zombi" case's hospital — one road links the birth of the state and its most-photographed rumour.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gona%C3%AFves (via fetch summary); Kuser 1921; M04 (Felix-Mentor)
  • Confidence: Low-Medium (thin on the occupation years).
  • Divergence check: holds; 1936 events are BENDS lightly (M04: the hospital and Garde are under American officers).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Ennery cross-ref: the hospital yard where Hurston photographed a woman her neighbours knew as a zombi, and where a Marine medical officer signs the chart. Respect: Felicia was a real, suffering woman.

Part 4 — Santo Domingo and the Dominican Republic

Santo Domingo, "La Capital" under the military government — the Ozama river's west bank

  • What it was in the occupation years: Franck (1920): "a prettier town than Santiago, and somewhat larger. It is less compact, has more trees and open spaces, and many curious old ruins — palaces, gates, fortresses, and churches — so many old churches, in fact, that some of them are now used as theaters and government offices." Two score or more automobiles "hover about the central plaza"; good macadam roads run 16, 25 and 30 km in three directions; the theatres show films; church bells begin "long before dawn" and, in the influenza epidemic of 1918–19, "thumped for nine mortal days without a pause, until the marine doctors protested that most of the victims were dying for lack of sleep". "The gaunt old American battleship Memphis [sic — an armoured cruiser] still sitting bolt upright on the rocks beside the seaside promenade of the capital just where a wave tossed her in September, 1916." (USS Memphis was wrecked on 29 August 1916 — confirmed at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Memphis_(ACR-10): Franck's "September" and "battleship" are his errors; she was an armored cruiser, 43 dead or missing, sold for scrap in 1922, and the last of the wreck did not leave the shore until 1938) ⟨fixed: V_M20⟩ The military governor's proclamation was 29 November 1916 (M04); Wikipedia (via fetch summary): Marines and Dominicans clashed in the city on 24–25 October 1916 (two Marines and three Dominicans dead), with Harry Shepard Knapp installed as military governor. Franck's "American governor" was announcing that 60 percent of the highway to Monte Cristi was complete.
  • Why an investigator goes there: The seat of the naval military government, the customs, the Guardia's headquarters, the cathedral — the whole occupation of the DR is within the old walled quarter.
  • The odd detail: A wrecked American warship left on the rocks beside the promenade for more than three years — the occupation's first monument in the capital was an accident.
  • Sources: Franck 1920 (ch. X, "Santo Domingo under American Rule"); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santo_Domingo (via fetch summary); M04 (landing and military government)
  • Confidence: High for Franck's snapshot (early 1920); the Memphis date (29 Aug 1916) is now confirmed ⟨fixed: V_M20⟩.
  • Divergence check: holds — 1916–20 is occupation-era; BENDS after 1924 (the withdrawal never happens; M04 Part G), so the military government's palace, ruins and streets persist unrenamed.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A capital full of churches used as theatres and offices: the IHS priest's grievance is that the Republic's cinema sits where a chapel's altar was. And an old cruiser rusting on the promenade as a reminder that the Americans arrived by storm.

The Alcázar de Colón (the House of Columbus) — a height above the Ozama, at the foot of the old wall

  • What it was in the occupation years: Built by Diego Columbus between 1511 and 1514 (Wikipedia via fetch summary), of coral rock, originally 55 rooms (about 22 survive); Diego's widow María de Toledo lived there until 1549; Drake looted it in 1586; it was "nearly converted into a prison" by 1776; declared a National Monument in 1870; restored 1955–57 as a museum. So in the occupation years it was a ruin. Schoenrich (1918): "reduced to a mere shell, roofless and windowless; in a part of its interior there is a little palm thatch shelter for stabling horses; while the court yard and terrace reek with offal from dirty cabins round about"; it stands next to the Gate of San Diego / "Gate of the Admiral", which gave access to the wharves on the Ozama, and near "part of the old city wall erected in 1537" (Schoenrich).
  • Why an investigator goes there: A palace the poor lived around: a Columbian relic hidden in a slum, with a stable in the throne room's shell.
  • The odd detail: The reception hall "behind the viceroy's throne" held a carved coat of arms of Castile (Oviedo, per Schoenrich); the throne-room shell was a horse-shelter in 1918.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alc%C3%A1zar_de_Col%C3%B3n (via fetch summary); Schoenrich 1918 (ch. "Cities and Towns")
  • Confidence: High for Schoenrich's 1918 report; Medium for Wikipedia's dates (via fetch summary).
  • Divergence check: holds — pre-1916 building; the 1955–57 restoration BENDS (the setting's occupation, or its owner-ruled Dominican authorities, may restore it or not).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A Columbus ruin with horses in it and a slum around it: a Circle interest in Columbus's house is a folk-horror seed (Schoenrich notes the "long inheritance litigation" among the Columbus heirs and that the house's history after 1578 "becomes obscure"). A digger's trench at the terrace's foot in 1936 is the GM's start.

The cathedral and the Columbus tomb — Santa María la Menor, on the central plaza

  • What it was in the occupation years: Begun 1504 by Julius II's order, construction from 1512, completed 1550; consecrated 31 August 1541; Primate of the Americas 1546; sacked by Drake 1586; raised to a Minor Basilica by Benedict XV in 1920; nave 54 m long (Wikipedia via fetch summary). The Columbus remains "were housed in the cathedral for a time, transferred in 1795 to the Cathedral of Havana and finally, between 1898 and 1899, to Seville" (the same page; the Dominican claim is that the true remains stayed in Santo Domingo and were found in 1877 — M04). Franck (1920): "a picturesque pile of old stone, without tower or spire", with an "elaborate tomb of the famous Genoese just inside its main portal", "splendid bronze reliefs", Velázquez and Murillo pictures, "a picture brought over by Columbus" seen only with the sexton's key, "a cross which the discoverer of America is said to have set up nearby" behind glass doors because people chipped it for relics; a Syrian merchant, "long resident in 'La Capital' but deceased in New York" was buried in the marble floor a few feet from the tomb during his stay; the interior "restored by daubing the walls with gleaming white" but "the Pope vetoed" whitewashing the outer walls; the tomb of "the archbishop who was once president" (Meriño, in Wikipedia's list of burials).
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is the twin of Havana's cathedral (M11) and one of two claimants to Columbus's bones; the Archbishop's records; a place of relics and paid-for burials.
  • The odd detail: In 1920 a merchant paid for a grave beside the Discoverer — "it does not seem necessary to be of Columbian stature to be able to sleep one's last sleep beside the doughty old navigator" (Franck). And the Pope himself refused the whitewash.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Santa_Mar%C3%ADa_la_Menor (via fetch summary); Franck 1920; M11 (Havana cathedral, "two graves claim Columbus"); M04 (Columbus Lighthouse, fukú)
  • Confidence: High for what the book prints; Medium for Wikipedia dates.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916; holds. IHS (Catholic) presence is a setting given.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers of truth: Seville's, Santo Domingo's, and whatever is in the box beside the Syrian merchant. The fukú (M04 Part F): the "discoverer's own name is the island's curse word" — a relic that is a curse is a natural IHS case.

The Ozama fortress and the river gate — the Torre del Homenaje, the customs compound and the ceiba

  • What it was in the occupation years: Built 1502–08 under Governor Ovando to a design by Gómez García de Varela, "the oldest military construction of European origin in the Americas"; the Torre del Homenaje is 18 m high with 2-m walls; "coral stones extracted from the sea"; the mortar "a mixture of gypsum, clay, lime and the blood of animals" (all via fetch summary of Wikipedia); it served as a prison (the page names the ex-presidents Jacinto Peynado and Horacio Vásquez among those who "did some time in the prison"; the summary places that in the "eighteen & 19 century", which cannot be right — flag: Vásquez was a 20th-century president); "in 1938 the riverside … was enclosed by a tall, thin, gray, reinforced concrete wall" by Trujillo; "in 1965 the Fortaleza ceased to serve as a prison". Franck and Schoenrich both mention the aged tree in the custom-house compound on the bank of the Ozama to which Columbus is said to have tied a ship (Schoenrich: "a very old ceiba … near the lumber market").
  • Why an investigator goes there: The cells; the port; the customs house where the receivership took its cut (M04: customs in American hands since 1905).
  • The odd detail: The gray concrete wall of 1938 is a Trujillo-era addition to a 1502 tower; "the blood of animals" in the mortar (a construction claim from one page).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortaleza_Ozama (via fetch summary); Franck 1920; Schoenrich 1918
  • Confidence: Medium (fetch summaries; the presidents' dates flagged).
  • Divergence check: holds pre-1916; the 1938 wall BENDS (needs Trujillo in power — M04).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A tower built to watch the river mouth and used to hold the men who wanted the Americans out; the customs compound's ceiba — a tree tied to a legend — is the place a GM stashes a ledger.

The government palace — the Americans' seat, on or beside the central plaza (exact site not confirmed)

  • What it was in the occupation years: Franck (1920): "The government palace, occupied now by Americans in navy and marine uniforms, is full of capacious leather-upholstered chairs, in striking contrast to the average uncomfortable Dominican seat. No wonder they fought one another to become president." He places it in the paragraph on the cathedral and the central plaza, but does not name the building; I found no source for the "Marine HQ" building itself (Kuser's and Seabrook's Haiti Brigade HQ is not the Dominican one). M04: the military government was proclaimed by Capt. Knapp on 29 November 1916, succeeded by Rear Adm. Edwin Anderson Jr. and others; customs receivership from 1905.
  • Why an investigator goes there: A palace where naval officers sign the country's decrees. It is the DR's whole executive.
  • The odd detail: Naval officers in capacious leather-upholstered chairs in a palace where, in Franck's joke, Dominican presidents had fought one another for the seat.
  • Sources: Franck 1920; M04 (military government)
  • Confidence: Low-Medium (one line; no address).
  • Divergence check: holds; BENDS after 1924 (the setting keeps the naval government, M04 Part G Q2).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A palace where a military governor recalled in 1936 leaves behind a desk drawer (M04's question: what did the previous governor see that got him recalled?).

The old wall, the Puerta del Conde and the Gate of San Diego — the trapezium of the old city

  • What it was in the occupation years: Schoenrich (1918): the old city "in the form of a trapezium occupying an area of about 200 acres", its walls "erected in 1537", the north wall longest; "numerous portions remain intact, though all traces of the moat have disappeared"; the Gate of San Diego ("Gate of the Admiral") near Diego Columbus's house is one of the three original gates; another "bulwark of the twenty-seventh of February". Franck: "several ancient stone gateways remaining from its former city wall"; the "Puerta de la Independencia" on "a pretty outer plaza" — "curious because the Dominicans pretend it is the gate through which the triumphant army entered after driving out the Haitians in 1844, though any street urchin knows the entry was really made by a less ornate gate nearer the sea".
  • Why an investigator goes there: Gates are ready-made borders: a curfew, a checkpoint, a route in and out of the old quarter.
  • The odd detail: A national gate that everybody "knows" is the wrong one — the country's founding story pinned to a gate its own children know is false (Franck, with his tone).
  • Sources: Schoenrich 1918; Franck 1920
  • Confidence: Medium-High (two books; modern names not checked).
  • Divergence check: holds — pre-1916; modern renaming unverified.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers: the official gate, the street urchin's gate, and what is walled into the gap. A GM's Marine patrol uses the "wrong gate" because it is closer to the wharf.

The colonial ruins — San Nicolás de Bari and the churches that became offices

  • What it was in the occupation years: The Hospital San Nicolás de Bari (begun 1503, in service by 1522, "the oldest hospital built in the Americas", cross-shaped, 70 beds, "Gothic and Renaissance elements, with some considerable Mudéjar influence") was "already a ruin in 1908 when part of its façade collapsed" (Wikipedia via fetch summary; the page does not say a storm did it). Franck saw "so many old churches, in fact, that some of them are now used as theaters and government offices", and Schoenrich's language for the House of Columbus is of neglect, not of hurricane. The convent and monastery ruins of the colonial city (San Francisco, the Dominicans) are not sourced here (my 404 on the Colonial City page).
  • Why an investigator goes there: Ruins are where things are hidden and where storms open things.
  • The odd detail: The first hospital of the New World was a ruin before the Marines came; the roofless 1500s hospital's cross-shaped plan is a ready floor plan for a GM.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital_San_Nicol%C3%A1s_de_Bari (via fetch summary); Franck 1920
  • Confidence: Medium-Low (one fetch summary + one remark).
  • Divergence check: holds pre-1916; the 1930 hurricane's effect on the ruins BENDS in the setting (M04: the storm holds; its politics bend).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): M04's San Zenón hook: "which old walls, crypts and colonial foundations the wind opened … and what walked out before the Marines arrived." The hospital's cellars are the place.

Santo Domingo after San Zenón — the flattened city and its rebuilt streets

  • What it was in the occupation years: M04 has the storm (3 September 1930; roughly half the city levelled, three districts almost entirely; 2,000–8,000 dead) and the rebuilding under Trujillo, who took emergency powers; Wikipedia adds that the rebuilt city was renamed Ciudad Trujillo (1936, M04) and "renamed back to Santo Domingo following his assassination in 1961" (via fetch summary). Wikipedia's Ozama page adds a 1938 gray concrete wall at the fortress. I found no source in this session for the new city's landmarks (the Obelisk, the seafront avenue, hotels): the fetch said the article "does not mention" them.
  • Why an investigator goes there: A city with half of it new — new streets, new plans, empty lots where the dead were burned (widely reported; UNVERIFIED here per M04).
  • The odd detail: None of the fetches on the Alcázar, the cathedral or the Ozama fortress mentions San Zenón damage — a gap in what I read, not evidence that they came through untouched (the Wikipedia summary for the fortress says only that a wall was added in 1938).
  • Sources: M04 (San Zenón, Ciudad Trujillo); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santo_Domingo and Fortaleza_Ozama (via fetch summary)
  • Confidence: Low for new-city features; High for the storm (M04).
  • Divergence check: the storm holds; BENDS in politics (the setting: the military government, or whoever commands the Guardia, rebuilds; Ciudad Trujillo's name needs Trujillo in power).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A rebuilt city needs a plan: whoever drew the new avenues chose which colonial ruins were cleared. A GM's demolition permit signed the week after the storm.

Santiago de los Caballeros — the Cibao's capital, the rival of the capital

  • What it was in the occupation years: Founded 1495, moved to its present site in 1506, wrecked by the 2 December 1562 earthquake; the "peak of architecture" in the late 1800s — the cathedral built in 1895 by Onofre de Lora and the Palacio Consistorial 1892–95 by Louis Bogaert (Wikipedia via fetch summary). Schoenrich (1918): "one of the richest and most flourishing cities of the island", aspiring "to become the capital of the Republic, so that an intense rivalry exists with Santo Domingo", burnt in 1863 in the Restoration War. Franck (1920): a valley "piled up a sloping high ground", a shaded plaza, a bulking, weather-peeled cathedral with tin-voiced bells, a casa consistorial, clubs, "the cathedral, the municipal building, many a private residence, our very hotel room were speckled with bullet-holes more or less diligently patched"; a truck-load of Marines blocked the road; carriages, lottery-ticket sellers, notices in the cathedral about heads covered and spitting. The February 1930 revolt against Vásquez began here (M04). The Trujillo monument (1944) and the San Luis fort's later use as a prison are out of window.
  • Why an investigator goes there: The Cibao's tobacco capital and the DR's second city; the 1916 Marines' second conquest; the coup's cradle.
  • The odd detail: A cathedral with bullet-holes in 1920 — a town's revolutions written on the church wall.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiago_de_los_Caballeros (via fetch summary); Schoenrich 1918; Franck 1920 (ch. IX, "The Land of Bullet-Holes"); M04
  • Confidence: High for the books; Medium for the Wikipedia dates.
  • Divergence check: holds pre-1930; the 1930 revolt BENDS (needs a sovereign president to topple — M04).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A bullet-pocked cathedral in a town that wants to be the capital: the rival capital is where a GM's coup rumour starts, and where Berlin's or the Circle's man buys tobacco.

Dajabón and the Massacre River — the northern crossing, Ouanaminthe to Dajabón

  • What it was in the occupation years: Wikipedia (via fetch summary): Dajabón was "initially founded between 1771 and 1776", abandoned in the war of independence, resettled after the 1865 Restoration War; on the Dajabón (Massacre) River; a bridge to Ouanaminthe; Haitians may cross on Mondays and Fridays to sell goods (modern). Franck (1920) came in from Haiti and forded the Massacre by car: "the change from one civilization to another … was as sudden … as the dash through the apparently unfordable stream"; "the shallow Massacre is as definite a dividing line as though it were a hundred leagues of sea"; Dajabón was "strewn from the sandy crest of the eastern bank to the arid plains", its houses better than Haiti's shacks (his period comparison), and beyond it a nearly treeless plain of cattle. (M04: the river was called "Massacre" from 1728; the 2 Oct 1937 speech and killings are in M04.)
  • Why an investigator goes there: A market town on a border everybody crosses; where a line on the map is a ford.
  • The odd detail: In 1920 an American traveller took a car across the river bed: the "frontier" was a shallow ford, with Marines helping at Ouanaminthe.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dajab%C3%B3n (via fetch summary); Franck 1920; M04
  • Confidence: High for Franck's crossing; Medium for Wikipedia's dates.
  • Divergence check: the ford and the town hold; BENDS for the border's meaning after 1924 — both sides are American in the setting, so the river is an internal line (M04 Part G Q4).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Respect: this is the place of the 1937 killings; no occult layer sits with the victims. If the setting has the massacre, the order's chain of command belongs to the occupier's or a native strongman's paper trail. A ford is a good place to find who crossed with a ledger.

Monte Cristi — the north-west coast town, with its clock tower

  • What it was in the occupation years: Marines took Monte Cristi on 1 June 1916 (M04). Franck (1920): "a considerable town", wide unpaved streets; the "commander-in-chief of Monte Cristi, a Marine captain" gave the travellers his whole house; the "Morro", "a great bulking rectangular hill"; in the plaza "a near replica of the Eiffel Tower, its open-work steel frame crowned by a large four-faced clock some fifty feet above". His tale (an anecdote at Dominican expense): a congressional candidate promised each town what it wanted; Monte Cristi asked for a town clock, the tower went up "without waiting for … congressional sanction", the bill never got approved, and "with the sudden coming of the Americans" the town was presented with a foreign-issued invoice reading "To one clock and tower, Dr $16,000 — Please Remit". M04: bodies were thrown into the sea at Monte Cristi in Oct 1937.
  • Why an investigator goes there: A corner of the coast where Americans run a town by a Marine captain's word.
  • The odd detail: The $16,000 clock tower the occupation billed the town for — "the ridiculous Americans who had taken in charge the revenues of the country brought with them the absurd doctrine that municipalities should pay their bills" (Franck's joke, whose mockery is his).
  • Sources: Franck 1920 (ch. IX); M04
  • Confidence: Medium (the tower is real). [UNVERIFIED — V_M20: the $16,000 bill is Franck's anecdote alone. Dominican local sources give a different history: the clock tower stood in the plaza from 11 March 1895 and was inaugurated on 29 June 1895 (that article also says Máximo Gómez and José Martí attended, which cannot be right for June — Martí died on 19 May 1895 — so it must mean the March installation), its original value was about 15,000 pesos, and the purchase was paid by a collection organised by Monte Cristi's ayuntamiento — https://7dias.com.do/2023/06/29/el-historico-reloj-publico-de-montecristi-cumple-hoy-128-anos-de-su-inauguracion-y-se-habla-de-su-prometido-remozamiento/ . The "Eiffel" attribution is a popular claim; the tower predates the Marines by 21 years.] ⟨fixed: V_M20⟩
  • Divergence check: holds; the 1937 Monte Cristi killings BEND (M04).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The receivership as a bill: a tower whose price is a debt, a town paying for a foreigner's idea of a clock. The Circle's method ("held by the ledger", §29.4) in one anecdote.

San Pedro de Macorís — the eastern sugar port

  • What it was in the occupation years: Franck saw "the dangerous bottle entrance to the harbor of San Pedro de Macoris, with its wrecked schooner" from a submarine chaser lent by the military governor. M04: Gregorio Urbano Gilbert (b. Puerto Plata to a Dominican mother and a Bahamian father) fired on the Marines as they landed at San Pedro on 10 January 1917, killing C. H. Burton; the eastern provinces (El Seibo, San Pedro) were the gavillero war's country (144 Marines killed in action, 74 native constabulary casualties, and about 950 insurgents killed or wounded, 1917–22 ⟨fixed: V_M20⟩). Franck's road from the west passed "another large mill with its creaking ox-carts and striking negroes" — the eastern sugar belt.
  • Why an investigator goes there: The port of the sugar coast — mills, labourers from the British islands, a Marine garrison, and the 1917 shot.
  • The odd detail: A harbour with a "bottle entrance" and a wrecked schooner in it — the landing place of the first Marine shot.
  • Sources: Franck 1920 (ch. X); M04 (Gilbert, gavilleros)
  • Confidence: Medium-Low (a passing description).
  • Divergence check: holds — occupation-era.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A port where one clerk's revolver tied the DR to Nicaragua (M04); the ledger of who paid Gilbert's lawyer.

La Romana and the Central Romana mill — "virtually American", a quarter-million-acre estate

  • What it was in the occupation years: Wikipedia (via fetch summary): La Romana was founded in 1897 as an oil town; after 1917 the South Porto Rico Sugar Company's "mammoth Central Romana mill" made it "a one-company town"; the mill "was the only sugar operation not taken over by Rafael Trujillo" (Gulf+Western, Casa de Campo etc. are much later). Franck (early February 1920; the government boat ran there): "though it is in Dominican territory, La Romana is virtually American, a vast estate belonging to a great sugar company of Porto Rico": one of the "huge centrals" as in Cuba — "big-business atmosphere", "long rows of excellent dwellings built of light coral rock along the edge of the jagged coast", its own stores, clubs and movies, standard-gauge railway, "bateys of white wooden huts raised on stilts"; about a quarter-million acres, only 16,000 under cane; the company was pushing back "the forests and the bandits"; it employed "from 7500 to 9000 men, of whom a bare hundred are Americans", the rest "chiefly Haitians and Porto Ricans", plus West Indians; wages $1.20 a day for cane-cutters to $4 for mechanics, 12-hour shifts and a 20 percent bonus; English, Puerto Rican and Dominican schools. Franck's own gloss on the Dominicans ("too Castilian to work for a living") is period contempt, not a fact.
  • Why an investigator goes there: The biggest single employer in the DR, a company town with its own police-like structure, railway and clubs, where a hundred Americans rule eight thousand others. The ledger machinery in the flesh.
  • The odd detail: The estate is fifteen times larger than the cane it grows (250,000 acres; 16,000 planted) — the rest is forest and pasture "the company is pushing back".
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Romana,_Dominican_Republic (via fetch summary); Franck 1920 (ch. X); M04 (Haitian cane workers in the DR after 1937)
  • Confidence: High for Franck's report; Medium for Wikipedia (its "1917 mill" conflicts with Franck's 1920 description of a working "central" — check).
  • Divergence check: holds; company-town holds under any occupation. BENDS for Trujillo's leaving it alone (no Trujillo in power).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A company estate with more forest than cane and a hundred Americans is the Golden Circle's cheapest plantation: cult versus population — eight thousand cane-cutters (Haitian, Puerto Rican, West Indian) and a few dozen who see what the clearing turned up. The bacá (M04 Part F) is the planter's spirit, never the cutter's.

San Juan de la Maguana — the southwestern valley, near the Haitian border

  • What it was in the occupation years: M04: Olivorio Mateo ("Papá Liborio") was born here (1876), vanished in a 1908 hurricane and returned after seven days as a healer-prophet; he was killed on 27 June 1922 (date confirmed) by a detachment of the Dominican constabulary (the "P.N.D.") under a Capt. Williams and Lt. Luna — not, in the Dominican accounts, by Marine riflemen as such (whether Williams was a US Marine is UNVERIFIED) — and his body was taken to San Juan, photographed and shown to thousands (the Acento piece says paraded "throughout the city"; a town-park display is in other accounts I did not open) to show he could die ⟨fixed: V_M20: https://acento.com.do/cultura/101-anos-de-la-muerte-de-liborio-el-lider-mesianico-dominicano-9215773.html and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivorio_Mateo, which puts the shooting at Sabrosa Hill⟩. M04 also puts the galipote legend in this same valley. I found no period source for the town itself (buildings, streets, garrison).
  • Why an investigator goes there: The seat of Liborismo, a living popular devotion; a valley the Marines could not pacify for six years.
  • The odd detail: The occupation's constabulary chose a town square as the place to show a dead messiah ⟨fixed: V_M20⟩ — the same logic as the Péralte door, one border away (M04).
  • Sources: M04 (Papá Liborio; galipote)
  • Confidence: Low for the place; High for M04's event.
  • Divergence check: holds — 1922 is occupation-era.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Respect: Liborismo is a living devotion; the prophet is not the monster. A Marine officer who remembers the Parque Central that day is a witness; a pilgrim at the spring is a friend.

Divergence questions for the owner (don't settle — note)

  1. Who lives where. In the setting the Haitian client president (Vincent, as recorded, from 1930) and the DR's naval military government both survive past 1934/1924. Are the National Palace, the old Sam palace and the Santo Domingo government house seats of a native government, of the occupation, or both? (M04 Part G Q1–Q2.)
  2. The Hotel Oloffson. A Marine hospital "for the duration" (1915–34, Wikipedia) that never ends means no 1935 hotel — does the setting keep a "Swedish sea captain from Germany" arriving anyway (a first concrete address for Germany contesting the hemisphere, §8.6)?
  3. Sans-Souci and the Citadelle. Franck's 1920 remark that the forces of occupation are "threatening to tear down" the palace for barracks is a fork the setting can take or leave.
  4. Péralte's grave. Franck's five fake funerals make an unmarked grave plausible; the 1934/35 state funeral cannot happen (M04). Which grave, if any, is real is the owner's.
  5. Ciudad Trujillo. The rebuilt capital's name and the 1938 wall at the Ozama fortress need Trujillo in power; M04's three shapes (Guardia chief / strongman / mutineer) apply.
  6. The border. Dajabón's ford is an internal line in a setting where both halves are American (M04 Part G Q4).
