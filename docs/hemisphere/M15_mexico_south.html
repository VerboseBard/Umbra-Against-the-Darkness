M15 — Mexico's unceded south, 1900–1940: Yucatán, Quintana Roo, Chiapas, Oaxaca, Tabasco, Campeche

  • Lane: M15 — the henequen hacienda and "Barbarous Mexico"; the Caste War's aftermath, the Cruzob Maya and the Talking Cross; Felipe Carrillo Puerto and Alma Reed; the archaeologists (Chichén Itzá, the Sacred Cenote, Carnegie, Tulane's Blom and La Farge); Tabasco; Chiapas and the Lacandón; Oaxaca (Monte Albán's Tomb 7, Mitla, Huautla); the Isthmus of Tehuantepec; folklore attested before 1940 (dated and labelled).
  • Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (research agent) · Brief: BRIEF.md in this folder + the Chicago sweep's BRIEF.md (entry format, sourcing rules).
  • Setting sources read first: BRIEF.md (hemisphere summary, divergence rules, Respect section); ALTERNATE_HISTORY.md §8 (hemisphere), §29 (Mexico/Caribbean theatres, "three layers of truth", "cult versus population", "secular destruction of consecrated ground"); M01/M02/M12 tables of contents (not repeated here — cross-referenced where they overlap).

How to read the divergence lines. This lane sits entirely in UNCEDED MEXICO — the south is the part of the country the Southern War never took. What bends is not the ground but the world around it: the setting's American-held north, the Tampico enclave and the Veracruz customs receivership; the Panama Canal Zone held outright; British Honduras (Yucatán's neighbour and the Cruzob's arms-and-trade window) sold at the "fire sale" of §8.4; no Great-War American entry (so no Office of Naval Intelligence war of 1917–19 as recorded); no Soviet Union (Yucatán's "socialism" and Tabasco's Red Shirts are then home-grown); and International Harvester of Chicago and Wrigley of Chicago — the two Chicago firms that sit at the root of Yucatán's and Quintana Roo's economies — are, in the setting, plain neighbours of the book's first city. Before March 1916 everything happened as recorded.

Overview (42 entries). The south is the part of Mexico the Southern War never took, so almost nothing here cannot happen; what bends is who governs it and who is watching. The richest single finding for the setting is how many Chicago fingerprints sit on it: the International Harvester contract (October 1902) that let a trust fix Yucatán's price; C. H. Kerr's Chicago edition of Barbarous Mexico (1910); Wrigley's chicle; University of Chicago's Redfield at Chan Kom; the Field Columbian Museum among Thompson's sponsors; and a Chicago Daily News correspondent who knew who armed the 1923 rebels and never printed it. The second: a whole stratum of American archaeologists who were also spies (Morley's ONI net of March 1917, with Spinden and Lothrop) working under a Mexican permit beside a smuggler (Thompson, who sent the Sacred Cenote's gold to Harvard by consular mail pouch). Third: the Talking Cross and the last shots of the Caste War (Dzula, April 1933) — handled here as a living faith, not a cult. Fourth: the state that burned its saints (Grajales, 20 November 1934) and the people who hid them in caves. Fifth: Oaxaca 1931–32 — earthquake (14 January 1931), Eisenstein's plane, Tomb 7 (January 1932), the Guelaguetza (25 April 1932). Plus Mitla's sealed door (Burgoa, 1674), Valle Nacional (a plantation called La Sepultura), Huautla's mushroom vigils reaching Harvard (1936–39), and the Tehuantepec railway, seized in April 1917 — the year of the setting's treaty — which is the hardest open question the lane raises. Where sources conflict (casualty counts, several dates, the fate of the original Cross and of the Christ of the Blisters), the entry says so. Most pages were read through the fetch tool's summaries (three PDFs — Hulme on Carrillo Puerto, Heyden on the Tulum cross, Guzmán Monroy on Chiapas — were read as full text); sources known only from search-engine summaries are marked "via search." Distances from Havana are my own great-circle estimates.

Part A — Yucatán: green gold, slavery by another name, and the revolution that came late (1870s–1924)

1. "Green gold": the henequen boom and the Casta Divina — c. 1870s–1920

  • Where: Mérida, the port of Progreso, and a ring of some 1,200 haciendas within about 80 km of Mérida at the peak (Yucatán, UNCEDED).
  • What happened: Henequen (the agave whose fibre became binder-twine and rope) turned Mérida, in Hulme's words, "from a dusty provincial backwater into the most modern city in Mexico," on the back of soaring American demand for binder-twine. A tight circle of 20–30 interrelated creole families, the Casta Divina ("Divine Caste" — they preferred gente decente), owned or marketed most of it: one count gives ~1,000 haciendas in 1880–1915, ~850 with processing plants, owned by ~400 families, with the top 20–30 controlling about half of extraction and nearly 90% of exports. By 1910 seven-eighths of the state's population lived off some part of the industry (Hulme). Mérida was called "the White City" and "the Paris of Mexico" (Cámara mansions, the Teatro Peón Contreras) and boasted, in the oft-repeated line, more millionaires per capita than any city in the world. The system underneath was debt-peonage: Maya workers bound to the hacienda, paid in hacienda tokens, whipped. (Two figures on the Wikipedia "Divine Caste" page — a price fall from "$9.48 to 8 cents a pound" and "$1.7 billion in 1900" — do not add up and are not repeated here.)
  • The odd detail: Felipe G. Cantón, planter and culture-patron, "used the Progreso–New York steamship line like ordinary commuters use the bus" (Hulme) — and is the man who told Turner, with a smile, that it was "necessary to whip them" (see 3).
  • People: Olegario Molina — planter, governor 1902–07, Díaz's Secretary of Development 1907–11; Avelino Montes — his son-in-law and partner (via search); Felipe G. Cantón — planter, antiquities patron.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Caste · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haciendas_of_Yucat%C3%A1n · https://escholarship.org/content/qt516354z1/qt516354z1_noSplash_390e6f0f30ac10542dc8bb67df440d8d.pdf?t=r4vo44 (Hulme 2021, read in full via text extraction)
  • Confidence: High on the structure; Medium on the exact counts (Wikipedia-level; scholarly sources differ).
  • Divergence check: holds — pre-1916 and UNCEDED. Only the collapse bends: Hulme dates it to henequen prices "plummeting after the end of the First World War," which in the setting needs a different trigger (America never entered; Germany's peace and sanctions reshape the markets — compare the setting's own Depression story).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): an old-money caste that turned a whole population into a fibre and then into cash — "the rich and their scandals" in a tropical key. Its members have a bolt-hole in Havana: Molina resigned in 1911, stayed in Mérida until 1914, then fled to Paris and settled in Havana ⟨fixed: V_M15⟩ (where his daughter María and her husband Avelino Montes lived) and died there on 28 April 1925; the Golden Circle's map is centred on the same city.

2. The October 1902 contract — Chicago's harvester trust fixes Yucatán's price — 1902–1911

  • Where: Mérida ↔ the International Harvester Company (formed 1902; Chicago-headquartered — general knowledge, not re-verified this session).
  • What happened: In 1902 the International Harvester Company was formed from the five largest US harvester makers (the deal is described as J. P. Morgan's — via search). In October 1902 Molina's export house, Olegario Molina y Cía, signed a secret contract with it: per the Hispanic American Historical Review study of the Molinas, the firm was to "employ every effort" to depress the price of sisal fibre and pay only the prices Harvester dictated, with 10,000 bales placed at its disposal to sell low [UNVERIFIED — V_M15: a Spanish account of the contract text reads 100 mil pacas (100,000), and the price-depressing effort was to begin Monday 27 October 1902 ⟨fixed: V_M15⟩]. Hulme summarises the result: Harvester bought effectively the entire crop at a price it could fix, having supplied the credit that built the industry. Molina had taken office as governor on 1 February 1902 (term to 27 March 1907); he was then Díaz's Secretary of Development from 21 March 1907 until his resignation on 24 March 1911, as the Revolution began; he stayed in Mérida until 1914, then fled to Paris and Havana ⟨fixed: V_M15⟩.
  • The odd detail: the contract was signed the same year Molina became governor — the state's chief executive was also the trust's Yucatán buyer.
  • People: Olegario Molina — governor and buyer; International Harvester Co. — the Chicago buyer; J. P. Morgan — financier (via search).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olegario_Molina · https://read.dukeupress.edu/hahr/article/62/2/224/149204/Family-Elites-in-a-Boom-and-Bust-Economy-The (abstract, via search) · Hulme (as above)
  • Confidence: High on the contract's existence and date; Medium on the wording ("via search" for the HAHR abstract).
  • Divergence check: holds — pre-1916; UNCEDED. Chicago's own harvester industry is, in the setting, just the Chicago book's neighbour.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Chicago corporation with a secret paper-trail to a Mexican governor and a Havana bolt-hole; the Secret Service old guard follows a contract, not a monster, and finds the Circle at the other end.

3. Barbarous Mexico — "The Slaves of Yucatán" — American Magazine, October 1909; book 1910 (Chicago)

  • Where: Yucatán (San Antonio Yaxché and other henequen haciendas); the Chicago publisher C. H. Kerr & Co.
  • What happened: The muckraker John Kenneth Turner went undercover in 1908–09 (assisted by the Mexican lawyer Lázaro Gutiérrez de Lara, financed by a Boston benefactor, posing as an investor with money to sink in henequen). His first chapter, "The Slaves of Yucatán," claimed "more than one hundred thousand" slaves in the state — in his breakdown ~8,000 Yaqui deportees from Sonora, ~3,000 Koreans (the passage I read renders them "Chinese (Koreans)"), and 100,000–125,000 Maya — at a going price of about 400 Mexican dollars a man (down from $1,000 a year earlier, he says, after the 1907 panic); he says planters paid the government about $65 per Yaqui and resold at $400, and that two-thirds of the Yaquis die in their first year — a statement Turner attributes to the planter Enrique Cámara Zavala, president of the Cámara Agrícola ⟨fixed: V_M15⟩. At San Antonio Yaxché, fifteen miles west of Mérida, where he spent two days, he counted about 1,500 slaves and about thirty bosses (the "700 able-bodied men" figure was not found in the chapter [UNVERIFIED — V_M15]), fed once a day on beans, tortillas and fish; Felipe G. Cantón, secretary of the agricultural chamber, explained that whipping was "necessary." The first installment ran in The American Magazine in October 1909; Diaz's supporters pressed the magazine to drop the series, and the socialist Appeal to Reason ran six pieces from it; the book followed in 1910 from Charles H. Kerr & Company of Chicago (archive.org lists Kerr editions of 1910, 1911, 1914).
  • The odd detail: the henequeneros avoided the word "slavery," so Turner proved it the practical way — by asking what it would cost to buy a man (Hulme).
  • People: John Kenneth Turner — journalist; Lázaro Gutiérrez de Lara — Mexican lawyer and guide; Felipe G. Cantón and Joaquín Peón — planters interviewed.
  • Sources: https://archive.org/stream/barbarousmexico00turnuoft/barbarousmexico00turnuoft_djvu.txt (chapter I, read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kenneth_Turner · https://archive.org/advancedsearch.php?q=title%3A%28barbarous+mexico%29&fl[]=identifier&fl[]=title&fl[]=date&fl[]=publisher&rows=10&output=json
  • Confidence: High that the book and figures are Turner's; the numbers are his claims, not audited totals — treat "slavery" as his word and the period's dispute.
  • Divergence check: holds — 1909–10, pre-1916. The Yaqui deportation is a Sonoran story; Sonora is the CEDED NORTH in the setting after 1917 (see M01), so the Yaqui still-in-Yucatán are a living memory there.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Chicago's own socialist press printed the book that made Mexico a scandal; a Chicago 1930s reader would know the phrase "barbarous Mexico." Three layers of truth: the hacendado says "servants," Turner says "slaves," and the hacienda's cenote says something else.

4. Koreans on the hacienda — the Ilford sails, spring 1905; 1,033 people

  • Where: Incheon (Jemulpo) → Salina Cruz (Oaxaca) → across the Isthmus by rail → Progreso → 32 henequen haciendas.
  • What happened: In 1905, 1,033 Koreans signed four-year (some sources: five-year) labour contracts and sailed on the British steamer Ilford. They landed at Salina Cruz, crossed the Isthmus of Tehuantepec (see 35 — presumably by the railway then being rebuilt), took a boat from the Gulf side and arrived at Progreso in May 1905 (the Mexico News Daily article gives 14 May 1905 for the landing at Progreso; the "4 May" date is unsupported ⟨fixed: V_M15⟩). Sold into the hacienda system, they were worked as the Maya were. In 1910 Korea was annexed by Japan, leaving them stateless. They founded schools (Sung-Mu, 1910; Hae-Dong, 1917, San Sebastián, the first recognised by Mexico's education ministry) and — when the hacienda economy collapsed — about 300 left for Cuba in 1921 to cut sugar. Their descendants (Aenikkaeng in the community's word) are in Mérida, with a monument inscribed with all 1,033 names.
  • The odd detail: they were part of Turner's count of slaves (about "3,000 Koreans") four years after they landed; and they crossed the Isthmus two years before the rebuilt Tehuantepec railway formally reopened (1907).
  • People: Kim Ik-joo ("Joaquín Kim," 1890–1956) — arrived at 15, later ran a Korean-style cafeteria in Tampico and supported Korean independence.
  • Sources: https://mexiconewsdaily.com/culture/the-green-gold-miners-from-korea-a-forgotten-diaspora/ (read) · https://mexiconewsdaily.com/culture/forgotten-story-of-mexicos-korean-migrants/ (via search)
  • Confidence: High on the 1,033, the ship, 1910 and the 1921 Cuba move; Medium on the day-level dates.
  • Divergence check: holds — pre-1916. The 1921 move to Cuba lands them in the setting's occupied Cuba (Marines never left).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Korean sugar-cutter in 1930s occupied Havana who was born a henequen slave and speaks Maya; the Circle's map would treat such a person as invisible, and that is the point.

5. Alvarado's Yucatán — peonage abolished on paper (11 September 1914), then in fact (1915)

  • Where: Mérida; the henequen belt (UNCEDED).
  • What happened: Colonel Eleuterio Ávila, governor in 1914, decreed peonage abolished on 11 September 1914 ("little effect"); on 8 February 1915 governor Toribio V. de los Santos decreed it again, forbidding hacienda tokens. Carranza then sent General Salvador Alvarado, appointed governor on 27 February 1915, who reached Mérida by 19 March 1915 and put down the separatist rising of Abel Ortiz Argumedo. He cancelled workers' debts, banned confinement and corporal punishment, revived the Comisión Reguladora del Mercado de Henequén (November 1915) to control the crop's sale, and built schools and libraries. He also "closed churches, confiscating icons and relics" (Wikipedia) — see 6. He was a "socialist" in the pre-1917 sense of a liberal modernizer (Hulme), and left the henequales alone. He served as Treasury Secretary in 1920, backed the De la Huerta rebellion, and was killed on 10 June 1924.
  • The odd detail: the same general who freed the peons closed the churches — and a scholarly article dates Yucatán's post-revolutionary prohibition ("Dry Law") to 1915–35, so the temperance and the anticlericalism arrive together.
  • People: Salvador Alvarado — governor Feb 1915–Nov 1918 (Wikipedia) ⟨fixed: V_M15⟩; Eleuterio Ávila — governor 1914; Abel Ortiz Argumedo — the rebel governor Alvarado removed.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Alvarado · https://online.ucpress.edu/msem/article-abstract/30/2/366/61571/The-Organization-of-Hacienda-Labor-during-the (via search) · Hulme (as above)
  • Confidence: High on the sequence; the dates for Alvarado's arrival vary by source (27 February appointment; 19 March in Mérida).
  • Divergence check: holds to 1916 (all pre-1916). After: the Sonoran generals (Alvarado, Obregón, Calles, De la Huerta) come from the CEDED north — see M01 Part E — so Alvarado's 1920s arc needs the same ruling as theirs.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "a modernizer who frees the peasants and closes the churches in the same year" is the recurring IHS-versus-anticlerical-state fault line, in the one region where the peasants had a church of their own (see 13, the Talking Cross).

6. The night of 24 September 1915 — the sack of Mérida Cathedral

  • Where: Catedral de San Ildefonso, Plaza Grande, Mérida (built 1562–99 on the site of the Maya town T'Hó; "the oldest cathedral on mainland America").
  • What happened: After speeches in the Plaza Grande calling for an end to "religious fanaticism," a crowd led by dock workers from Progreso broke down the door on Calle 61 and entered the cathedral. They destroyed the 17th-century main altarpiece (ripped from the apse, stripped of gold leaf, dismembered and burned in the street) and other baroque and Churrigueresque altarpieces, images of the Lord of the Conquest, Our Lady of Mercedes and St Ildefonso, damaged three chapels, wrecked the Walcker organ (German-made, 1902), and the cathedral treasury was lost. The municipal band was made to play La Cucaracha [seen only in a search summary of es.wikipedia's cathedral article; not on the pages read — V_M15]. The revered Christ of the Blisters was attacked: one account has the crowd fail to burn it and leave it in the street, where police took it to military headquarters and its fate was lost; the cathedral's later account says the original was destroyed and the image now venerated is a replica. Alvarado disowned the attack — though a Yucatán Times summary says he ordered the cathedral stripped. Only two carved panels of the altarpiece survive (the Adoration of the Shepherds in Mérida's city museum; the Adoration of the Magi at the Dzibilchaltún museum). Three days later the wrecked cathedral was handed to Canon José Correa and Father Pablo Ortiz.
  • The odd detail: the band was ordered to play a revolutionary dance-tune while the altar burned; the Christ that survived is said to have been left lying in the street.
  • People: Salvador Alvarado — governor (disowned it); Canon José Correa and Fr Pablo Ortiz — received the ruin; the Progreso dock workers — the crowd.
  • Sources: https://www.marista.edu.mx/noticia/760/evocan-el-saqueo-a-catedral (read) · http://colonialmexico.blogspot.com/2013/12/the-altarpieces-of-yucatan-lost-retablo.html (read) · https://theyucatantimes.com/2023/05/san-idelfonso-cathedral-one-of-meridas-most-precious-gems/ (read) · https://revistabicentenario.com.mx/index.php/archivos/el-atentado-a-la-catedral-de-merida/ (title/snippet via search only — page would not open)
  • Confidence: High on the date, the crowd, the altarpiece and organ; Medium on Alvarado's role and the Christ's fate (the accounts disagree).
  • Divergence check: holds — 24 Sep 1915 is pre-1916 and UNCEDED. The setting's own hook ("secular destruction of consecrated ground") is here in its purest real form.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): anti-clerical modernisers strip a cathedral that stands on the Maya town of T'Hó — the setting's line about "records lost with the priest who kept them" lays easily over the lost 17th-century altarpiece and treasury. One account has the crowd fail to burn the Christ of the Blisters; whether it was left in the street or destroyed is exactly the kind of fact an IHS file would keep in two versions.

7. Felipe Carrillo Puerto — from Motul to the firing squad — 1874–3 January 1924

  • Where: Motul → Mérida → flight to the coast near Holbox → Mérida (UNCEDED).
  • What happened: Born 8 November 1874 in Motul, Maya-speaking, a small rancher who had spent time with Zapata, he became Alvarado's rural organizer (agente de propaganda) and in March 1917 centralized the Ligas de Resistencia of the Socialist Party of Yucatán. Governor from 1 February 1922, he gave his inaugural address in Maya, opened 417 schools in the first year, founded the Universidad Nacional del Sureste (25 February 1922), backed birth control and women's rights, and tied the state's future to its ruins (roads to Chichén and Uxmal). His last, most radical move: agrarian laws of 28 November–4 December 1923 (expropriation of whole haciendas, collective operation by workers). When the De la Huerta rebellion began (4 December), the Mérida garrison joined it. He fled, was captured near Holbox on 21 December 1923, and was shot in the early hours of 3 January 1924 with three brothers (Wilfrido, Benjamín, Edesio) and eight companions. One reading (Howard S. Phillips of the Chicago Daily News, quoted in Alma Reed's memoir) says the rebellion's arms came via British Honduras, backed by Royal Dutch Shell's Mexican subsidiary, and that the hacendados' land interests made Carrillo Puerto's removal necessary (how far they fomented it, Phillips leaves unclear ⟨fixed: V_M15⟩); the historian James Carey concludes "some members of the old Casta Divina took financial roles." His enemies called him the "red dragon with the green (or jade) eyes."
  • The odd detail: the Chicago Daily News man had the story of who armed the rebels and never printed it (Hulme: apparently to avoid involving the country of his birth — he was English-born); it survives because Reed quotes it in Peregrina (published 2007), and Hulme thinks she would have known him socially after 1952 — when and how he told her is not stated ⟨fixed: V_M15⟩.
  • People: Felipe Carrillo Puerto — governor; Manuel Berzunza — his predecessor; Felipe G. Cantón — planter who took over the Henequen Export Commission in 1924; Howard S. Phillips — Chicago Daily News correspondent.
  • Sources: https://escholarship.org/content/qt516354z1/qt516354z1_noSplash_390e6f0f30ac10542dc8bb67df440d8d.pdf?t=r4vo44 (Hulme 2021 — read in full) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felipe_Carrillo_Puerto
  • Confidence: High on the dates and the fact of execution; the who paid story is Low-Medium (memoir-recorded, contested — Hulme calls the outline "undisputed" but the causes "myth-making on a grand scale").
  • Divergence check: the killing sits in an unceded state, so it can happen as recorded — but the De la Huerta rebellion needs a sovereign Mexico with Obregón as president (a Sonoran), so the reason bends (see 5). British Honduras is one of the colonies the setting has America buy at the fire sale (§8.4) — so "arms via British Honduras" becomes "arms via an American colony." Arms from Germany's patron would fit the setting's "Germany as patron of other people's revolutions."
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): an unsolved political killing with a paper-trail through a Chicago newsman, a Havana-linked caste, Royal Dutch Shell and a colony; the Circle's fingerprints are exactly what a Chicago investigator finds in a Daily News file.

8. Alma Reed and "Peregrina" — Valentine's Day 1923 – 1966

  • Where: Mérida (the February 1923 reception), Uxmal, Chichén Itzá; later New York (Delphic Studios); Mérida General Cemetery.
  • What happened: Alma Reed (born San Francisco 1889) arrived in Yucatán in early 1923 (via the SS Mexico from New York; she reached Progreso in February) as the New York Times's correspondent with the visiting archaeologists. She met Carrillo Puerto at a reception on 14 February 1923; the party visited Uxmal on 15 February. They fell in love; that summer the poet Luis Rosado Vega wrote the words and Ricardo Palmerín the music of the song "Peregrina" at the governor's request — it began as a Cuban-style danza and later became a bolero; Rosado Vega said the inspiration came in an afternoon rain on the road into San Sebastián. They were engaged in July 1923 and planned to marry in San Francisco (in January 1924 per Hulme; March 1924 per another account) and live at "Villa Aurora." The telegram announcing his death reached her at the Fairmont Hotel in San Francisco, addressed to Pixan Halal — her Maya name. She wore a copper bell from the Sacred Cenote in a red-triangle setting inscribed, in Maya, "I will not forget." She later founded the Delphic Studios in New York (1928), became José Clemente Orozco's patron, and died on 20 November 1966. She was buried in Mérida's General Cemetery, and used to visit his tomb each 3 January.
  • The odd detail: her April 1923 Times scoop on the Sacred Cenote (see 9) outed Thompson as a smuggler at the very moment her lover was building an alliance (Carnegie, the planters, the Maya) in which Thompson was a guide — Hulme notes she "paid no heed" to the consequences.
  • People: Alma Reed — journalist and patron; Luis Rosado Vega — poet; Ricardo Palmerín — composer; Manuel Cirerol — met her ship in July 1923 with a string orchestra.
  • Sources: https://cultura.nexos.com.mx/alma-m-reed-la-peregrina-1889-1966/ (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alma_Reed · Hulme (as above)
  • Confidence: High on the dates and the song's authors; Medium on the "planned wedding 1924" detail (sources differ slightly).
  • Divergence check: holds to 1923 (pre-break in the sense of Mexico being sovereign here); Reed's later New York career happens in a Depression the setting reframes but does not erase. Orozco's US commissions (Pomona 1930) sit on American soil.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the "who-was-she-to-him" question with a period songbook attached: a Chicago revue in 1934 sings "Peregrina" and a Yucatecan émigré weeps. Alma is a ready NPC (a real woman, so sketch, don't cast).

Part B — The archaeologists: Chichén Itzá, the Sacred Cenote, and the spies in the ruins (1894–1940)

9. The Sacred Cenote — Thompson's dredge, the consular mail pouch, and the lawsuit — 1904–1911; 1923; 1926; 1943/44

  • Where: Chichén Itzá, Yucatán (the Cenote Sagrado — about 60 m across, cliffs of ~27 m to the water, joined to the site by a ~300 m sacbe); the Peabody Museum, Cambridge, Massachusetts.
  • What happened: Edward H. Thompson, US consul in Yucatán, bought the Hacienda Chichén (with the ruins) in 1894 with funds secured by Alison V. Armour and worked the site for thirty years on behalf of the Field Columbian Museum (Chicago), the American Antiquarian Society and Harvard's Peabody Museum. From 1904 to 1910–11 he dredged the well with a bucket-and-pulley rig ("ten hauls in the morning and six or eight in the afternoon"), and in 1909, with two Greek divers, he descended and "trod the bottom" himself. The catch: gold discs, jade, copper, copal, rubber, cloth, wooden weapons and human bones. Friar Diego de Landa (1566) had written that the Maya threw living people into the well; modern scholars think most victims were killed first. Thompson sent the objects to Harvard — Thompson himself told Alma Reed in February 1923 that all of it went "via the U.S. consular mail pouch." Her Times cable ran on 2 March 1923 ("Great Maya Find of Relics Revealed"); when she and Roberto Casas Alatriste turned up unannounced at the Peabody, the hoard was in an upper-floor storeroom in rows of glass-covered cases. T. A. Willard's book The City of the Sacred Well (1926) put a value on it; Mexico seized the hacienda (1926) and charged Thompson with theft and illegal export; he was sued in a civil case for over a million pesos (the "1.3 million" figure [UNVERIFIED — V_M15]; Hulme says "more than a million"), with the Peabody and Harvard named as accomplices. He never returned and died in New Jersey in 1935; the Mexican Supreme Court dismissed the civil case in 1943 or 1944 (sources differ) and the hacienda went back to his heirs. Some objects were returned in the 1960s–70s — 334 artifacts in two tranches, with about 2,700 said to remain (Hulme fn. 7); a search summary gives 1959 and 1976 ⟨fixed: V_M15⟩. Modern accounts put about 30,000 objects into the Peabody's holdings through Thompson's expedition.
  • The odd detail: the treasure of the Maya was smuggled through the diplomatic bag of the United States consulate, by the consul.
  • People: Edward H. Thompson — consul, hacendado, dredger; Alison V. Armour — financier of the 1894 purchase; Alma Reed — the journalist he confessed to; T. A. Willard — sponsor and author whose book triggered the seizure; Roberto Casas Alatriste — Mexican companion at the Peabody.

10. The Carnegie project at Chichén Itzá — permit of 3 July 1923; the Temple of the Warriors, 1925–1931

  • Where: Chichén Itzá; the Hacienda Chichén as base.
  • What happened: The Carnegie Institution of Washington approved a Chichén project in December 1913; the Mexican Revolution and the Great War delayed it. Its Yucatán front was the Archaeological Institute of Yucatan, announced in late October 1922; on 15 February 1923 its scouting party (with Carnegie's president John C. Merriam and trustee William Barclay Parsons) toured Uxmal with Governor Carrillo Puerto. The Mexican government granted the Carnegie a ten-year permit on 3 July 1923 (later extended another ten). Excavation began in the 1923–24 season under Sylvanus G. Morley, with Earl H. Morris as field director; from January 1925 there were four full seasons on the Temple of the Warriors (with the "complex of a thousand columns" that suggested Toltec influence). The project wound down in 1940.

  • People: Sylvanus G. Morley — Carnegie's Maya director; Earl H. Morris — field director; Ann Axtell Morris — copyist of the murals; Jean Charlot — artist; John C. Merriam — Carnegie president; William Barclay Parsons — trustee and engineer.

12. The Mason–Spinden expedition — the New York Times goes east, January–May 1926

  • Where: the east coast of Yucatán/Quintana Roo (Cozumel, Muyil, Okop, Paalmul, Chakalal, Xkaret; Tulum).

Part C — Quintana Roo and the Cruzob: the Talking Cross and the last of the Caste War (1850–1935)

A note on manner. The Cruzob ("those of the Cross") are a real people and a living faith: the Church of the Speaking Cross is still kept by families of guards in Felipe Carrillo Puerto and the shrine villages, and Mexico formally recognised it in 2002. The period's sources call them sublevados ("the risen"), "rebels," "savages," "Indians." Those are the sources' words, not ours. In the setting's terms this is the clearest case of cult versus population inverted: the whole population is the religion, and there is no villain in it.

13. The Talking Cross — Chan Santa Cruz, October or November 1850

  • Where: the jungle near a cenote known as Lom Ha ("Cleft Spring"), later the town of Chan Santa Cruz ("Little Holy Cross"), now Felipe Carrillo Puerto, Quintana Roo (UNCEDED).
  • What happened: The Caste War of Yucatán had begun in 1847 (the Tepich uprising is dated 30 July 1847 in most accounts; one source says 30 June). In October or November 1850, in the fourth year of the war, a small cross appeared on a mahogany tree beside a cenote. It "spoke." The rebel leader José María Barrera said it had been sent to help the Maya against the whites; the voice was that of Manuel Nahuat, an Indian ventriloquist who, in the version told by Heyden, had been instructed by Barrera, "supposedly the inventor of the device." Juan de la Cruz preached its sermons. Nahuat was discovered by government troops and killed (1851 in one account, after a failed attack on Kampocolché on 4 January 1851), and the cross taken or destroyed — but other crosses appeared, some without voice (written messages attached to them, or a "voice" interpreted by patrons or priests), and three "daughter" crosses replaced the original at Chan Santa Cruz, with others at Tulum, Chancah, Chumpón and Muyil. Later leaders worked the oracle: after Barrera's death (31 December 1852) Venancio Puc took control and, per the HistoricalMX account, used trickery to make it speak; a man named José Nah was exposed as its "speaker" and executed in December 1863. The faith organised a Church: a Nohoch Tata (supreme pontiff), cardinals, archbishops and eighty priests, with a parallel military command. Because the Catholic Church had barred the Maya from ordination, the maestros cantores — lay choir-masters — became the priests. The crosses are dressed in huipiles and skirts, fed twice weekly with suckling pigs, and honoured with fiddle-and-drum mayapax, culminating each 3 May (the Day of the Holy Cross). Heyden and Villa Rojas note that speaking idols already belonged to the Maya religious tradition (the priests spoke for them at Cozumel), which is why the Cross was so quickly and fervently accepted.
  • The odd detail: Britain recognised Chan Santa Cruz as a de facto independent country from the 1850s to 1893 and traded with it (arms and gunpowder among the trade); in 1887 the Cruzob petitioned London to make them a British protectorate.
  • People: José María Barrera — mestizo rebel general; Manuel Nahuat — the ventriloquist; Juan de la Cruz — the preacher; Venancio Puc — later leader; José Nah — "speaker of the cross" (HistoricalMX).
  • Sources: https://revistas-filologicas.unam.mx/tlalocan/index.php/tl/article/download/292/290 (Heyden, "Birth of a Deity"; read via text extraction) · https://historicalmx.org/items/show/145 (read) · https://mexicounexplained.com/mayan-cult-talking-cross/ (read) · https://naatikmexico.org/blog/the-talking-crosses-of-felipe-carrillo-puerto (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chan_Santa_Cruz (read) · https://www.mexicolore.co.uk/maya/home/british-involvement-in-nineteenth-century-maya-rebellions (read)
  • Confidence: High on the outline; the accounts differ on who first inspired the cross (Barrera vs Juan de la Cruz), whether the original was destroyed in 1851, and whether the "ventriloquist" explanation is the rebels' own belief or the government's charge — the entry reports the government-side charge as such and does not treat it as settled.
  • Divergence check: holds — 1850, and the town is in UNCEDED Mexico. What shifts is the British side of the story: in the setting Britain is a defeated empire that has sold British Honduras cheap to America (§8.4).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the setting's three layers of truth: (1) the period says "a fraud, a ventriloquist"; (2) the real folklore is a Catholic-Maya faith with pre-Hispanic speaking-idol roots; (3) what is actually there is the GM's call — but do not make it the monster. The right use is a population that outlasted a war because of its faith, met by outsiders who arrive expecting a cult.

14. The fall of Chan Santa Cruz — 4/5 May 1901 — and the making of a Territory, 1902

  • Where: Chan Santa Cruz → renamed Santa Cruz de Bravo; the new Federal Territory of Quintana Roo (UNCEDED).

18. Chicle — Chicago's chewing gum out of the Maya forest, 1920s–1930s

  • Where: Quintana Roo, Campeche and the Petén-Belize forest; William Wrigley Jr.'s Chicago.
  • What happened: Chicle — the latex of the sapodilla tree — was the base of American chewing gum. Wrigley's (Chicago) built a 1920s "bull market" in Yucatecan chicle by sending gum to every address in every state's phone book; Philip Wrigley blended chicle from Campeche and Quintana Roo with chilte from Talpa, Jalisco. By the 1920s the average American chewed over a hundred sticks a year; by 1930 the US imported some 15 million pounds of chicle. Chicleros (tappers) scored the bark in zigzags for months in the forest; they lived under debt-servitude because the chicle companies sold them all their food; foreign gum firms were given land grants that displaced local Maya; over-bleeding killed much of the tree stock. In the 1940s synthetic bases ended the boom (general knowledge; not verified this session). The east-coast Maya's ferocity, Heyden says, had been softened by money brought by "red men" (North Americans) searching for chicle and for ruins.
  • The odd detail: the lost Maya city Calakmul was found in the same chicle forest by a botanist studying how to tap sapodilla for the gum industry (see 19).
  • People: William Wrigley Jr. and Philip K. Wrigley — Chicago gum makers; the chicleros.

19. Calakmul — rediscovered from the air, 29 December 1931

  • Where: Campeche, about 35 km north of the Guatemalan border and 38 km north of El Mirador (UNCEDED).
  • What happened: On 29 December 1931 the biologist Cyrus L. Lundell of the Mexican Exploitation Chicle Company (one source: the Tropical Plant Research Foundation, Washington, which had sent him to study how to tap Achras zapota for the gum industry) rediscovered the Maya city from the air. He named it "Calakmul" — ca (two), lak (adjacent), mul (artificial mound), "City of the Two Adjacent Pyramids." He reported it to Sylvanus Morley at Chichén Itzá in March 1932 and Morley visited that year; Carnegie surveys in the 1930s mapped the core and recorded 103 stelae. Calakmul was the first of some sixteen sites Lundell found [UNVERIFIED — V_M15]. (Lundell: born 5 November 1907, died 28 March 1994; his early work was for the sapodilla-gum trade, of which Wrigley was "a prominent user.")

20. Río Bec B — the temple "lost for 60 years" that locals never lost — 1912; the 1930s model; 1973

  • Where: Campeche (Río Bec region, near the Quintana Roo line).
  • What happened: The Río Bec group was first reported by the French explorer Maurice de Périgny in 1908. In 1912 an expedition for Harvard's Peabody Museum under Raymond E. Merwin with Clarence L. Hay photographed and planned "Building B" — the best-preserved building found in the region, six rooms flanked by two towers with decorative cresting. Vegetation covered it again and its location became an enigma to scholars; a scale model built from the 1912 photographs stood in New York's American Museum of Natural History from the 1930s, while the building itself was, to the outside world, lost. It was not lost to the woodcutters and chicle workers who had crossed the region since the late 18th century; one of them, Juan de la Cruz Briceño (1934–2006), guided the documentarians Suzanne and Hugh Johnston to it, and the New York Times announced on 12 June 1973 that the "Mayan Temple Lost for 60 Years Is Rediscovered." Excavation later showed that the "temple" had interior benches — it was an opulent residence.
  • The odd detail: in the 1930s a museum in Manhattan exhibited a copy of a building whose whereabouts no scholar could give.
  • People: Raymond E. Merwin, Clarence L. Hay — the 1912 expedition; Maurice de Périgny — 1908 discoverer of the group; Juan de la Cruz Briceño — the local guide; Suzanne and Hugh Johnston — 1973.
  • Sources: https://cemca.org.mx/el-templo-maya-perdido-que-no-estaba-perdido-ni-era-un-templo/ (read) · https://washington.office.cnrs.fr/rio-bec-an-extraordinary-mayan-site-hidden-in-the-heart-of-the-yucatan-jungle/ (via search)

21. Blom and La Farge find La Venta — the Tulane expedition of 1925 (Tribes and Temples)

23. Aluxes, the Xtabay and the Dwarf of Uxmal — Yucatec folklore and its earliest attestations (LABELLED FOLKLORE)

  • Where: milpas, ruins and ceiba trees across Yucatán; Uxmal.
  • What happened: (a) Aluxob (singular alux) — knee-high invisible beings of the field and the ruins, said to be "created with mud, leaves and divine breath by the ancient Mayan gods"; in modern tradition a farmer builds a little house for one in the milpa, it guards the crop for seven years, and then the doors and windows are closed to seal it in. Wikipedia notes there is "no direct evidence" of pre-Columbian belief. First scholarly attestation found this session: Chan Kom (1934, chapter VII; "p. 120" [UNVERIFIED — V_M15]) — a carved figure called "Alux" that villagers treated as the portrait of a supernatural being. (b) The Xtabay — the woman who waits behind the ceiba and lures men to death; in the story-cycle a "good" woman, Utz-colel, and a "bad" one, Xkeban, whose death turns her into the sweet xtabentún flower, while the cold one becomes the foul-smelling tzacam cactus and, praying to evil spirits, the Xtabay. The name comes from Ixtab, the Maya goddess of death by hanging. Wikipedia points to Frederick Starr's "Notes upon the ethnography of southern Mexico" (1904) as earlier ethnographic documentation — not checked by me. (c) The Dwarf of Uxmal — a magical dwarf hatched from an egg who built the Pyramid of the Magician (Adivino) in a single night to defeat a king; the earliest written account is John Lloyd Stephens's Incidents of Travel in Yucatan (1843), heard from local guides. (d) The iiko'ob — the harmful winds that live in ruins — which Gann's 1926 hosts asked their god to keep from him (see 15).
  • The odd detail: in the Xtabay story the woman the village called "bad" becomes the fragrant flower and the one it called "virtuous" becomes the demon — the moral is inverted on purpose; and the alux is a tenant: you give it a house and, after seven years, you must lock it in.
  • People: John Lloyd Stephens — traveller; Redfield and Villa Rojas — 1930 field workers; Frederick Starr — ethnographer (attribution per Wikipedia).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alux (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xtabay (read) · https://mayan.org/myths/dwarf-of-uxmal/ (via search) · Chan Kom (as in 22)

25. Tozzer among the Lacandón — three winters, 1902–1905 (LIVING RELIGION; the period's frame marked)

  • Where: the Lacandón forest of Chiapas and Campeche; a small settlement on Lake Pethá.
  • What happened: As Traveling Fellow of the Archaeological Institute of America, sponsored through Charles P. Bowditch and Harvard's Peabody Museum, the Harvard anthropologist Alfred M. Tozzer spent three winters (1902–05) living with and studying the Lacandón Maya; in 1903 he lived for several weeks at Lake Pethá "witnessing and even participating" in their ceremonies and returned in 1904. His PhD and A Comparative Study of the Mayas and the Lacandones (1907) compared their ceremonial life with that of the Yucatec Maya. The Lacandón call themselves Hach Winik, "the Real People." Their religion centres on god-pots — clay incense burners with a deity's face on the rim, "considered to be alive and to have a soul," animated with sacred pebbles from Maya ruins and cacao beans and replaced in renewal ceremonies (Tozzer's plan shows fifty pots, large and small, ceremonially disposed of, of which about ten remained through the year); vessels were left at the ruins of Yaxchilán. Male heads of household perform basic offerings; certain men are recognised for ritual knowledge and healing. In 1943 their language was described as "almost extinct." (Casa Na Bolom, founded by Blom and Gertrude Duby in San Cristóbal in the early 1950s, is outside the period.)
  • The odd detail: the pots are alive and are renewed on a calendar — an object that is a person, replaced when it is full or broken.
  • People: Alfred M. Tozzer — Harvard anthropologist; Charles P. Bowditch — patron; the Lacandón — the community.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Tozzer (read) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lacandon_people (read) · https://www.penn.museum/sites/bulletin/2834/ (via search — the god-pot count)
  • Confidence: Medium-High (the god-pot detail is one search summary; the fieldwork dates are consistent across sources).
  • Divergence check: holds — UNCEDED Chiapas, and the forest lies inside the Golden Circle's ~1,200-mile map of Havana (my own great-circle estimates, not in the setting's files: Havana → Mérida ≈ 490 mi, → the Lacandón forest ≈ 700–800 mi, → Oaxaca city ≈ 1,000 mi).

26. The Chamula rising of 1867–70 — the talking stones — and the crucifixion that never happened (LIVING MEMORY; the period's racism marked)

  • Where: Tzajalhemel, the road to San Cristóbal de las Casas; San Juan Chamula; San Cristóbal (Chiapas, UNCEDED).
  • What happened: In the late 1860s three obsidian stones turned up at Tzajalhemel on the San Cristóbal road; Agustina Gómez Checheb, a Chamula woman, found them, and the Chamula fiscal Pedro Díaz Cuscat helped interpret their divine messages. The stones "spoke"; Cuscat told arriving pilgrims that Agustina had borne them and was "the Mother of God." The parish priest Miguel Martínez punished them; when he and three others seized one of the idols, the crowd killed all four. An anarchist from San Cristóbal, Ignacio Fernández de Galindo, joined the Chamula, trained about 7,000 armed fighters and marched to the edge of San Cristóbal to demand the release of Cuscat and Agustina; the Convenio de Esquipulas ended it with Galindo, Luisa Quevedo and Benigno Trejo imprisoned (Galindo and Trejo were later executed). The Chamula "lost the war, though they won several battles." The crucifixion story — that on Good Friday 1868 Cuscat ordered a child (named as Domingo Gómez Checheb, aged 10–11) crucified to make a Chamula Christ — comes from a single account published in 1888 by the San Cristóbal conservative Vicente Pineda, two decades after the supposed event; no contemporary document supports it, Pineda framed the Indians as "nuevos judíos" in the old blood-libel pattern (the "Santo Niño de la Guardia" myth, Toledo 1490), and the historians Jan Rus and Ulrich Köhler treat it as fabricated mythology. Rosario Castellanos's novel Oficio de tinieblas (1962) and its 1979 film made it a popular "fact."
  • The odd detail: a stone that speaks in 1867 and a cross that speaks in 1850 — both used by an oppressed Maya people to make God talk back.
  • People: Agustina Gómez Checheb — discoverer of the stones; Pedro Díaz Cuscat — fiscal and interpreter; Ignacio Fernández de Galindo — anarchist ally; Miguel Martínez — priest killed; Vicente Pineda — the 1888 chronicler.
  • Sources: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebeli%C3%B3n_Chamula_de_1869 (read) · https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S1665-80272015000100010 (read — the crucifixion as ritual-crime myth) · https://www.culturalsurvival.org/publications/cultural-survival-quarterly/possible-sacred-aspects-chiapas-rebellion (via search)
  • Confidence: High on the events' outline and on the fabrication of the crucifixion (scholarly consensus per the article read); details of the number of fighters are Spanish-Wikipedia level.

27. "The last stone of the last church" — Grajales's Chiapas, 20 November 1934 (and the saints in the caves)

  • Where: Tuxtla Gutiérrez, San Cristóbal and the Zoque country (Copainalá, Tapalapa, Pantepec, Coapilla, Chicoasén) of northern Chiapas; Garrido's Tabasco next door.
  • What happened: Colonel Victórico Grajales, governor from December 1932 (the standard list ends his term in 1936; one study gives 1932–38), ran a "defanatization" campaign — his slogan, quoted by the Chiapas historian Julio Ríos Figueroa, was that humanity would not reach perfection until "the last stone of the last church" fell on "the last priest." Following predecessors Carlos A. Vidal and Raymundo Enríquez, he passed laws limiting priests to four for the whole state, cut that to one by 1934, ordered saints' names dropped from place-names, and used a Ley de Prevención Social to force every Catholic priest out of the state. On 20 November 1934 he ordered the burning of religious images in the public squares of the state's main towns. In the Zoque north the people carried their saints out of the churches before the "saint-burners" arrived — some hidden in houses and in caves in the hills, some later given chapels of their own; Copainalá's municipal president, René Cortázar, was nicknamed "el quemasantos" and burned the rest. The oral testimonies (recorded in 2003 by the INAH researcher Virginia Guzmán Monroy) say raiders from Tabasco — "garridistas" — crossed the border to sack churches. A different, earlier layer: Carrancista troops in 1914–18 looted the churches, and the same informants say that "revolutionaries" (they variously name constitutionalists, Zapatistas and Villistas) hanged men from the two ceibas outside Copainalá's church and from a great pochota tree in front of the church at Chicoasén — the tree, they say, dried up for it. Cárdenas broke the Calles-Grajales line: the bishop Gerardo Anaya and most exiled priests returned to Chiapas in 1938; the church at Copainalá reopened in 1939 under Fr Felipe Ramos.
  • The odd detail: the state that burned the saints also renamed the towns — a place could no longer be "San" anything — and the people answered by hiding the images so that the saints outlived the governor.
  • People: Victórico Grajales — governor 1932–36; Tomás Garrido Canabal — "el quemasantos" of Tabasco (M01); René Cortázar — Copainalá's mayor; Efraín Gutiérrez — Cárdenas's man who succeeded him; Gerardo Anaya — the bishop who returned in 1938; Virginia Guzmán Monroy — historian.

30. The Oaxaca earthquake — 14 January 1931 — Eisenstein's camera, the fallen churches, the Guelaguetza

  • Where: Oaxaca city; the Valley of Oaxaca (Zimatlán, Guelatao); Miahuatlán de Porfirio Díaz (UNCEDED).
  • What happened: At about 18:50 on 14 January 1931 an 8.0 (Ms) earthquake, intensity X, struck the southern state. One count gives 114 deaths, another over 200; "seventy per cent" of Oaxaca city's buildings were destroyed or badly damaged, and Miahuatlán was reported wholly destroyed. Churches fell on full congregations. The film director Sergei Eisenstein (then filming ¡Que viva México!; his party — Grigori Alexandrov and Eduard Tissé — had entered Mexico in December 1930 or January 1931) reported the disaster: a Mexican account puts 51 dead in the collapse of the church at San Pablo Guelatao (Wikipedia, citing Eisenstein's report, says 71 worshippers died at a fiesta there), and about 20, including the priest Nabor Ramos, in the dome collapse at Zimatlán. The Soviet party chartered a plane, flew in low over the ruins, and shot about 2,000 feet of film — released under titles such as The Disaster in Oaxaca / La destrucción de Oaxaca (one Mexican account calls it "The Disaster in Zimatlán"). Afterwards, "many people abandoned the city" and low property prices meant that "a small number of families were able to gain ownership of most of the city." The recovery produced the Guelaguetza: on 25 April 1932, in the city's 400th-anniversary year, a Homenaje Racial — women of the districts led the regional delegations — was staged on the Fortín hill to lift morale.
  • The odd detail: the first film of the ruined city was shot by one of the world's most famous directors from a chartered plane, apparently hoping to beat the newsreel companies (per a film-history summary via search).

In 1972 much of it was restored and it became the Oaxaca Regional Museum, the home (since then) of the Tomb 7 treasure (see 31); a huge restoration begun in 1994 won the Queen Sofía International Prize.
  • The odd detail: the building that holds the Mixtec treasure was, for well over a century, an army's barracks — and, on one account, the army was not fully out of it until 1994.
  • People: the successive garrisons; the restorers of 1972 and 1994.
  • Sources: https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/4252 (via search) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Santo_Domingo_de_Guzm%C3%A1n (via search) · https://historicalmx.org/items/show/22 (via search)
  • Confidence: Medium (search summaries; the "1866–1902" dates and the 1994 date conflict slightly across pages).
  • Divergence check: holds; and in the setting, where America garrisons Cuba and Mexico's north "and never leaves," the Oaxaca convent is a real precedent for a foreign or state army living inside consecrated ground.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): consecrated ground with a barracks in the cloister — the setting's occupation-of-churches image, already historical. A barracks over an old Dominican vault is a fine place for something to have been left.

Divergence questions this lane raises (proposals — nothing here settles them)

  1. Who signed the Cruzob peace? The 1929 Siurob–May agreement and the 1935 treaty need a sovereign Mexican government south of the treaty line to sign with (see M01 Part E, the "who governs after the treaty" gap). The same rump state would have to be the party at Dzula in April 1933.
  2. Who holds the Isthmus? Carranza seized the Tehuantepec railway in April 1917 and settled with Pearson on 14 December 1917 — the year of the setting's treaty. America already holds the Canal Zone; Germany is the natural rival bidder; Britain is bankrupt. Taken, leased, in receivership like Veracruz's customs house — or left as the one land bridge the empire does not control?
  3. Are the Casta Divina "Havana men"? Molina fled to Paris then Havana; Montes lived there; the Circle's map is centred there. Are any of Yucatán's old henequen families in it?
  4. Who armed the 1923 rebels through British Honduras? In the setting Belize is an American colony; the arms door is then American — or a German patron's.
  5. The archaeologist-spies (Morley, Spinden, Lothrop). Their ONI net began March 1917. In a world where America never enters the war, is it a peacetime service watching Germany's Caribbean footholds — and which faction (Secret Service, Hunter Guild) inherits it?
  6. No USSR: who patrons the anticlerical governors? Alvarado (1915), Grajales (1934) and Garrido (M01) all borrow a socialist vocabulary; without a Soviet Union it is homegrown — or German money again.
  7. A Yucatán republic? (38.) With the north gone and Havana 490 miles away, a Yucatecan secessionist movement is more plausible than the real one.
  8. Eisenstein. The 1931 quake footage and the "Sandunga" film need a Soviet director; the setting's Russia is a defeated empire. Keep, rename or replace.
  9. Cárdenas's 1937 Yucatán reform needs a president; see M01 Part E on the Sonoran chain.
  10. Recognition, September 1923. The US telegram of 1 September 1923 announcing recognition of Obregón's government depends on the Bucareli deal the setting has rewritten.
