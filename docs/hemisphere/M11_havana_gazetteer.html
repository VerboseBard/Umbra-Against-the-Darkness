M11 — A GM's gazetteer of 1920s–30s Havana: the working map of the Golden Circle's centre

  • Lane: M11 — places in and around Havana an investigator team would actually go to, 1920s–30s. Format follows the Chicago sweep's R09 gazetteer (### <Place> — <neighbourhood / address>). Events at these places (the 1933 Hotel Nacional siege, the ABC's tunnel under the Colón Cemetery, the Porra and the shark, the Sergeants' Revolt, Atarés, the 1926 hurricane, the Maine's salvage, the Isle of Pines, Oriental Park's 1915 fight, the Frontón, El Templete's ceiba, the Giraldilla, the 9 o'clock gun) are in M03 and are only cross-referenced here; this file is about the places.

  • Setting sources read first: ALTERNATE_HISTORY.md §8 (esp. §8.2: Cuba, "they simply never leave"; §8.3–8.6) and §9 (the Golden Circle's map centred on Havana); M03's contents and overview (so as not to repeat its events).

How this file was sourced (read this before quoting anything)

WebSearch was not used. Two kinds of source carry most of the weight:

  1. Two period guidebooks, read in full text from the Internet Archive and grepped locally: The Standard Guide to Havana and Cuba (Reynolds, printed 1922; https://archive.org/details/standardguidetoh00reyn) — hereafter "1922 guide" — and Havana Mañana: A Guide to Cuba and the Cubans (Consuelo Hermer & Marjorie May, Random House, 1941; https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.157137) — hereafter "1941 guide". The 1941 guide is one year past the sweep's window but describes the Havana of the late 1930s that its authors had just visited; where it names a business or price it is a tourist's snapshot, not a sworn record. Both guides use the period's own (often condescending, racist or anti-Cuban) framing; where I quote or paraphrase them for an atmosphere or a rumour, the framing is theirs, not mine.
  2. Wikipedia article text (plain-text extracts fetched from the API, so the wording is verifiable) for dates, dimensions and architects.

Where an item rests on only a tourist guide's say-so, or on a widely repeated story I could not trace, it is marked UNVERIFIED or legend. Modern addresses/status are not given unless the source gave them.

How to read the divergence lines. Before 1917 everything happened as recorded. The setting's hinge for Cuba is the February–March 1917 Marine landing (the Chambelona revolt), after which in the record the Marines left in 1922 and in the setting they never leave; the presidents are Marshall 1916–21 → Wood 1921–27 → Dawes 1927–29 → Hoover 1929–33 → FDR 1933– (Wood governed Havana 1899–1902); America never entered the Great War; Germany won; no USSR. Each entry says holds (still makes sense under a permanent occupation), BENDS (happens differently), or CANNOT HAPPEN AS RECORDED (needs a sovereign Cuba, the Great War, a president the setting lacks). Two systematic bends: (a) any building whose story is "built by President Machado / Menocal, seat of the Cuban Congress / of the Republic's government" holds in outline (the Platt-era Republic already had a Congress under US tutelage) but who sits in it and who signs the cheques is open (ALTERNATE_HISTORY §8.6); (b) any 1930s scene involving a US Ambassador (Sumner Welles, 1933; the annual Maine Day speech) needs the setting's occupation authority to stand in — flagged as the setting's stand-in, never as a ruling.

Overview

49 place entries for a Havana a GM can walk his investigators through: the seafront and the Prado; the Spanish immigrants' palaces and the American and German clubs; the cathedral, an asylum church, the secularised convents and a Jesuit weather observatory; the forts (La Punta, El Morro, La Cabaña, Príncipe) and the old city jail; the Colón Cemetery and the older bone-pit in the San Lázaro quarter; the Black Virgin's town of Regla and the old hill town of Guanabacoa across the bay; Vedado's rock-cut sea baths and a mansion with a moat and caves; the Country Club and the American colony; the University; the Bacardí tower with its bronze bat; the track, the casinos, the moonlit garden club, the army's camp and the Tropicana at Marianao; Chinatown; and Guantánamo. What surprised me most: the setting's own cast is already written into the city's bronze — the 1925 Maine monument on the Malecón carries busts of McKinley, Leonard Wood and Theodore Roosevelt and was unveiled with President Zayas and General Pershing present; the Capitolio floor has a kilometre-zero diamond from which every road in Cuba is measured (a ready-made centre for the Golden Circle's map); the 1922 guidebook lists the German Club and the American Club at one address (Prado 83); the Iglesia del Espíritu Santo was Havana's only "immune" church (asylum by papal bull 1772 and royal decree 1773); the Casa de Beneficencia had a foundling wheel that gave every child the founding bishop's surname; Camp Columbia's soldiers' club displayed Batista's battered Chevrolet; the Riverside Club (the Public Works chief's estate) had a moat and drawbridge, caves that had held a private zoo of hyenas and tigers, and a mosaic shrine "brought from India"; and the tourist guides of 1922 and 1941 record a string of rumours and legends with a date on them — the sharks under El Morro's cells, the tunnel from Príncipe to La Punta, the blood in Regla's peeling stucco, the pool at Sans Souci "where infuriated suitors throw the girls who say no." Sourcing: two period guidebooks read in full text (1922 and 1941), plus Wikipedia extracts; no WebSearch. Respect: Regla and Guanabacoa (Part 4) are the Afro-Cuban religious heartland of Havana; the entries there describe the towns, not the religions, and no living religion is made a villain. Divergence: every entry has its own line; the two systematic bends are (a) who holds the Republic's buildings when the Marines never leave (open, §8.6) and (b) the American Ambassador's role, which the setting must stand in for.

Part 1 — The spine: Old Havana, the Malecón, the Prado and the government

Old Havana inside the vanished walls — Habana Vieja, the peninsula between the harbour and the Prado

  • What it was in the 1930s: The colonial city, still the commercial and political core, crowded into the space the old wall had enclosed. The 1922 guide says the wall ran from the harbour shore east of the Arsenal, along a line east of the Prado, to the waterfront in line with the Carcel; La Punta, the Carcel, the Prado, the Tacón Theatre and the Campo de Marte had all lain outside it. Fragments survived in the 1920s at Monserrate and Refugio (near La Punta) and on Monserrate near Teniente Rey; the 1941 guide says another fragment is preserved opposite the Presidential Palace. The shopping streets were Obispo and O'Reilly: narrow, one-way for wheeled traffic, "heavy cornices and overhanging balconies," signs strung across, awnings stretched roof to roof in the sun (1922 guide). The 1899 census counted 235,981 people in Havana, and the same guide, quoting an 1879 US health report, says three-quarters of them lived "in the most densely populated localities in the world" — one-storey tenements round a central court, five to ten people to one or two dark rooms.
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is the whole city in one walk: cathedral, palace square, the fortresses across the water, the banks, the bars, the tenements. The 1941 guide warns visitors that the older streets are harder to get around at night.
  • The odd detail: The city's edge is a ghost. Every "outside the walls" place in this file (Prado, Capitolio, Carcel, Punta, Malecón) is outside a wall that had been pulled down, and a visitor could still stand at Monserrate and Refugio and put a hand on what was left.
  • Sources: 1922 guide (https://archive.org/details/standardguidetoh00reyn); 1941 guide (https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.157137)
  • Confidence: High for the wall route and the 1899 census (guide text); the 1941 wall-fragment beside the Palace is one line of a tourist guide.
  • Divergence check: pre-1917 fabric; holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A city that pulled down its own walls, and whose surviving fragments mark where they ran. A Golden Circle "line" on the map that follows the old wall's route, marked by those fragments, would put the Circle's centre-point on the one street the Spaniards could not defend.

The Malecón — the sea wall from La Punta west along the north shore

  • What it was in the 1930s: Havana's seafront road and promenade, begun in 1901 under the US military government (Wood's occupation) and extended in stages: 1901–02 from the Prado to Calle Crespo (the first roughly 500 m, with a US-built steel-reinforced-concrete roundabout at the Prado, where bands played Cuban music on Sundays); 1902–21 as far as the Maine monument; 1927–30 along the mouth of the bay; by 1923 it had reached K and L streets in Vedado, "where the United States Embassy was built" (Wikipedia's wording). The 1922 guide calls it a "substantial sea wall" with a "broad concrete promenade and macadamized driveway," a bandstand of twenty Ionic columns, and "one of the noblest among the cities of the world." Its purpose was to keep the sea out: the winter frentes fríos (cold fronts) throw waves over it onto the buildings behind. The Miramar Hotel, the first in Cuba whose waiters wore dinner jackets and gold-buttoned vests, stood facing the first roundabout.
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is where Havana walks, fishes and looks at the sea, so it is where a tail, a drop or a body in the water all look ordinary; and it is the string on which the palace, the Punta, the Maine monument, the Hotel Nacional and the American embassy are all threaded.
  • The odd detail: The Malecón is an American work with a Cuban soul: laid out by the occupation in 1901, finished by Machado's engineers in 1930, and used every 15 February as the platform for the annual Maine Day speech at the foot of the Maine monument — in 1941 by "the incumbent American Ambassador," with Cuban and American flags on the city (1941 guide).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malec%C3%B3n,_Havana ; 1922 guide; 1941 guide
  • Confidence: High (dates, stages, the bandstand from period text). The "United States Embassy" placement is Wikipedia's phrasing and should be checked against a period source before use.
  • Divergence check: holds (built 1901–1930 under the Platt regime). BENDS for the Maine Day speech: it is by "the incumbent American Ambassador" in the record; in the setting, where the Marines never left, the speaker is the occupation's own governor or general — the setting's stand-in, not a ruling.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Every 15 February a senior American stands on the Malecón and tells Havana it was a mine. Someone in the crowd has spent thirty years listening for the moment the speaker's voice falters on the word.

The San Lázaro quarter — along the Malecón in Centro Habana (San Lázaro and Belascoain): leper hospital, insane asylum, orphanage, the old cemetery and a quarry

  • What it was in the 1930s: The Barrio de San Lázaro, along the Malecón's central stretch, held the city's institutional periphery: the San Lázaro leper hospital, the Casa de Beneficencia (orphanage), the Casa de Dementes de San Dionisio (insane asylum), the old Espada Cemetery, the quarry, the Batería de la Reina (demolished 1904), and the Torreón de San Lázaro (a Martello-type watch tower at the San Lázaro inlet, "where the Cuba-Key West cable lands," per the 1922 guide). The Espada Cemetery — founded by Bishop Espada in 1804–06 — is built like a Roman columbarium: about 12,000 wall niches rented by the term; when a term lapsed the bones were thrown into the Osario (bone-pit) at its south end. The 1922 guide says its empty niches gave a night's lodging to homeless vagrants in the "reconcentration" under Weyler.
  • Why an investigator goes there: Everyone the city did not want to see was put here: the sick, the mad, the unclaimed, the dead. Case files, asylum records and orphan registers all live in this quarter.
  • Also here — the Casa de Beneficencia (San Lázaro and Belascoain, across from the Malecón): an orphanage for illegitimate children (the 1941 guide says "established by Bishop Valdes in 1709" — ⟨fixed: V_M11⟩ that date is wrong: the foundling home began as the Casa Cuna, founded by Bishop Diego Evelino de Compostela in 1687 and completed by Bishop Jerónimo Valdés about 1710–11, and the San Lázaro/Belascoain building is the Real Casa de Beneficencia y Maternidad of 1794 under Governor Luis de las Casas), still run by the Sisters of Charity in 1941, "famous for the revolving contraption at a side door where the mother places her child … the turnstile whisks around, depositing the baby inside, thus preserving its anonymity." All children left there take the founding bishop's surname (1941 guide). Next door in 1941 stood "the astounding Cerveza Polar sign which shows honest-to-goodness movies from twilight until the early hours," and on the next block "a miniature Coney Island" with merry-go-rounds and side-shows.
  • The odd detail: In 1871 the University students accused of desecrating the tomb of Castañón were executed for it — and that tomb, the 1922 guide says, was one of the wall niches in this very Espada Cemetery. The children of the foundling wheel all carry one dead bishop's name; the dead in the niches were rented, then emptied, by the term.
  • Sources: 1922 guide (Espada Cemetery, Torreón, Batería); 1941 guide (p. 101, Beneficencia); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malec%C3%B3n,_Havana (list of San Lázaro buildings); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Col%C3%B3n_Cemetery,_Havana
  • Confidence: High for the institutions and the niche/osario system; the 1709 date is the 1941 guide's and is wrong ⟨fixed: V_M11⟩ (the Valdés surname rule and the turning wheel are confirmed); Medium for the Castañón niche (1922 guide text, tied to the 1871 execution; M03 has the pilgrimage of San Lázaro).
  • Divergence check: pre-1917 fabric; holds. The Sisters of Charity and the Bishop's name fit the setting's IHS-Church layer without change.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A foundling wheel where every child is registered under one bishop's name is a ready-made hidden lineage: a character raised at Beneficencia is "a Valdés" and can trace nothing. Also a bone-pit that was emptied into and never counted; an IHS priest is sure that the unnumbered dead of the Osario are the reason the Colón was built so big. See M03: San Lázaro of El Rincón.

The Capitolio — Paseo de Martí / Parque de la Fraternidad side, on the old Villanueva railway station site

  • What it was in the 1930s: Brand new. The National Capitol was built 1926–29 for President Machado on land that had been the Villanueva Railway terminal, by the American firm Purdy & Henderson to the design of Eugenio Rayneri Piedra: 207 m by 91 m, dome about 92 m (302 ft), more than 5,000 workers over 3 years 3 months 20 days, about US$17 million. It was the tallest building in Havana until 1956 and had the third-highest dome in the world when built. A 55-step granite stair (Wikipedia gives 55 in one place and 56 in another) climbs to a portico of twelve Ionic columns, flanked by two 6.5 m statues by Angelo Zanelli (Work and The Tutelary Virtue). Inside, under the dome, stands Zanelli's Statue of the Republic: bronze cast in Rome in three pieces, 15 m tall, gold-leaf covered (the article gives 49 tons in one place and 30 tons in another). The floor's central diamond marks Kilometre Zero of Cuba's roads. The two side halls are the Salón de los Pasos Perdidos (Hall of Lost Steps, named for its acoustics) leading to the Senate and Chamber. The 1941 guide charged 25 cents (open 9–5) and noted "marble pillars, expanses of carved mahogany, draperies of the velvet used for peers' robes, specially woven in England, mosaic and gold-leaf ceilings … a curiously Victorian air."
  • Why an investigator goes there: to see a legislature — who votes what, who lobbies whom, and where a bill from the American authorities gets stamped Cuban. The cafés across the Prado (the Saratoga and Pasaje hotels' sidewalk tables with all-girl orchestras, 1941 guide) are where the deputies' business is done.
  • The odd detail: The distance to every town in Cuba is measured from a diamond in the floor at the feet of a golden goddess — and the diamond that visitors see is a replica; the real one, said to have been the Tsar's (Wikipedia: "said to have belonged to Tsar Nicholas II"), was stolen on 25 March 1946 and mysteriously returned on 2 June 1947 (out of window; note only). Another statue, El Ángel Rebelde (The Rebellious Angel), stands in the north patio, "donated after the inauguration."
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Capitolio ; 1941 guide (p. 87)
  • Confidence: High (dimensions, dates, architects). The diamond's provenance is a legend; the 1946 theft is a later story.
  • Divergence check: holds in outline. A Cuban Congress under the Platt regime already existed; in the setting who sits in it, and how much of it the occupation controls, is open (§8.6). The Capitolio is Machado's monument: its story on M03 runs Machado → 1933.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Golden Circle's map (§9, ~1,200-mile radius) needs a centre-point; the Capitolio gives it a surveyed one, a kilometre-zero diamond. "Measure the Circle from the Capitol floor." See M03: Machado & the Capitolio's diamond.

The Presidential Palace — Old Havana, facing the Avenida de las Palmas across from the Malecón

  • What it was in the 1930s: The seat of the Republic's presidents from Menocal (who inaugurated it in 1920) to Batista. Designed by the Cuban architect Rodolfo Maruri and the Belgian Paul Belau (who also designed the Centro Gallego), decorated by Tiffany Studios of New York. The 1941 guide calls it "an amazing conglomeration of about seventeen different architectural styles, topped off by a glass dome like the top layer on a wedding cake," with a special clubroom on the ground floor for the palace guards; a fragment of the original city wall "is still preserved opposite the palace." Before 1920 the residence of the head of state had been the Palace of the Captains General on the Plaza de Armas (1922 guide: built 1777 "to replace the first church"; the Governor-General's residence), which the military governors Brooke and Wood used and which then became the city hall.
  • Why an investigator goes there: The most important door in Cuba. A note to "the Palace" is a note to whoever runs the island.
  • The odd detail: The 1922 guide devotes a paragraph to the old Carcel at the foot of the Prado — which "is not infrequently mistaken by tourists for the Palace" — so that in the same visitors' mind the country's palace and its jail were the same building on the seafront.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_the_Revolution_(Cuba) ; 1922 guide; 1941 guide (p. 101)
  • Confidence: High (architects, dates); the "seventeen styles" is a guidebook opinion.
  • Divergence check: the building holds (1913–20, pre-1917 conception; Menocal). BENDS for who occupies it after 1921: the setting's president in Washington is Wood (1921–27) — whose old post was in the older palace on the Plaza de Armas. Whether Havana has a Cuban president at all (§8.6) is open.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Wood's shadow on the Palace. The older palace on the Plaza de Armas was the seat of the US military government in which Wood governed (1899–1902); the new one was finished in 1920 with Tiffany interiors. A room in the new palace is kept unused, "for the Governor."

The Hotel Nacional de Cuba — Taganana Hill, Vedado, on the Malecón

  • What it was in the 1930s: Opened 30 December 1930 as the National Hotel of Cuba, designed by McKim, Mead & White, financed by the National City Bank of New York, built in fourteen months by Purdy & Henderson, and run by the American managers of the Plaza, Savoy-Plaza and Copley Plaza hotels. Sevillian-Roman-Moorish-Art Deco, on two Greek crosses so most rooms see the sea; steel frame, coral-stone detail. The site had been the Santa Clara Battery (completed 1797 per the 1922 guide; the Count of Ricla's title), and the 1941 guide says "some of the old guns still mount guard." The gardens were "legendary," with a tiled pool and tennis courts; the hotel ran a beach club 15 minutes away with a "perambulating bar" and free shuttle (locker 60¢, cabana $2); the cab fare to town was 40¢. It was renamed Hotel Nacional de Cuba in 1939 and bought by the Chicago developer Arnold Kirkeby in July 1943 (out of window).
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is where the powerful sleep — American special envoys, bankers, gentlemen of the Circle. In September 1933 it housed Sumner Welles; a month later the Cuban officers who had helped depose Machado held it against Batista's men (M03: the siege of the Hotel Nacional, 2 October 1933).
  • The odd detail: The hill is Taganana, named for a Canary Islands cave where the Guanche princess Cathaysa was said to have hidden before she was captured and sold into slavery in 1494; a parallel Cuban legend has a native girl of the same name hiding in a cave beneath this hill, and the novelist Cirilo Villaverde wrote La Cueva de Taganana about it. (The tunnels the hotel now shows tourists date from the 1962 missile crisis — do not put them in a 1930s scene.)
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotel_Nacional_de_Cuba ; 1922 guide; 1941 guide (pp. 13–14)
  • Confidence: High (dates, architects, financing); the cave legend is literary folklore (Villaverde), not a surveyed cave.
  • Divergence check: the opening holds (no dependence on the Great War). BENDS for Sumner Welles as "special envoy": in the record he was FDR's envoy to a sovereign Cuba; in the setting he'd need to be a different person or role under the occupation (setting's stand-in). Also: Wikipedia records the hotel barred Black guests for its first decades, including Joe Louis, Marian Anderson and Josephine Baker.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Nacional is built on a legendary cave that a novelist made into a hunted girl's refuge. A reader of Villaverde in 1930 goes looking for the entrance in the hotel's lower floors; the bank that financed it insists they are "structural." See M03 for the siege (a hotel built on a cave, shelled with 300 officers inside).

The Sevilla-Biltmore — Calle Trocadero at the Prado, between the Malecón and the Parque Central

  • What it was in the 1930s: Opened 22 March 1908 as the Hotel Sevilla, a four-storey Moorish-revival building by Arellano y Mendoza; bought in 1919 by John McEntee Bowman and Charles Francis Flynn and renamed the Sevilla-Biltmore; in 1924 the Bowman-Biltmore company added a ten-storey tower wing with a rooftop ballroom by the New York architects Schultze & Weaver. The 1922 guide says the hotel had a post-office branch; the 1941 guide calls it "a bit older than the Nacional, not quite as sumptuous" but "a completely modern hotel," one block from the Prado, a 20¢ cab ride anywhere in the city, and mentions an arcade with a Bohío bar. The Mercado Colón — a huge, half-condemned market "sprawled over a full city block" bounded by Zulueta, Trocadero, Monserrate and Animas — stood across the street. In 1939 the Italian-Uruguayan Amleto Battisti y Lora bought it; Wikipedia says cocaine arrived weekly from Madrid by airliner and was sold in the Longchamps Restaurant in its arcade at $15–50 a gram.
  • Why an investigator goes there: The American traveller's base; the place a newcomer's luggage, mail and gossip come to rest.
  • The odd detail: Al Capone is said to have booked the whole sixth floor (Wikipedia's version; a citation there gives room 615 — the tellings differ) — legend, unverified; M03 handles the Capone tale. The Sevilla is also where Graham Greene's fictional Wormold is recruited (1958 novel — out of window).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotel_Sevilla ; 1922 guide ; 1941 guide (pp. 14, 50)
  • Confidence: High for the dates and architects; Low for the Capone floor/room; Medium for Battisti's 1939 purchase and the narcotics account (Wikipedia; no primary source read).
  • Divergence check: holds; the building is a Bowman-Biltmore American investment in a Cuba the Americans already ran.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The hotel with a post office and a market at its door is the perfect dead-letter box. A Chicago visitor who "booked the whole floor" (Capone, §27 — the Service knew what he was and took him anyway) is not on the sixth floor for the gambling; the floor below the rooftop ballroom is where a Circle courier leaves the map in pieces at the desk.

The Prado (Paseo de Martí) and its lions — from the Malecón south to the Fuente de la India

  • What it was in the 1930s: The first European-style boulevard outside the walls, proposed 1770 by Don Felipe Fons de Viela and finished in the 1830s under Captain General Tacón (who also built the Carcel and the Tacón Theatre). In 1925 the French landscape architect Jean-Claude Nicolas Forestier redesigned it with trees, coral-stone walls, marble benches — and in 1928 bronze lions (by Jean Puiforcat and the Cuban caster Juan Comas Masique, cast from decommissioned cannon), commissioned by Machado. It was "Fashionable Havana's parade ground on Sunday afternoon" (1922 guide) — a double promenade with seats and carriage drives, linking the Parque Central, Parque de la India and Parque de Colón. Along it stood the Gran Teatro (Centro Gallego), the Sevilla, the Casino Español, the Centro de Dependientes ("an ornate million-dollar building on the Prado") and cinemas. When the Capitolio was built in 1929 the promenade's southern section was removed. Carnival's comparsas processed here (M03); the 1941 guide says the Prado's cafés were best for seeing them.
  • Why an investigator goes there: to be seen, and to see who else is on it.
  • The odd detail: the lions are guns melted down: Havana's promenade is guarded by the empire's cannon. In the 1922 guide, at the Prado's foot stands the Carcel, "outside the wall," and near it, "where the Students' Memorial now stands," had been the place of public execution — the foot of the promenade was also the city's old public killing ground.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paseo_del_Prado,_Havana ; 1922 guide (pp. 56–57); 1941 guide (pp. 52–54, 152)
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: holds (1925–29, Machado).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Cannon-bronze lions and a promenade that was once the execution ground — the kind of place a summoner would call bound. A Prado night-walker who never crosses the lions' line.

Sloppy Joe's Bar — corner of Zulueta and Animas, Old Havana (behind the Parque Central)

Part 2 — Old Havana's institutions: the Spanish societies, the hotels and the churches

The Centro Gallego and the Gran Teatro — Prado at San Rafael, facing the Parque Central (the Galician immigrants' palace)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The home of the Centro Gallego, the mutual-aid society of Havana's Galician immigrants, built 1914–15 by the Belgian architect Paul Belau (who also designed the Presidential Palace) with the American engineers Purdy & Henderson, around the old concert hall of the Tacón Theatre (1838) — the hall that had hosted Sarah Bernhardt and Enrico Caruso and later Anna Pavlova. A Baroque Revival palace with about 97 marble statues by Giuseppe Moretti and Geneva Mercer. The 1922 guide puts the membership at over 25,000; the 1941 guide says each of the Centro Gallego and Centro Asturiano had "about forty thousand members paying monthly dues of two dollars, which entitles them to free medical and dental care, legal advice, education, hospitalization and even free burials." The buildings are "baroque palaces, lavish with marble, loaded with frescoes, murals, statuary and gilt in the best Paris-Opera-House tradition," with marble stairs winding to ballrooms, then "mahogany-paneled libraries, schoolrooms, gymnasiums and billiard rooms." Each society ran hospitals ("quintas") and old-folks' homes in the suburbs; the Hijas de Galicia had a beach club for women. In Carnival they ran weekend balls.
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is a state within the state: forty thousand members, their own doctors, lawyers, teachers, ballrooms and graves.
  • The odd detail: "Cubans really live in their clubs" (1941 guide). A man who belonged to the Centro from his first schoolday to his burial never had to deal with anyone outside it — a perfect place for someone to vanish within a community that looks after its own.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gran_Teatro_de_La_Habana ; 1922 guide (p. 116, clubs); 1941 guide (pp. 52–54, 96)
  • Confidence: High for building facts and the 1941 membership/benefit figures (guide); the "free burials" line is the guide's.
  • Divergence check: holds. Spain's loss of Cuba in 1898 (pre-1916) is what created these societies' role; nothing about the Great War changes them. (Spain's own position in a German-won world is open.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A Galician immigrant society with its own hospital, school and graveyard is the best cover a cell could want. "Cult versus population": the Centro's forty thousand are ordinary; the three men on the junta who order the free burials are not. IHS priests of the Spanish clergy would recruit here.

The Centro Asturiano, the Centro de Dependientes and the Casino Español — the Prado and Zulueta around the Parque Central

  • What it was in the 1930s: The Centro Asturiano (founded 1886 by fifty members of the Asturian colony, for medical care, instruction and recreation) built its present palace in 1927 to the architect Manuel Bustos (Wikipedia's spelling; also given as del Busto); the 1922 guide puts its old club house "on Zulueta street, opposite Central Park," with a ballroom fitted up for $35,000, chandeliers imported at $11,500, a library of 5,000 volumes and night schools; its Covadonga Sanitarium was at the Cerro. The Centro de Dependientes — the clerks' association, founded 1880, 27,000+ members in 1922, "an ornate million-dollar building on the Prado," sanatorium of La Purísima Concepción — ran the Carnival's best masked balls (the 1941 guide: "Nothing like them ever happens in our country; probably the saturnalias of the ancient Romans are the nearest"). The Casino Español, "the Castilian club," dues $7 a month, with "paintings of all the Marqueses of Pinar del Río — the Club's patron family," trophy rooms of Toledo steel and framed letters "signed in the flourishing handwriting of the late King Alfonso of Spain"; the 1941 authors were startled to see "a large room given over to poker tables — what a brash American touch in the midst of all this feudal splendor!" The Manzana de Gómez — Zulueta, Neptuno, San Rafael and Monserrate, facing the Parque Central

  • What it was in the 1930s: Cuba's first shopping arcade, built on part of the old city wall's moat and foundations, planned by Don Julián de Zulueta (1873 origins), designed by Pedro Tomé y Veracruisse (construction began 1890), finished by Andrés Gómez Mena, with four floors added 1916–18 — eight elevators, an X-shaped plan of interior passages, 560 offices (law firms, consular and diplomatic agencies), the Pitman commercial academies, and the Ibero-American Institution of Culture chaired by Fernando Ortiz.   • Why an investigator goes there: to find a lawyer, a consul, a foreign agency office. Any small firm that does not want an address on the Prado has its brass plate here.
  • The odd detail: Andrés Gómez Mena was shot dead in Havana on 11 January 1917 by a Catalan watchmaker, Fernando Reugart, who "argued that Gómez Mena had disrespected his wife" — one month before the Marines landed. The arcade's four diagonal streets "form an X."

The Hotel Inglaterra — Paseo del Prado 416, between San Rafael and San Miguel

  • What it was in the 1930s: The oldest hotel in Havana: origins in an 1844 café (the Escauriza Saloon, later "El Louvre"), the Gran Hotel de Inglaterra opened 23 December 1875. The Cathedral and its Plaza — Empedrado at San Ignacio, Old Havana

  • What it was in the 1930s: Havana's cathedral, Catedral de la Virgen María de la Concepción, the seat of the Archdiocese, begun by the Jesuits in 1748, completed 1777, consecrated 1782 (the 1922 guide gives 1704 and two older bells dated 1664 and 1698); a Baroque church of coral stone, "fossilized marine fauna and flora" visible in the walls, asymmetrical towers, thirty by forty-nine metres. It housed the remains believed to be Columbus's from 1796 to 1898 (moved to Seville when Spain left) — but "the authorities of the Cathedral of Santo Domingo discovered other bones, which they claimed to identify as the real, original and only genuine remains" (1922 guide). In the plaza opposite: the palace of the Conde de Bayona (1720), in 1941 the Havana Club Rum Company's free bar, "with its wildly overgrown patio and cool, dim salas," and a cobbled square "paved in the same cobblestones originally laid down by the Spaniards" (1941 guide). The cathedral museum (25¢, 1941): antique vestments in virgin gold, "the six-hundred-pound, nine-feet-tall, sterling-silver monstrance carried in the Corpus Christi processions." The 1941 authors object to a "modernization": "the hoary old walls are painted to resemble a theatrical back-drop, and the holy water is dispensed from … liquid soap containers." A statue of St Christopher, patron of Havana (cast 1632 in Seville by Martín de Andújar Cantos), is inside, and the seminary of San Carlos adjoins.
  • Why an investigator goes there: the Church's seat in Cuba, the archdiocesan records, the museum's silver and vestments, and a square where an American hunter can shake off a tail.
  • The odd detail: Two graves claim Columbus. The Cathedral held one set until 1898 — first in a niche in the chancel wall, then "in a magnificent tomb erected in the center of the church under the dome" (1922 guide) — while Santo Domingo's cathedral claims the other and built "a costly tomb of sculptured marble" for it. The 1922 guide says the Havana bones were taken "upon the evacuation of Havana by the Spanish."

  • Confidence: High (dates, dimensions; guide wording). The Santo Domingo counter-claim is in the 1922 text; M03's Columbus entry covers the 1898 removal.
  • Divergence check: pre-1917 fabric; holds. IHS (Catholic) presence is a setting given — the cathedral's diocesan seat is where the IHS's Havana desk would sit.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers of truth: the Spanish story (they took the Admiral home), the Dominican story (they took the wrong bones), and what is actually in the tomb. The 1898 removal left an empty niche in a Jesuit-built church and a monsignor who knows what did not come out.

The Plaza de Armas: the Captains General's Palace and the Real Fuerza — Old Havana, the seat of every government since Spain

  • What it was in the 1930s: The Palace of the Captains General (built 1776–92, under Governor Felipe Fonsdeviela, Marqués de la Torre, completed under Luis de las Casas — 1922 guide: built 1777 on the site of the parish church) was, in order: the Spanish governors' residence; the seat of the US military government 1898–1902 (the era of Brooke and Wood); the Presidential Palace of the Republic 1902–1920; then the city hall (Ayuntamiento) — the 1920s to 1967 (now the Museo de la Ciudad). Its street, Calle Tacón, was paved with wooden blocks in 1841 to muffle carriage noise near the government buildings (Spanish Wikipedia). Beside the Palace is the Castillo de la Real Fuerza (1558–77), the oldest stone fort in the Americas, whose 1634 Giraldilla weathervane stands atop it (M03); El Templete and its ceiba are on the same square (M03).
  • Why an investigator goes there: The archives; the city's registry; the place a birth, deed or death is recorded, and the address of the previous government.
  • The odd detail: The building that had been the seat of the US military government in 1898–1902 was, from 1920, Havana's city hall — the ordinary municipal government sitting in the rooms the occupation once used. The street outside is paved with wood (Calle Tacón, 1841) so carriages would not disturb the officials inside.

The Church of the Holy Spirit (Iglesia del Espíritu Santo) — Calle Cuba 702 at Acosta, Old Havana

  • What it was in the 1930s: One of the oldest churches in Havana — founded 1635, rebuilt and enlarged 1648 — a coral-stone Baroque-Mudéjar church about 60 m long, austere, with a wooden ceiling of crossed beams and a three-level bell tower (1808). Part 3 — The harbour, the forts and the jails

La Punta and the harbour mouth — the Malecón's east end, opposite El Morro

  • What it was in the 1930s: The Castillo de San Salvador de la Punta, the small fortress on Havana's western side of the harbour entrance: lookouts from 1559, works begun about 1590 by the engineer Battista Antonelli, a heavy copper chain laid across the mouth to El Morro in 1630 (a lattice of chains and wooden beams in 1762; "some fragments … still remain"), the bulk of it dismantled to three bastions. The 1941 guide says the chain used to be dragged across to Morro every night at nine, "then the cannon at Cabañas was fired to signify the closing of the port for the night. Though the port is no longer closed, the cannon still booms out the nightly signal." Next to La Punta stood a preserved fragment of wall (1922 guide): "the ground in front of the wall was a place of public execution; it was here that certain students of the University of Havana were sacrificed" — the eight medical students shot on 27 November 1871 (drawn by lot from 43 accused of defacing the glass over the tomb of Colonel Castañón in the Espada Cemetery; the 1922 guide says "the oldest sixteen" — ⟨fixed: V_M11⟩ wrong: modern accounts give the eight as aged 16 to 19, and say three were chosen by lot; the 1922 guide's account). Boats to the forts left from the Punta Landing, Caballería and Machina wharves, ten cents each way.
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is the meeting point of the sea road, the boat ferries and the Prado; the place to hire a boat over to Morro/Cabaña or Regla.
  • The odd detail: The 9 o'clock gun outlived the chain — the harbour is not closed at night, but every night the gun still says it is. The 1941 guide: "When the cannon roars, Cubans look at their watches and say 'Nine o'clock' in tones of great surprise, though they should be used to it by this time."
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castillo_de_San_Salvador_de_la_Punta ; 1922 guide (pp. 57–59); 1941 guide (pp. 100–101)
  • Confidence: High (fort history from Wikipedia; the students and the gun from guides). M03 covers the gun and the Giraldilla as events/legend.
  • Divergence check: pre-1917; holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A nightly signal that outlived its purpose — the port's "closing." An investigator who is on the harbour at 9 p.m. hears something answer the gun from the sea side. See M03: El Morro, La Cabaña and the nine o'clock gun.

The harbour and the Maine monument — the Bay of Havana; the monument on the Malecón at the end of Calle Línea

  • What it was in the 1930s: The Bay of Havana was Cuba's front door and its quays: Luz wharf (the ferries to Regla and Casablanca, 5 cents, connecting to the United Railways), Caballería, Machina and the Punta landing. The Maine's wreck lay in the harbour until 1911–12 (M03) — Wikipedia gives the explosion site as about 23°08′07″N 82°20′3″W — and its refloated stern was towed out on 16 March 1912 by the tug Osceola and scuttled in 600 fathoms; the main mast went to Arlington (dedicated as a memorial by Woodrow Wilson in 1915) and the foremast to Annapolis. The Monument to the Victims of the USS Maine was built 1924–25 by Félix Cabarrocas on the Malecón at the end of Calle Línea in Vedado and inaugurated 8 March 1925 with President Zayas and General John J. Pershing present: two 40-ft Ionic columns, two cannon and part of the ship's anchor chain (salvaged 1911) at the base, and a bronze eagle — the first eagle's wings were raised, and the October 1926 hurricane damaged it, so it was replaced with a horizontal-winged one (Wikipedia: the first eagle's head is in the US Embassy in Havana and its body and wings are in the Havana City History Museum ⟨fixed: V_M11⟩; spanamwar.com dates the hurricane 20 October 1926 and says it also brought down the columns and lintel). A plaque read "To the Victims of the USS Maine. The people of Cuba." Three bronze busts of Americans were on it: President McKinley, Leonard Wood (the second US military governor of Cuba, after John R. Brooke; Wikipedia's "first" is wrong ⟨fixed: V_M11⟩) and Theodore Roosevelt [UNVERIFIED — V_M11: no source read dates the busts to the 1925 unveiling; Wikipedia says only that there were "originally" three]; a fourth (Andrew Rowan) was added 4 July 1943. Every 15 February — Maine Day — the American Ambassador spoke at its foot (1941 guide).
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is where the setting's founding story is set in bronze; and on 15 February an occupation official addresses the whole city here.
  • The odd detail: In the 1925 monument, the setting's own history is already sculpted: Leonard Wood — in the setting, the sitting president in March 1925 (1921–27) — and Theodore Roosevelt — the Hunter Guild's founder — stand on the Malecón as two of three bronze Americans, with Pershing at the unveiling. (The monument was pulled down in January 1961 — out of window.)
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_to_the_Victims_of_the_USS_Maine ; 1922 guide (Luz wharf ferries) ; 1941 guide (p. 69, Maine Day)
  • Confidence: High for the monument's dates, artists, busts and unveiling.
  • Divergence check: the 1898 explosion, the 1911–12 salvage, the 1924–25 monument: pre-1917 or holds. BENDS in one respect: Pershing is the setting's Southern War general; his 1925 presence at a Havana unveiling is plausible as an occupation commander. Zayas (real Cuban president 1921–25) is a Cuban figure the setting has not ruled on. (A bronze bust of the man who is the setting's sitting president, put up by a Cuban government he governed: an obvious pressure point.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Two cannon and a length of the Maine's anchor chain at the base are the wreck's only public relics; what the 1911 salvagers actually found (66 sets of remains, one identified — M03) is not on the plaque. Simply: the setting's whole hemisphere story is three bronze heads on a sea wall, and a Pershing in the crowd. See M03: the Maine salvage.

El Morro — the Castillo de los Tres Reyes del Morro, east side of the harbour entrance

  • What it was in the 1930s: The great harbour fort, designed by Battista Antonelli, finished 1589 (the 1922 guide: built 1587–97), 100–120 ft above the sea, moats 70 ft deep, a light tower (lit 1845–46) "visible eighteen miles at sea" (1922 guide). Taken by the British in 1762 (Lord Albemarle; the Spanish commander Velasco was mortally wounded in the final assault, after a British mine was blown under a bastion). By the 1920s–40s it was a sightseeing stop — boats left the Machina Wharf and Punta landing for ten cents; its 1930s garrison and use are not confirmed here. The 1941 guide: "Don't miss the cells below sea level, with chutes cut through the solid rock from which prisoners were thrown to the sharks. Contrary to popular belief, however, this is no longer the accepted method of execution in Cuba."
  • Why an investigator goes there: to overlook the harbour, to shadow a ship in, or because the lighthouse keeper sees everything that comes through the mouth.
  • The odd detail: The "prisoners thrown to the sharks" chutes are a tourist-guide tale in a 1941 book — a rumour with a date and an author, ready to be a real thing. The 1941 authors' joke ("no longer the accepted method") is the source. M03 has the actual shark incident, in Havana harbour in 1930–33 (the Porra's shark).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castillo_de_los_Tres_Reyes_del_Morro (via a fetch summary) ; 1922 guide (p. 63); 1941 guide (pp. 100–101)
  • Confidence: High for the fort; legend (folklore, first attested in what I read: 1941 guide) for the shark chutes.
  • Divergence check: pre-1917 fabric; holds. Cuba's garrison of it in the setting is the occupation's.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A sea-level cell with a chute is the perfect tidal prison for something that will not stay held on land. Whoever built the chutes knew what was in the water.

La Cabaña — the Fortaleza de San Carlos de la Cabaña, the ridge east of the harbour, above Casablanca

  • What it was in the 1930s: One of the largest fortresses in the Americas (Wikipedia: the third-largest; the 1941 guide: "one of the largest fortresses in the world"), built 1763–74 after the British capture: the 1922 guide gives the cost as $14,000,000 and repeats the tale that the king, told the sum, "gazed intently toward the west, declaring that the walls must be high enough to be visible across the sea." A drawbridge and sally-port, ditches 40 ft deep, a vaulted hall to the Laurel Ditch (Los Fosos de los Laureles), "the place of execution of insurgents and political prisoners": scores were shot kneeling, face to the wall, by a file of Spanish soldiers; the line of bullet holes "traceable for 85 feet" was called "the deadline," and a bronze memorial (an angel receiving a dying patriot's soul) was set in the wall by public subscription. The 1922 guide's verdict: "Cabaña has never fired a shot in defense of Havana, nor has it served any other purpose than that of barracks for Spanish troops and a prison house and execution ground for political offenders"; 800 Frenchmen were shut up there in 1795. The 1941 guide calls it "one of the largest fortresses in the world" where the nightly 9 o'clock cannon is fired. The US occupation of 1899 had proposed moving the island's penitentiary convicts here (the plan was dropped).
  • Why an investigator goes there: to get a political prisoner out, or to confirm one is inside. It is the old address of execution by firing squad, the sort of place a fixer asks about in a whisper.
  • The odd detail: The 1922 guide's claim that yellow fever came to Cuba with convict labourers imported from Veracruz to build the Cabaña — "It might be not altogether fanciful to charge up to Cabaña … the entire cost of the yellow fever scourge." (Tale, not a documented finding; UNVERIFIED.) Mexico's Veracruz customs house is the setting's American receivership (§29).
  • Sources: 1922 guide (pp. 72–75); 1941 guide (p. 101); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortaleza_de_San_Carlos_de_la_Caba%C3%B1a (via a fetch summary; 1959 executions under Che Guevara's command are out of window — do not put them in a 1930s scene)
  • Confidence: High for dates and the Laurel Ditch (guide text); the yellow-fever line is legend.
  • Divergence check: holds; the fortress's function under a permanent US occupation is a prison and an execution ground again — cf. M03's Frontón "Palace of Screams" and the Isle of Pines.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Prison as archive: the Laurel Ditch's "deadline" wall is a map — the bullet marks in a line 85 ft long. A Circle man reads them as coordinates. See M03: El Morro, La Cabaña and the nine o'clock gun.

The Castillo del Príncipe — the Loma de Aróstegui, west of the city

  • What it was in the 1930s: A fort built 1767–79 on the hill overlooking the city from the west, "named after Charles of Bourbon, Prince of Asturias" — an irregular pentagon, two bastions and a redan, deep trenches and mine galleries, a garrison for 1,000 and 60 cannon, and a system of red-brick tunnels linking its outposts. It served as Cuba's principal detention facility for over a century (Wikipedia): by 1922 the federal prisoners of the old Carcel had been moved to Príncipe, and the 1941 guide calls it "an old bastioned fortification, now used as a jail." Its prisoners included José Martí's teacher Rafael María de Mendive, Julio Antonio Mella, Eduardo Chibás, Raúl Roa and Juan Marinello, and "El Caballero de París" (per Wikipedia). It overlooks the Avenida Carlos III toward the Quinta de los Molinos — Weyler's "summer residence," in 1941 the University's agricultural experiment station and botanical garden.
  • Why an investigator goes there: to visit a political prisoner (Machado's students; the ABC's men; later the Porra's) or to look at Havana from the best hill in town.
  • The odd detail: The 1941 guide records a rumour: "Rumor says that secret passages, tunneled underneath, connect it with La Punta on the Malecón. Interesting equipment for a prison, if true." A tunnel from the hill to the harbour mouth is what the 1941 guide passes on as a rumour; the fort's real red-brick galleries make it not impossible.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castillo_del_Pr%C3%ADncipe ; 1922 guide (index and its front insert on the federal prisoners); 1941 guide (p. 102, botanical gardens and Príncipe)
  • Confidence: High for the fort and its detainees; the Punta tunnel is a rumour, first attested (in what I read) in the 1941 guide.
  • Divergence check: the fort and its prisoner role hold. Mella's detention (real, 1920s) and the later student and ABC prisoners are Machado-era; in the setting the jailers may be American.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A rumoured tunnel from the political prison to the harbour-mouth fort. The Circle's exfiltration route out of Príncipe is the "tourist joke" — one investigator finds the brick gallery.

The old Carcel and the Vivac — the foot of the Prado ("Carcel street"), and Zulueta near the Colón market

  • What it was in the 1930s: The Havana city jail was the immense yellow building at the seaward foot of the Prado, "not infrequently mistaken by tourists for the Palace": 300 by 240 ft, room for 5,000, built in 1838–39 by Governor-General Tacón with the labour of chain gangs of "runaway slaves, white malefactors and Carlist prisoners," on public funds "diverted by dishonest officials." Its entrance on the Prado was for the Carcel (city jail) and on Zulueta for the Presidio (the island's penitentiary), with a courtroom (audiencia). It held 600 when the Americans came in 1899, "many of whom had been incarcerated for years without trial" — 100 released, 60 commuted. It contained the garrote, "the Cuban instrument of capital punishment" (the 1922 guide describes its mechanism and calls it "a merciful instrument of death," a period judgment). The occupation planned to move the jail prisoners to the Hospital Militar and the convicts to the Cabaña, and to make the building offices or a hotel; the scheme was dropped, and by 1922 "the Federal prisoners have been removed to Príncipe." The Vivac on Zulueta near the Colón market held the municipal prison and a correctional court room.
  • Why an investigator goes there: a police blotter, a court hearing, a name on a list — the ordinary-crime end of the system, at the tourists' promenade.
  • The odd detail: Tourists mistook it for the Palace — the jail was the biggest yellow building on the seafront, and it looked like the seat of government to anyone who didn't know.
  • Sources: 1922 guide (pp. 56–57 and its insert on federal prisoners); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paseo_del_Prado,_Havana (Carcel street)
  • Confidence: High for the 1922 description. What became of the building in the late 1920s–30s I did not confirm (a later demolition is probable but UNVERIFIED here).
  • Divergence check: the fabric and the 1899 clean-up are pre-1917. A permanent occupation would keep it in use and make it more, not less, American.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The seat of government and the jail look alike. An occupation-era clerk transposed the doors — someone is in the Carcel because of a Palace paper.

The Colón Cemetery — Vedado, Zapata and 12th Street

  • What it was in the 1930s: Havana's city of the dead: the Necrópolis de Cristóbal Colón, founded 1876 (first stone 30 October 1871; burials from 1868) to replace the Espada Cemetery, designed by the Galician Calixto de Loira (its first resident, who died before it was finished). About 50 hectares (620 by 800 m), 500+ major mausolea, a Greek-cross plan with an octagonal Central Chapel modelled on the Florence Duomo; the entrance arch's group (Wikipedia: Faith, Hope and Charity by José Vilalta Saavedra; the 1922 guide says a Crucifixion panel by Saavedra topped by Columbus "bringing the light of religion to the New World"). Plots were assigned by class — areas for priests, soldiers, brotherhoods, the wealthy, the poor, infants, epidemic victims, "pagans and the condemned." The Firemen's Monument (Agustín Querol; for the 28 firemen killed in the fire of 17 May 1890) "cost $79,000" (1941 guide). The Capilla del Amor — built by Juan Pedro Baró for his wife Catalina Lasa (the 1941 guide: "one bereaved Cuban spent $150,000 on a mausoleum of Lalique crystal … to house the remains of his wife"). The Students' Monument for the 1871 eight; the Tomb of Calixto García; a plot for the Maine's dead in 1898 (disinterred December 1899 for Arlington); baseball monuments (1942, 1951). An open-air flower market ran along 12th Street and 23rd (1941). The cemetery's extension was completed 1934.
  • Why an investigator goes there: to find a grave, a will's proof, an exhumation record; to meet at a mausoleum; to be near the dead of every trade.
  • The odd detail: Wikipedia says remains are exhumed "after three years … boxed and placed in a storage building." The 1941 guide flatly says the opposite: "Don't believe the old wives' tale that when poor people can't afford to pay rent on graves, the bones are dug up. It isn't so." (The Espada system in the 1922 guide did rent niches and empty them into a bone-pit.) Havana's dead were a matter of rumour, and the sources disagree with each other.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Col%C3%B3n_Cemetery,_Havana ; 1922 guide (pp. 77–81); 1941 guide (p. 102)
  • Confidence: High for the founding, design and figures; the exhumation rule is contested (as above). Catalina Lasa's death year and the tomb's date: UNVERIFIED here.
  • Divergence check: the cemetery holds. The ABC's tunnel under the Colón, La Milagrosa, and the Espada bone-pit are M03.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers: the officials' story (you can't dig them up), the poor's story (they do), and the fact — a storage building that nobody has ever counted. See M03: The Colón Cemetery; La Milagrosa; the ABC tunnel.

Part 4 — Across the bay: Regla, Guanabacoa and Cojímar

Respect note. Regla and Guanabacoa are the Afro-Cuban religious heartland of Havana. Regla de Ocha (Santería), Palo Monte and Abakuá are living religions and institutions (see M03 Part E). The guidebooks that describe these towns in 1922 and 1941 are written by American visitors and use the period's condescending framing; where I use them it is for streets, fares, churches and what a visitor was shown, and it is marked. The population of Regla and Guanabacoa is the population; the setting's monsters are an overlay and are not put on the religions.

Regla — the town across the bay: the Black Virgin's sanctuary, the warehouses and the ferry from the Luz wharf

  • What it was in the 1930s: Reached by a five-cent ferry from the Luz wharf (a ten-minute ride, 5¢ round trip in 1941) that also carried passengers for the United Railways' trains to Matanzas and beyond. A working town of sugar and tobacco warehouses — the 1922 guide names the builder Juan Eduardo Fesser, who has a statue there — a railway terminal, once a bull-ring town, and the seat of the sanctuary of Our Lady of Regla (Nuestra Señora de Regla), a Black Madonna, patroness of Havana Bay and of sailors and boatmen: a hermitage founded by the pilgrim Manuel Antonio (1687/1690), destroyed by the 1692 hurricane, rebuilt 1694 with an image donated by Pedro de Aranda, named patron of Regla on 23 December 1714; her feast is 8 September, with a procession by boat across the bay. The 1922 guide describes the church walls as hung with offerings of miniature arms, legs, hearts and other silver tokens given to the Virgin for answered prayers. In Santería she is Yemayá, the orisha of the sea. Regla is also where the first Abakuá lodge (Efik Butón) was founded in 1836 (M03 Part E), and one of the two places of the 1919 "brujo" lynching panic (M03). The 1941 guide describes an evening there: the Mexico Bar on the main street, "fully a block long," with Mexican murals and "Cuban flag by the yard," and "the only reservadas we have seen complete with victrolas and wash-basins"; the Plaza Facciolo with the Palacio Municipal (city hall, police station and town hospital in one), whose festival lights from the previous 4 September had never been taken down; and the evening paseo, girls walking clockwise, young men counter-clockwise.
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is the port's other side: the warehouse men, the stevedores and boatmen, the people who load what leaves Havana. The 1919 panic (M03) shows what happens when a scared city turns on it.
  • The odd detail: The last piracy legend of the coast. The 1922 guide records that a Regla pirate's "magnificent palace" had red stucco that constantly peeled off, and neighbours said "so much blood is mixed with it that it cannot stick"; the guide says the last act of piracy on the coast was the brig Halcione in 1839, all pirates garrotted in Havana. A folk saying, recorded in a 1922 tourist guide, that is unusually good as a haunted-house line.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Lady_of_Regla (via a fetch summary) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regla (via a fetch summary; the page lacks 1930s or ferry detail) ; 1922 guide (pp. 121–122); 1941 guide (pp. 189–190)
  • Confidence: High for the sanctuary's history; Medium for the 1930s town, which rests on two tourist guides; the peeling-stucco line is folklore first attested in the 1922 guide.
  • Divergence check: holds; the shipping and warehouse economy is the kind an occupation supervises closely. The 1919 Regla panic falls after the setting's 1916 break, but a brujo panic does not depend on Cuban sovereignty or the Great War (M03 gives its own divergence line).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Cult versus population: the Virgin's procession across the bay each 8 September is the single largest crowd of the year and everyone is on the water. The Golden Circle wants a night when the harbour is full of boats and no one is looking at the shore; the IHS's local priest knows the boatmen's devotion is real and will not hand them to the Circle. The peeling red stucco is a ready-made haunted-house detail; the Abakuá lodge of 1836 is not a villain.

Guanabacoa — the old town in the hills east of Regla

  • What it was in the 1930s: A colonial township about five kilometres south-east of Havana — founded as an Indian town in the sixteenth century (the 1922 guide: "the poverty stricken remnants of various tribes being gathered together here"), briefly Cuba's capital in 1555 after the French pirate Jacques de Sores attacked Havana ("Like putting Havana in Guanabacoa" is the saying for fitting something too large into too small a space). It became by 1854 "the fashionable watering place of the island" for the summer (1922 guide); it was "famous for its historical Santería" and the home of the first African cabildo in Havana; and a Jewish community grew up around light manufacturing — Samuel Epstein's Sedanita knitting works in the late 1920s (200 workers, $75,000 of imported equipment) and Charles Shapiro's factory in the 1930s. The 1922 guide describes the mineral baths of Santa Rita and the church of Potosí outside the town "containing the miracle-working image of Jesús Nazareno del Potosí"; the 1941 guide: "the colonial atmosphere preserved in the older homes (really palaces), the narrow streets, barred windows and wall-niches for figures of St. Anthony," the Church of San Francisco with its "centuries-old statue of Our Lady of the Assumption, patron saint of the city" (feast in August, processions of "La Tutelar"), the Convent of Saint Anthony with cloisters of carved mahogany, the Loma del Indio with "mineral springs discovered by the Siboneys and still in use today for medicinal purposes," and the town's specialty papas rellenas. Ernesto Lecuona and Rita Montaner were born there.
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is the town where Havana's Afro-Cuban religion, its old Catholic shrines and its healing springs all sit in one small hillside place — someone looking for a healer, an old house, or a cabildo records goes here.
  • The odd detail: Florida Indians were evacuated to Guanabacoa in 1763 (Apalachees and Yamasees, when Spain ceded Florida to Britain) — a refugee people settled in a town that had been built as a native reduction two centuries earlier.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guanabacoa ; 1922 guide (p. 122); 1941 guide (pp. 190–192)
  • Confidence: High (Wikipedia and both guides agree on the shape of the town); the founding date is given variously (Wikipedia's infobox: 1581; the 1922 guide: "the sixteenth century") and is not settled here.
  • Divergence check: holds. Cuban Jewish life in Guanabacoa is a 1930s Depression-era detail, and in a world where no Nazi Germany exists, the Jewish community's later refugee arrivals (1938–39) would need a different cause.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A town of miracle-working images (Jesús Nazareno del Potosí), healing springs, a cabildo and old palaces: this is where a hunter with an injured investigator goes for a healer — and where the Golden Circle's agents come looking for something the town has no intention of giving them and are marked at once as the outsiders they are.

Cojímar and the "Little Morro" — the bathing beach east of Guanabacoa

  • What it was in the 1930s: A bathing beach on the seashore beyond Guanabacoa, with the old fort taken by the British in 1762, called by the 1922 guide "the 'Little Morro.'" The liquid bitumen found in the hills of Guanabacoa had, the same guide says, been used by Sebastián de Ocampo in 1508 to careen his ships and gave the harbour its early name Puerto de Carenas.
  • Why an investigator goes there: a quiet coast east of the city for a boat or a night landing outside the harbour.
  • The odd detail: The natural tar of Guanabacoa gave Havana harbour its first name (Carenas — "careening").
  • Sources: 1922 guide (p. 122)
  • Confidence: Medium (one 1922 guide; the Ocampo tale is a tour-book history).
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A natural tar seep in the hills — the thing that first sealed a Spanish hull here — is a period-plausible ingredient for anything a summoner wants sealed, and a reason a Circle shipping firm quietly buys land in Guanabacoa.

Part 5 — Vedado, the American colony and the towers

Vedado's seafront — the sea baths, the Trotcha Hotel and the Malecón amphitheatre

  • What it was in the 1930s: Vedado, the planned grid suburb opened for building in 1859 on land that had been a vedado — a forbidden military zone — since the sixteenth century, and the city's rich residential quarter with its gridded numbered streets, was the address of the Hotel Nacional and the University. The 1941 guide describes its shore: three old sea baths — El Progreso (First and Calle Baños), Las Playas (First and Calle D) and Carneado (First and Paseo) — "big ramshackle" buildings open to the sea, pools hollowed out of the coral shelf, "thick, roof-suspended ropes" to cling to, "heavy iron bars run between clumps of rock … This keeps the large fish out and the bathers in, for swimmers are prohibited from venturing outside, where it is open, shark-infested ocean." The Trotcha Hotel, "built in French Colonial style and quite old," with "profusely overgrown gardens with thickly foliaged paths," where American film companies had once shot and where "the old crocodile that occupied a pen at the back of the gardens is gone long since." The open-air amphitheatre on the Malecón at Avenida Céspedes, framed in Ionic columns (the 1922 guide's bandstand), where the Cuban Philharmonic, the Police Band and the Municipal Orchestra played free four evenings a week, 8 to 10:30. A huge municipal playground at Calles G and H with a pool whose subterranean dressing rooms had portholes onto the underwater view.
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is Havana's respectable west end, where the rich live and the public swims at a fixed hour; a body found in the baths raises less alarm than one on the Prado.
  • The odd detail: The bathing pools were cages for people, not for fish: iron bars in the coral kept the big fish out, and swimmers were forbidden to venture beyond them — compare M03's shark in Havana harbour.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedado ; 1941 guide (pp. 61–63); 1922 guide (the Malecón bandstand)
  • Confidence: High for the described places (guide) — but all are the 1941 authors' snapshot.
  • Divergence check: holds.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): A rock-cut pool with iron bars against the sea and a "shark-infested ocean" beyond: a ritual site with a built-in boundary. The Trotcha's overgrown gardens are the standard "abandoned Hollywood set" a GM can land a scene in. See M03: the Porra and the shark.

The Riverside Club — the end of Vedado on Calzada, across the Almendares bridge from Miramar

  • What it was in the 1930s: "An enormous pile of green stucco," built as a private home by Carlos Miguel de Céspedes, head of the Department of Public Works during Machado's reign — "an amusing example of the grandiose extremes to which Cubans were carried during the Dance of the Millions" (1941 guide). The house is "unusual only because of size," but the grounds slope to a private landing pier at the river's edge, "dominated by a mosaic shrine, brought from India at a cost of $200,000 and assembled bit by bit"; "an intricate moat and drawbridge arrangement leads to caves, hollowed out of the natural rock formation, where there used to be a private zoo. Now plaster images of saints on improvised altars within each cave replace the hyenas and tigers! Grottoes, summer houses and trees sculptured into bizarre shapes are scattered with abandon. The result is real Alice-in-Wonderland." It "has undergone many vicissitudes. Sometimes it is open, sometimes it is boarded up."
  • Why an investigator goes there: A public-works chief's private estate is where the men who built the Capitolio and the Central Highway kept their appetites — and their records.
  • The odd detail: A moat and drawbridge into a cave with a private zoo (hyenas, tigers), later replaced by saints' images on makeshift altars. The building of the Republic's public works and the grounds of a man's nightmare are one address.
  • Sources: 1941 guide (pp. 63–64)
  • Confidence: Medium (one 1941 tourist guide; the identification of the owner is the guide's — M03 lists Céspedes as Machado's Public Works secretary "UNVERIFIED"; ⟨fixed: V_M11⟩ his role is confirmed elsewhere (Forestier's patron; Quinta Avenida's end is named for him), but [UNVERIFIED — V_M11: the moat, drawbridge, caves, private zoo, India shrine and the name "Riverside Club" appear only in the 1941 guide; no other page read mentions them]); the Indian shrine cost is the guide's figure.
  • Divergence check: the estate's building predates or straddles 1917–33; the man (Machado's Public Works chief) is a Machado-era figure and BENDS only as much as Machado does (§8.6).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): The Riverside Club is a ready-made summoner's estate: moat, drawbridge, caves, saints' images on altars, a mosaic shrine from India. A Circle member who "collected" it in the Dance of the Millions; the plaster saints in the caves are what a later owner set up, not what was there first. (Do not put a living religion in the caves; the saints here are a ruined rich man's decoration.)

The Havana Country Club, Country Club Park and the Havana Yacht Club — the American colony's suburb

  • What it was in the 1930s: The Country Club of Havana was founded "thirty years ago" (c. 1910–11, by the 1941 guide's count) by Americans and was, the guide argues, "one of the most important factors in changing the social-behavior pattern of the Cubans": before the golf course, Cuban gentlemen spent weekends in bars gambling and gossiping while their wives waited at home; after it, they took up sports and "daughters of Cuba's first families lounge or play on the clubhouse grounds with the same freedom our own girls enjoy" — the credit for "the relaxation of the hitherto rigid dueña system." Country Club Park, around it, was where "the sumptuous homes and estates of Cuba's millionaires put even Beverly Hills in the shade"; at its entrance "a lake shaped like the island of Cuba, illuminated at night," "a favored Lovers' Lane." Rents in the Country Club or Miramar section, 1941: $130 a month for a furnished house. The Havana Yacht Club in Marianao, founded 1886, "the first yacht club around Havana … the citadel of upper-crust society," with a collection of original etchings of early Havana; the Miramar Yacht Club and the Jaimanitas and Rovers clubs are others. The 1922 guide lists the American Club at Prado 83 (with the German Club) as the colony's city address, and Wikipedia's Malecón text records a US Embassy at K and L streets, Vedado, by 1923.
  • Why an investigator goes there: It is where the American colony — bankers, sugar men, embassy staff, consuls — lives and plays; a guest card is the key to every door.
  • The odd detail: A lake shaped like Cuba at the entrance to the millionaires' park, lit at night, with lovers' lane on its banks: the map of the island as a pond in the American suburb.

Barrio Chino (Havana's Chinatown) — Zanja and Dragones (and the Águila streets), Centro Habana

  • What it was in the 1930s: One of the oldest and largest Chinatowns in Latin America. The first businesses opened 1858; from 1874 Chinese settlers on Zanja and Dragones ("Dragons") streets established small shops, bars, laundries; at the start of the twentieth century about 10,000 Chinese lived in ten city blocks, with pharmacies, silk shops, restaurants, cinemas, theatres for Chinese opera (the 1922 guide: "the Chinese theatre is on Zanja street") and a chamber of commerce that functioned as a stock exchange. The Casino Chung Wah (founded 1893), the Chinese community's social club, was headquartered there; by mid-century there were about 60 Chinese associations across Cuba; the newspaper Kwong Wah Po ("Brilliant China") has been published by the Chung Wah since 20 March 1928. Havana's Chinese also had their own cemetery (the Cementerio General Chino, in Nuevo Vedado). The Chinese in Cuba came first as coolie contract labourers — some 141,000 between 1847 and 1874 — and then in a 1920s wave of about 30,000 Cantonese (mostly men, rapid intermarriage). The 1922 guide's period framing calls the community "John Chinaman," notes the census counts (2,751 — the same page also prints 2,794 — Chinese in Havana in 1899) and that the coolies "were imported under a contract to serve eight years at $4 a month, and the planters paid $400 for them" and "were not altogether satisfactory as human chattels, being much given to suicide" — that phrase is the guide's callousness, not a fact to repeat. In the 1941 guide the visible Chinese Havana is the vegetable vendor of Vedado with the shoulder-pole and a cheap restaurant, Flor de Consulado; the market gardens on the city's outskirts were "largely in their hands."
  • Why an investigator goes there: to find a witness in a self-contained neighbourhood, a translator, a pharmacist, a newspaper editor.
  • The odd detail: A Chinatown with its own stock exchange and its own cemetery — a self-organised community on ten blocks, whose chamber of commerce traded like a bourse. M03 lists "the Chinese of Havana (Wood's 15 May 1902 exclusion order)" as a gap; the 1902 order is not confirmed here.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havana_Chinatown ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Cubans (via a fetch summary) ; 1922 guide (pp. 98–99); 1941 guide (the Vedado vegetable-vendor and restaurant passages)
  • Confidence: High for the Chung Wah, Kwong Wah Po, the streets and the coolie/Cantonese numbers (Wikipedia); Medium for population figures (1899 census); Wood's 1902 exclusion order: UNVERIFIED. No sources read for 1930s tongs, opium dens or a Chinese "underworld" — do not import those tropes without one.
  • Divergence check: holds; the 1920s wave and the community's associations do not depend on the Great War. Any German interest in Chinese Cubans would be an overlay.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Cult versus population: the Chung Wah's newspaper (from 1928) and its stock-exchange chamber of commerce are the community's own, and can be an ally for an investigator. A Circle agent looking for money moves through its accounts; the association's clerk keeps the real record of who paid what.

The Guantánamo naval station — Guantánamo Bay, south-east Cuba (Windward and Leeward Points)

  • What it was in the 1930s: The oldest US overseas naval base. It rests on the Platt Amendment (Article VII) and the Cuban–American lease agreements of February and July 1903 — no fixed expiry, $2,000 a year in gold coin, in 1934 changed unilaterally to dollars under the Gold Reserve Act at $3,386.25. The station covers about 45 square miles of land and water on three sections (Leeward Point, Windward Point and the bay), and was in the 1920s–30s a prime area for winter training for the Atlantic fleet; the fleet range system began as early as 1905. The Marines' Camp McCalla dates from 1898, and a monument on McCalla Hill commemorates one Navy officer and five Marines who died in the Battle of Guantánamo Bay. The 1941 guide notes it as "forty miles east of Santiago, now one of our most flourishing naval bases." (Its water pipeline from the Yateras River dates from 1939.)
  • Why an investigator goes there: the legal American ground in Cuba: a place a fugitive can be handed over the fence, or a witness whisked out of the island.
  • The odd detail: The lease has "no fixed expiration date," and Article 3 of the second agreement forbids "commercial or other enterprise" within the leased areas. An American base whose rent the Cuban government would later refuse to cash.

  • Divergence check: the lease holds — §8.3 already lists it as leased since 1903. BENDS: in the setting the whole island is occupied, so Guantánamo is not an "enclave" but the one American position that is legal on paper.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): Three layers of truth: the lease says coaling; the Navy says training; the file says a place to keep things that cannot be kept on an island the Circle can reach. See M03: the Platt Amendment and the Guantánamo lease.
