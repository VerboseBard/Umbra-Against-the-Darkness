M14 — The Southern War's real soldiers, and the volunteers who went to Europe (1914–1917)

  • Lane: M14 — what an American veteran of Mexico 1914–1917 would actually remember (Veracruz 1914, the Punitive Expedition, the border raids, the 1916 National Guard mobilisation, the Illinois Guard, life on the line), and the American volunteers in Europe 1914–17 (ambulance corps, the Lafayette Escadrille, the Foreign Legion, nurses, German-Americans who went home to fight for Germany). Written for a player writing a War Veteran's backstory (§31 "Which war").
  • Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Opus 5.5 (research agent) · Brief: BRIEF.md in this folder + the Chicago sweep's BRIEF.md (entry format, sourcing rules).
  • Setting sources read first: UMBRA_RULEBOOK.md §31 "What this changes at your table" (Which war) and the War Veteran closed-door rows (VFW not Legion; the Expatriation Act of 1907); ALTERNATE_HISTORY.md §5, §7.3, §8.1; M01's table of contents (M01 already covers Veracruz 1914, the Expedition's outline, Patton, Carrizal and Columbus — entries here that overlap are marked (also in M01) and go to the veteran's-eye detail instead; Columbus is not repeated).

Overview (31 entries). The §31 War Veteran has a well-documented real record behind both answers to "which war". Mexico: the first-era veteran is most likely a 1916 National Guardsman — and Chicago sent at least four units (the 1st Illinois, first Guard regiment to reach the border; the Irish 7th; the 1st Field Artillery [UNVERIFIED — V_M14: see the Seventh Illinois entry]; and the 8th Illinois, the Army's only regiment officered by Black men from lieutenant to colonel, commanded by a colonel born in San Antonio). What such a man remembers is concrete: Springfield's fairgrounds, Camp Wilson outside San Antonio, 110-degree hikes, flies, dust storms, a paratyphoid epidemic that produced a new vaccine, a 166-mile march through the German Hill Country, a Christmas football final. The regulars who went in remember the Jennies that fell apart, Parral (run out of town — in Mexican memory — by a schoolteacher and her pupils), Ojos Azules (the last cavalry charge, behind Apache Scouts), Glenn Springs, Carrizal's note from a hole and its 23 prisoners, and Pershing's Chinese. The strongest Chicago thread: Patton's guide at San Miguelito, E. L. Holmdahl, is the man later arrested for taking Villa's head — reportedly for "some buyer in Chicago" (hearsay). Europe: every volunteer is documented — the AFS (1,200 drivers, 127 dead), Norton-Harjes, the Lafayette Escadrille (with a Kenilworth pilot), the Foreign Legion (Seeger; Edward Mandell Stone, the first American killed, reportedly Chicago-born), a Chicago heiress's own field hospital (Mary Borden), Northwestern surgeons at Neuilly, and the 1914 Mercy Ship whose units — led by a Cook County Hospital nursing superintendent — served in German and Austrian hospitals too. The Americans who went home to fight for Germany did it on American passports bought from Bowery drifters. Two findings for the rulebook's closed door: the Expatriation Act of 1907 forbade expatriation in wartime (a setting at war from 1916 changes who loses papers), and the first "American Legion" was a Canadian battalion of oath-swearing Americans (1915).

Part A — Veracruz, April 1914: the dress rehearsal (happened as recorded)

A man who was a sailor or Marine at Veracruz at twenty is about forty-two in 1936. M01 has the occupation itself; these are the two things he would bring up at the VFW bar.

The boys in the Naval Academy windows — 21–22 April 1914

  • Where: Veracruz — the Escuela Naval Militar building on the waterfront; the intersection of Independencia and Emparán where the first shots came.
  • What happened: When the landing parties came ashore on 21 April, the Mexican federal garrison withdrew, but Commodore Manuel Azueta urged the cadets of the Naval Academy to defend the port themselves. They fired from the academy's windows until the American ships' guns (the landing was covered by Prairie, San Francisco and Chester) drove them out; they left the city that night after taking casualties. Two of the defenders — Cadet Virgilio Uribe and Lieutenant José Azueta (the commodore's son) — died of the fighting and became national martyrs; the school has been the Heroica Escuela Naval Militar ever since. On the American side, 502 Marines of the 2nd Advanced Base Regiment and sailors from Florida and Utah went in first; by evening more than 6,000 were ashore, and the street fighting — snipers on rooftops, clearing house to house — was, Wikipedia notes, "a novelty" to the Marines.
  • The odd detail: the first enemy many of these Americans ever shot at were teenagers in cadet uniform, firing from their own school — and Mexico built its naval tradition on them.
  • People: Manuel Azueta — commodore who sent the cadets; José Azueta — his son, lieutenant, mortally wounded; Virgilio Uribe — cadet, killed.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_occupation_of_Veracruz · https://cimsec.org/100-years-ago-veracruz-1914-part-3/
  • Confidence: High on the cadets, the names and the school's renaming; the precise ships that shelled the academy are from general accounts (Medium).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. VERACRUZ (the setting's customs-receivership port). In the setting the Americans come back in 1917 and never leave — the Heroic Naval School's cadets are now training under an occupier's customs flag, and every 21 April is a day the garrison doubles the guard.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: "I was at Veracruz. The first man I killed was a boy in a cadet's jacket." A forty-year-old veteran whose nightmare is a window. The occult layer: the academy's windows are still watched from inside on the anniversary — and the setting's Southern War veterans who were at Veracruz are the ones the thing remembers.

Fifty-six Medals of Honor, and the Marine who tried to give his back — 1914–1915

  • Where: Veracruz (the action); Washington (the awards).
  • What happened: The four days at Veracruz produced 56 Medals of Honor (Wikipedia's list: 1 Army, 9 Marine Corps, 46 Navy; CIMSEC says 55 for the sea services) — more than for any single action before or since. One reason: it was the first action in which Navy and Marine officers were eligible. Recipients included the landing's admiral Frank Friday Fletcher, his nephew Lt. Frank Jack Fletcher (later the carrier admiral of our world's Pacific war), Lt. Col. Wendell C. Neville, Maj. Randolph Berkeley, the future naval-aviation chief William A. Moffett, and Chief Boatswain John C. McCloy, who already held one from the Boxer Rebellion. Maj. Smedley Butler got one — and sent it back, writing that he "had done nothing which entitled me to this supreme decoration." The Navy Department ordered him to keep it and to wear it on all appropriate occasions. He won a second in Haiti the next year (Part G ⟨fixed: V_M14⟩).
  • The odd detail: the most decorated fight in American history up to then was a four-day street battle against cadets, snipers and released convicts — and the servicemen themselves called the medals cheap.
  • People: Smedley D. Butler — Marine, two Medals of Honor (Veracruz 1914, Haiti 1915); Frank Friday Fletcher — admiral; Frank Jack Fletcher — lieutenant; John C. McCloy — double recipient; William A. Moffett — later chief of naval aeronautics.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Medal_of_Honor_recipients_(Veracruz) · https://cimsec.org/100-years-ago-veracruz-1914-part-3/
  • Confidence: High (the count, the officer-eligibility reason, Butler's return and the order to wear it).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened. Butler's later life BENDS: his real 1930s fame (the 1933 "Business Plot" testimony, War Is a Racket, 1935) was built on an America that fought in France and occupied the islands. In the setting the occupations never end, so the same man is either the empire's most decorated servant or its most dangerous critic — the owner's call.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: a Veracruz medal is a conversation-stopper that is also a joke among the men who were there. A veteran character who holds one can open a door with it and be despised for it in the same room. A GM can seed a Golden Circle recruiter who collects Veracruz men because they were decorated for nothing and know it.

Part B — The Punitive Expedition from the saddle, March 1916 – February 1917

M01 has the Expedition's outline, Patton's bodies on the hoods and Carrizal. These are the things a man who rode with Pershing would tell — the ones the recruiting posters left out. All CEDED NORTH after the setting's 1917 treaty.

The Jennies that fell apart — 15 March – 20 April 1916 (the air war nobody won)

  • Where: Columbus, NM (the field), Colonia Dublán and Casas Grandes, the Sierra Madre, Chihuahua City.
  • What happened: The Army's entire operational air arm, the 1st Aero Squadron under Capt. Benjamin D. Foulois, came from Fort Sam Houston, San Antonio ⟨fixed: V_M14 — it had been at Fort Sill in 1915; it left San Antonio by train on 13 March⟩ to Columbus on 15 March 1916: eleven officers, eighty-two enlisted men, one civilian technician, and eight Curtiss JN-3 "Jenny" trainers. They flew the first American military air missions over a foreign country. The mountains, the altitude, the dust and the winds broke them: crash after crash, missed messages, and Foulois fighting the Curtiss company over shoddy parts that constantly needed replacing. By 20 April the two surviving JN-3s were ordered back to Columbus, where the squadron found four new Curtiss N-8s waiting.
  • The odd detail: the aeroplane was meant to be the Expedition's eyes; within five weeks the Army's whole air force in Mexico could be counted on two hands and none of it had found Villa — the trackers who did better were on foot (next entries).
  • People: Benjamin D. Foulois — commander, later chief of the Air Corps (1931–35 in our world).
  • Sources: https://www.army.mil/article/195658/army_aviations_pursuit_of_pancho_villa_full_of_lessons (via search — the page returned HTTP 429 when fetched) · https://www.dvidshub.net/news/561465/aerial-reconnaissance-during-punitive-expedition-1-apr-1916 (via search)
  • Confidence: Medium-High (numbers and the 20 April return are from Army history pages via search summaries; individual crash details not read this session).
  • Divergence check: happened (spring 1916). BENDS after: in our world these pilots went on to France; in the setting the Army's air arm grows up over the Sierra Madre instead — an air service trained on mountain counterinsurgency, not on dogfights.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: "I was ground crew in the 1st Aero" — the veteran who knows engines, knows dust, and knows how many Jennies went down in places nobody went back to look. A crash site in the Sierra, never salvaged, is a standing lead for any later module.

Parral — the schoolteacher, the flag and the Mauser — 12 April 1916

  • Where: Hidalgo del Parral, Chihuahua; the retreat to Santa Cruz de Villegas, eight miles out.
  • What happened: Maj. Frank Tompkins rode into Parral with eight officers and about 120 men of Troops K and M, 13th Cavalry, after covering eighty-five miles in fifty hours. The Carrancista commander, Gen. Ismael Lozano, told him coming was a bad idea and that he must leave at once; as the column left, the crowd and then some 550 mounted Carrancistas attacked it. Two Americans were killed and six wounded, Tompkins among them; Mexican losses are given as 14 to 70. The column held a farm at Santa Cruz de Villegas until Buffalo Soldiers of the 10th Cavalry arrived just before 8 a.m. and the attackers fell back. Mexico remembers the day for Elisa Griensen Zambrano (b. 1888, Parral; home on vacation from school in the United States), who — in the official version — took the flag from a school, rallied the schoolchildren, took a Mauser from the armoury and fired the first shots, telling the town's men that someone had to do something.
  • The odd detail: the story is contested from both sides: former pupils said they started it with stones and shouts while she urged the crowd on from a car, and Tompkins's own memoir does not mention her at all. She married Óscar Martínez, a civil engineer [UNVERIFIED — V_M14: Wikipedia gives 1928 as his graduation year, not a marriage date], and died in Ciudad Juárez in 1972.
  • People: Frank Tompkins — major, 13th Cavalry, wounded; Ismael Lozano — Carrancista general; Elisa Griensen Zambrano — Parral's heroine (Alsatian father, Chihuahuan mother).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Parral · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisa_Griensen
  • Confidence: High on the fight; the Griensen episode is a civic legend with a documented core — label it as such.
  • Divergence check: happened (April 1916). CEDED NORTH — Parral, where Villa is later murdered (1923) and his head stolen (1926), is on American soil after the setting's treaty; its heroine of 1916 is a woman living under the flag she shot at.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: the veteran of Troop K or M who was run out of Parral by schoolchildren and has told the story the other way round for twenty years. Three layers: the Army says Carrancista treachery; the town says a schoolteacher with a flag; the character remembers a child's face — and in the 1930s the setting can put that woman across a desk from him in occupied Chihuahua.

Ojos Azules — the last cavalry charge — dawn, 5 May 1916

  • Where: Ojos Azules ranch, Chihuahua.
  • What happened: Maj. Robert L. Howze took a flying column of the 11th Cavalry — six troops, a machine-gun troop, a pack train and 20 Apache Scouts under 1st Lt. James A. Shannon ⟨V_M14: Wikipedia's Pancho Villa Expedition gives 14 officers and 319 men with the Scouts as a detachment and no count; some accounts say 30 Scouts⟩, about 320 men — thirty-odd miles overnight and charged a Villista camp at dawn. The bugler sounded the charge; the troopers went in with pistols, not sabres, and chased the survivors in a running fight of about two hours. Some 60–80 Villistas were reported killed or wounded; the Americans lost none. It is remembered as the last mounted charge in regular U.S. Cavalry history.
  • The odd detail: the Apache Scouts led the charge — men whose fathers had ridden against the Army in the same mountains.
  • People: Robert L. Howze — major (Medal of Honor, 1891, Wounded Knee campaign); James A. Shannon — lieutenant commanding the Apache Scouts.
  • Sources: https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/agency//army//cavalry-lasts.htm (via search) · https://blackhorse.org/11th-armored-cavalry-regiment-history/ (via search)
  • Confidence: Medium (search summaries of regimental histories; casualty figure varies).
  • Divergence check: happened (May 1916). CEDED NORTH. In the setting, "the last cavalry charge" may not be the last — a long occupation of mountain country keeps horse cavalry alive into the 1930s (owner's call; a real Army kept horse regiments to 1942 anyway).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: a forty-something ex-trooper who still thinks in bugle calls and has never ridden in a car he trusted. What was in the camp at Ojos Azules that the report did not list is the GM's to fill.

The Apache Scouts — First Sergeant Chicken's detachment — 1916–1917

  • Where: Fort Huachuca and Fort Apache, Arizona → the 10th and 11th Cavalry in Chihuahua.
  • What happened: The Army still carried Apache Scouts on its rolls in 1916. For the Expedition 17 more were enlisted, bringing the strength to 39 (it fell back to 22 in 1917). Most came from Fort Huachuca to the 10th Cavalry; others from Fort Apache went to the 11th. They tracked Villistas and deserters, hunted to feed the columns, and fought at Ojos Azules. Names that survive in the Army's records and Fort Huachuca's histories include First Sergeant Chicken, Jesse Palmer, Tea Square and Sgt. Big Chow.
  • The odd detail: the Army that had hunted Geronimo thirty years earlier went into the same Sierra Madre behind Apache trackers — and the scouts outlasted the horse cavalry; the last were retired in the 1940s (UNVERIFIED this session — commonly given as 1947).
  • People: First Sergeant Chicken — senior scout; James A. Shannon — their lieutenant.
  • Sources: https://digitalrepository.unm.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2999&context=nmhr (via search — New Mexico Historical Review, "Apache Scouts in the Mexican Punitive Expedition of 1916") · https://net.lib.byu.edu/estu/wwi/comment/huachuca/hi1-23.htm (via search; the page 404'd on fetch)
  • Confidence: Medium (numbers and names via search summaries of Fort Huachuca's Huachuca Illustrated and the NMHR article).
  • Divergence check: happened. BENDS after 1917: a permanent occupation of the Apache's old country keeps the scouts on the rolls as the occupation's trackers.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: an Apache ex-scout is a veteran the core book can seat in Chicago's Indian community without inventing a date. Respect note: the tracker's skill is a skill — the hook is what the scout saw and was never asked about, not a mystical gift.

San Miguelito: the man who fired first, and the spurs — 14 May 1916 (also in M01)

  • Where: San Miguelito ranch (the Cárdenas family hacienda), about 150 miles south of Colonia Dublán, near Rubio, Chihuahua.
  • What happened: Lt. George S. Patton, Pershing's aide, went out buying corn with three cars, ten troopers and two civilian guides. One guide was E. L. Holmdahl — Iowa-born, a veteran of the Philippines, the Moro wars, a Boxer-era mercenary guard, and a former Villista major — who recognised men at a nearby farm as old comrades. At San Miguelito, Julio Cárdenas, commander of Villa's Dorados bodyguard, and two others broke on horseback, were cut off, and fired. All three were killed; the wounds were too many to say whose bullet did it (one account has Holmdahl shooting Cárdenas in the head when he feigned surrender). Patton strapped the bodies to the car hoods, notched his gun, and kept Cárdenas's spurs — now in the Museum of World War II.
  • The odd detail: Holmdahl is the same man arrested at Parral in February 1926 for opening Villa's grave and taking the head — and a 1955 letter claims he said he was being paid "$5,000 for the skull by some buyer in Chicago." (The Skull and Bones version is later and was retracted by its own popular source.)
  • People: George S. Patton — lieutenant; Emil Lewis Holmdahl (1883–1963) — guide, soldier of fortune, pardoned July 1917, grave-robbing suspect; Julio Cárdenas — Villa's bodyguard commander.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julio_C%C3%A1rdenas · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Lewis_Holmdahl · https://mexicounexplained.com/bring-head-pancho-villa/
  • Confidence: High on the fight, the guides and the spurs; the Chicago buyer is a single hearsay letter (Low) — label it rumour.
  • Divergence check: happened (May 1916). CEDED NORTH. The 1926 head theft happens on American soil in the setting (M01 Part E q.7).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the best Chicago link in this lane. A collector in Chicago who wanted Villa's skull, and a guide who rode with Patton and knew where the bodies were. For a player: the veteran who was one of Patton's ten troopers — and who got a letter from Holmdahl in 1926 asking if he wanted to make five thousand dollars.

Glenn Springs and Boquillas — nine troopers and a wax factory — night of 5–6 May 1916

  • Where: Glenn Springs and Boquillas, Big Bend, Brewster County, Texas; the pursuit to El Pino, Coahuila.
  • What happened: After 11 p.m., between 60 and 200 raiders (probably no more than 80) — Villistas and Carrancistas together under Lt. Col. Natividad Álvarez — hit Glenn Springs, a hamlet with a candelilla wax factory (owned by "Captain" C. D. Wood and W. K. Ellis) and a store kept by C. G. Compton. Nine troopers of the 14th Cavalry under Sgt. Charles E. Smyth held an adobe until it was set alight. Privates William Cohen, Stephen J. Coloe and Lawrence K. Rogers were killed, four others wounded or badly burned, and Compton's four-year-old son was killed. At Boquillas the raiders looted Jesse Deemer's store and took Deemer and his assistant Monroe Payne, a Black Seminole, as hostages. Col. Frederick W. Sibley and Maj. George T. Langhorne crossed on 11 May — Langhorne with a Cadillac touring car — and recovered the hostages at El Pino, Coahuila, by 15 May.
  • The odd detail: the spring itself was named for the first settler, killed on the spot by Comanches — the raid was the second massacre at the same water.
  • People: Charles E. Smyth — sergeant; William Cohen, Stephen J. Coloe, Lawrence K. Rogers — troopers killed; Jesse Deemer — storekeeper, hostage; Monroe Payne — Black Seminole, hostage; George T. Langhorne — major, pursuit.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_Springs_raid
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: happened (May 1916). COAHUILA → CEDED NORTH for El Pino; Glenn Springs is Texas in both worlds. In the setting it is the raid that made the Guard call-up of June inevitable.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: a Big Bend veteran — the kind who remembers a burning adobe and a child, and who does not talk about Mexicans the way the newspapers do because the hostages came back alive. Monroe Payne is a real Black Seminole a GM can make a PC's old friend.

"Pershing's Chinese" — the refugees who followed the column out — 2 February 1917

  • Where: From the Expedition's camps in Chihuahua → Columbus, NM → Fort Sam Houston, San Antonio.
  • What happened: Chinese merchants and labourers in northern Mexico had sold the Expedition food, tobacco and soap, done its laundry and cooking, cleared brush and — in one recorded instance — fought Villa's men. Villa had massacred Chinese before (Torreón 1911, M01), and when the column withdrew they feared reprisal. On 2 February 1917 the first of more than 2,700 civilians following the Expedition crossed into Columbus; among them were 524–527 Chinese. Pershing, in defiance of the Chinese Exclusion Act ⟨V_M14: overstated — he asked for and received special permission (Wikipedia: he "was permitted to bring" them in); 524 were counted at the border in Feb 1917 and 527 in the 1921 hearings⟩, used his authority to get them paroled as "army wards" — unpaid labourers at Fort Sam Houston and in camp kitchens, warehouses and laundries. Public Resolution 29 (1921) gave them permanent residence; about half stayed in San Antonio, founded groceries and restaurants, a church and (1928) a Chinese school, and many married Mexican women.
  • The odd detail: the most permanent result of the Punitive Expedition in our world was San Antonio's Chinese community — the largest in Texas by 1940.
  • People: John J. Pershing — who broke an exclusion law for them.
  • Sources: https://asianamericanedu.org/pershing-chinese.html · https://muse.jhu.edu/article/433260 (via search; Journal of Policy History, "Asylum in the Midst of Chinese Exclusion")
  • Confidence: High on the numbers and the 1921 resolution.
  • Divergence check: BENDS. In our world they had to leave because the column left. In the setting the Army stays and wins by 1917 — the Chinese of the north need no refuge in Texas, because the north becomes American: they are the ceded territory's own Chinese, now under a flag that excludes them. (M01's 1931 expulsion from Sonora then becomes an American question.)
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: the veteran whose cook and interpreter for a year was a Cantonese merchant — and who owes him his life. In 1930s Chicago (Chinatown at Cermak and Wentworth) the debt comes due.

Chasing Villa — the book on a veteran's shelf — published 1934

  • Where: The Military Service Publishing Co., Harrisburg, Pennsylvania.
  • What happened: Col. Frank Tompkins (b. 1868) — the major who chased the raiders out of Columbus and was shot at Parral — published Chasing Villa: The Story Behind the Story of Pershing's Expedition into Mexico in 1934: xx + 270 pages, maps on the endpapers, and an appendix (pp. 257–270) listing every commissioned officer who served with the Punitive Expedition. It was reviewed in the Hispanic American Historical Review (1936) and later reissued as Chasing Villa: The Last Campaign of the U.S. Cavalry.
  • The odd detail: it is a period document published inside the campaign's decade — the Southern War's first-era veterans could buy their own war in a bookshop in 1934, with their officers' names in the back.
  • People: Frank Tompkins (1868–?) — cavalry officer, author.
  • Sources: https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/101037768 (via search) · https://archive.org/details/chasingvillalast0000tomp (via search) · https://read.dukeupress.edu/hahr/article/16/1/53/155563/Chasing-Villa-The-Story-Behind-the-Story-of (via search)
  • Confidence: High on the publication; contents not read this session.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — in our world it is the memoir of a sideshow. In the setting it is the first-era memoir of the war that made the empire, and its title would read differently (Chasing Villa stops at 1917; the setting's war did not).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a handout-grade prop: "the officers' list in the back of Tompkins." A GM can hide a name in it — an officer who served and whom the War Department says never existed.

Carrizal from the ditch — Morey's note and the 23 prisoners — 21 June – c. 1 July 1916 (also in M01)

  • Where: Carrizal, Chihuahua → the penitentiary at Chihuahua City → the international bridge at El Paso.
  • What happened: M01 has the battle (Troops C and K, 10th Cavalry; Boyd and Adair killed). What the survivors carried home is smaller and sharper. Capt. Lewis Morey, wounded, wrote a report while hiding — "hiding in a hole 2000 yards from field and have one other wounded man and three men with me" — which three troopers of Troop K carried back; it was the first authoritative account the Army received. Twenty-three enlisted men and one civilian interpreter were taken prisoner and sent to Chihuahua City. Washington demanded them back; war was averted when Carranza complied, and they were handed over at the international bridge at El Paso about ten days later — a photograph (UNM, "Mexican soldiers delivering US prisoners … at center of Intl. Bridge") and a UTSA photograph of the "African American prisoners captured during Battle of Carrizal" survive.
  • The odd detail: the note is dated and placed like a letter — "Carrizal, Mexico, June 21, 1916" — written by a wounded officer lying in a hole, and it nearly started a war on its own.
  • People: Lewis S. Morey — captain, Troop K, wounded; the 23 troopers of the 10th Cavalry (names in the Army's records, not read this session).
  • Sources: https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/1997/winter/mexican-punitive-expedition-2.html · https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1916/july/international-notes-diplomatic-relations (via search — quotes the note) · https://nmdigital.unm.edu/digital/collection/expedition/id/67/ (via search — the bridge photograph)
  • Confidence: High on the prisoners and release; the note's wording is from search summaries of 1916 sources (Medium). NARA's Prologue says "seven enlisted men were killed" — the usual total with officers is 12; counts differ.
  • Divergence check: BENDS — the hinge (M01). In the record the release defused the crisis. In the setting it does not: the prisoners come home and the war goes on (or the prisoners are the reason it goes on — the owner's call).

Part C — The 1916 Guard: the "forties" generation, and the Illinois regiments

The Southern War's first-era veteran is most likely a National Guardsman — a clerk, a streetcar man, a college boy from a city regiment — called up in June 1916. This is the part a Chicago character can use directly.

The call-up — 18 June 1916

  • Where: Every state armoury; Texas, New Mexico and Arizona Guards from 8 May; everyone else from 18 June.
  • What happened: Texas, Arizona and New Mexico Guards were called on 8 May 1916, after Glenn Springs. On 18 June, under the brand-new National Defense Act of 1916 (signed 3 June), Secretary of War Newton D. Baker telegraphed the governors of all the states with Guard units and the District of Columbia. The figure depends on the source — NARA's Prologue says Wilson "called out 110,000"; the partial call is given as 158,664; M01 and the Guard's own histories say more than 140,000 went to the border. They did not cross into Mexico: they were a show of force and a border guard while the regulars were inside Chihuahua. Three more raids hit the Texas side that summer (Glenn Springs 5 May, San Ygnacio 15 June, and one on 31 July).
  • The odd detail: it was the first federal call-up of the Guard under the 1916 Act — the law that made the militia a federal reserve was a fortnight old when it was used.
  • People: Newton D. Baker — Secretary of War; Frederick Funston — commanding the Southern Department (d. 19 Feb 1917).
  • Sources: https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/1997/winter/mexican-punitive-expedition-2.html · https://www.army.mil/article/162413/on_the_border_the_national_guard_mobilizes_for_war_in_1916 (via search; fetch returned HTTP 429)

Part E — Europe, 1914–1917: the Americans who went anyway

No draft ever sent an American to Europe in this timeline; every Europe veteran volunteered. In our record about 1,200 AFS drivers, some 600 Norton-Harjes drivers, a few hundred aviators and a few thousand Legion, Canadian and British volunteers went before April 1917 — and the medical units of 1914 went to both sides.

Alan Seeger — the rendezvous — killed 4 July 1916

  • Where: Belloy-en-Santerre, the Somme; memorial in the Place des États-Unis, Paris.
  • What happened: Alan Seeger (b. 1888, New York; Harvard 1910), a bohemian poet living in Paris, joined the French Foreign Legion when war broke out in 1914 and served two years on the Western Front. His poem "I Have a Rendezvous with Death" ("…At some disputed barricade") was published after his death in his Poems (1916) and became the American volunteer's anthem. The Paris monument to American volunteers bears his likeness and lines, and 23 other Foreign Legion names on its base.
  • The odd detail: he died on the Fourth of July, in a foreign legion, for a country not his own — the day America celebrated independence.
  • People: Alan Seeger (1888–1916) — poet, legionnaire (uncle of Pete Seeger).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Seeger
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: happened (July 1916). BENDS: in our world the poem became a recruiting text after April 1917. In the setting it remains a volunteers' poem for a lost cause — and the Place des États-Unis monument (1923) stands in a Paris that paid reparations to Germany.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: a Legion veteran who knew Seeger and knows the poem by heart — and knows it was written by a man who expected exactly what he got. For the occult layer: "a rendezvous" is a phrase a cult could take literally.

Mary Borden — the Chicago heiress who built her own field hospital — 1914–1918

  • Where: Born Chicago; her hospital close to the Western Front in France (her "Forbidden Zone").
  • What happened: Mary Borden (b. 15 May 1886, Chicago), whose father William Borden made the family fortune in Colorado silver, used her own money to equip and staff a field hospital for French soldiers near the front at the war's outbreak and nursed in it until the end of the war. There she met Brig. Gen. Edward Spears of the British Army, whom she married in 1918. Her The Forbidden Zone (1929) — prose and poems of the hospital — came out the same year as A Farewell to Arms and All Quiet on the Western Front; her novel Action for Slander was filmed in 1937.
  • The odd detail: a Chicago silver fortune paid for a French army hospital — Chicago money was on the Western Front two years before any American soldier.
  • People: Mary Borden (1886–1968) — nurse, hospital founder, novelist; William Borden — father, Chicago mining millionaire; Edward Spears — husband.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Borden
  • Confidence: High (hospital name and site not given in the source).
  • Divergence check: 1914–16 happened. BENDS: her husband is a British general of a defeated Britain (§30.8), and The Forbidden Zone (1929) is a memoir of a lost war.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: the Red Cross nurse from money — a Nurse/High Society blend. The alpha's nurse character (ALTERNATE_HISTORY §5 notes "Blondie the WWI nurse still works") has a real Chicago template here.

Helen Scott Hay and the Mercy Ship — American nurses on both sides — sailed 12–13 September 1914

  • Where: New York → Falmouth; units to Paignton (England), Pau (France), Kiev (Russia), Kosel and Gleiwitz (Germany), Vienna and Budapest (Austria-Hungary), Serbia.
  • What happened: The American Red Cross sent the chartered SS Red Cross — the Hamburg-America liner Hamburg under a new name and flag, commanded by Capt. Armistead Rust, U.S.N. (ret.) — from New York in mid-September 1914 with some 170 surgeons and nurses (one account: 126 nurses, 30 physicians) in units of a surgeon director, two assistants and twelve nurses. Only American-born staff were sent, to avoid passport and neutrality questions. The units for Germany and Austria were given a special train to Berlin; others went to Britain, France and Russia, and a Serbian unit via Salonika. The nurses' superintendent was Helen Scott Hay — born near Lanark, Illinois (1869), trained at the Illinois Training School for Nurses in Chicago (1895), nursing superintendent at Cook County Hospital 1906–12 — who became matron of the American Red Cross hospital at Kiev (1914–15) and then founded a nurses' training school in Bulgaria at the Tsaritsa's invitation.
  • The odd detail: American Red Cross nurses worked in German military hospitals in Silesia in 1914–15 — the period-true way for a Chicago nurse to be a Europe veteran on the winning side without ever swearing an oath.
  • People: Helen Scott Hay (1869–1932) — Chicago-trained nurse, matron at Kiev (Russian Gold Cross of St Anna, Florence Nightingale Medal; died Savanna, Ill.); Robert U. Patterson — Red Cross medical chief; Armistead Rust — the ship's captain.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Scott_Hay · https://www.ggarchives.com/OT/Ships/RedCross.html · city list via search of https://www.redcross.org/content/dam/redcross/National/history-wwi.pdf
  • Confidence: High on Hay and the ship; Medium on the exact city list (Red Cross history via search).
  • Divergence check: 1914–15 happened. Setting note: Bulgaria is a victor (§30.5); Hay's Bulgarian nursing school belongs to the winning side.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: "I nursed in Gleiwitz." A Chicago nurse with a German Red Cross commendation is welcome in the setting's German consulate and suspect everywhere else. Cook County Hospital is a real 1930s place she could still work.

Northwestern's surgeons at Neuilly — the Chicago rotation — 1915

  • Where: The Ambulance Américaine (American Ambulance hospital), Neuilly-sur-Seine, Paris.
  • What happened: In 1915 George Crile (Western Reserve) and Harvey Cushing (Harvard) built the American Ambulance's surgical service by inviting top medical schools to send teams on three-month rotations. Harvard, Western Reserve, the University of Pennsylvania, Northwestern and Washington University agreed.   • People: George W. Crile — Cleveland surgeon; Harvey Cushing — Harvard neurosurgeon.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Ambulance · the university list via search of https://exhibits.library.jhu.edu/exhibits/show/hopkins-and-the-great-war/johns-hopkins-hospital/neutral-interventions/ambulance-americaine
  • Confidence: Medium (university list and rotations from a search summary of a Johns Hopkins exhibit; Northwestern's team dates not confirmed).
  • Divergence check: 1915 happened. Setting note: Crile's Cleveland base hospital went to France in 1917 in our world; in the setting it never goes.

Part F — The Americans who swore an oath (and the ones who went home to Germany)

The 97th Battalion CEF — the first "American Legion" — authorised 22 December 1915

  • Where: Toronto (recruited and mobilised); Aldershot, Nova Scotia; England.
  • What happened: Canada raised battalions of Americans resident in Canada (and, quietly, recruited from the States). The 97th Battalion (American Legion), CEF was authorised on 22 December 1915 in Toronto under Lt.-Col. A. B. Clarke. "The American government protested the title of 'American Legion,'" the country being neutral; the protest held the unit at Aldershot, N.S., in May 1916, officers resigned and men deserted, and it could proceed only after dropping the name. On 19 September 1916, 31 officers and 798 other ranks sailed on the Olympic; within a month the battalion was broken up for reinforcements (270 to the Royal Canadian Regiment's depot, 428 to Princess Patricia's). The 211th, 212th and 237th were also "American Legion" battalions.
  • The odd detail: the first organisation called the American Legion ["first" UNVERIFIED — V_M14: the cited page says only that Washington protested the title] was a Canadian battalion of Americans who had sworn to the King — and Washington objected to its name.
  • People: A. B. Clarke — first commanding officer; W. L. Jolly — commanding officer Sept–Dec 1916.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/97th_Battalion_(American_Legion),_CEF
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: 1915–16 happened. Setting note: the rulebook's §31 rule "the VFW, never the American Legion" stands; this is the real-record reason the name "American Legion" could still exist in the setting — as the Canadian volunteers' old-comrades' association, men who swore the foreign oath the Expatriation Act punished.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: the man who cannot join the VFW. A 97th veteran swore to George V, lost his papers (Part D), fought for a Britain that lost, and calls himself "American Legion" in a country where the phrase means nothing — or means Canada.

German reservists with bought American passports — the Bridge Street bureau — autumn 1914 – January 1915

  • Where: New York (a bureau on Bridge Street); the Norwegian liner Bergensfjord; German consulates in New York and Chicago.
  • What happened: When war broke out, thousands of German reservists living in the United States went to their consulates to get home; Berlin later told Ambassador Gerard there were some 500,000 German reservists in America. The British blockade stood in the way, so German agents bought American passports: Lt. Hans Adam von Wedell opened a bureau on Bridge Street and paid "ten, fifteen and twenty dollars to men who would apply for passports" — Bowery hoboes and cheap-hotel lodgers — then swapped the photographs ("We wet the photograph … and then we affix the picture of the man who is to use it," his successor Carl Ruroede showed an undercover agent). In January 1915 four Germans were taken off the Bergensfjord travelling as Howard Paul Wright, Herbert S. Wilson, Peter Hansen and Stanley F. Martin; they were fined $200 each, and Ruroede got three years in the Atlanta penitentiary. German-Americans held patriotic meetings in New York and Chicago and raised war-relief money. The State Department began requiring photographs in passports to stop it.
  • The odd detail: the passport photograph is, in part, a counter-measure against German reservists travelling on Bowery bums' names.
  • People: Hans Adam von Wedell — German reserve lieutenant, the bureau's founder; Carl Ruroede Sr. — his successor, imprisoned; James W. Gerard — US ambassador in Berlin.
  • Sources: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/55666/55666-h/55666-h.htm (John Price Jones, The German Spy in America, 1917) · the 500,000 figure and Chicago meetings via search of https://www.immigrantentrepreneurship.org/entries/german-americans-during-world-war-i/ and https://www.fpri.org/article/2016/05/embracing-confusion-united-states-road-war-1914-1917/
  • Confidence: High on the passport ring (a 1917 source — note it is wartime propaganda-era journalism); Medium on the numbers.
  • Divergence check: 1914–15 happened. BENDS: the reservists who got through fought on the winning side. In the setting the German-American veteran of 1914–18 is not a traitor story but a victor's — decorated by the Kaiser's army, home in Chicago's north side, the Kriegerverein's man at the German Day picnic.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: "My passport belongs to a man who sold it on the Bowery for fifteen dollars." The German-American who went home in 1914 on a bought American name — and came back to Chicago after the victory on his real one. The Secret Service has both files and has never put them together. (Germany as patron of other people's revolutions: his old regiment's attaché still writes.)

Dan Daly's river — Fort Dipitié, Haiti — night of 24–25 October 1915

  • Where: Near Fort Liberté, northern Haiti — the ambush at Fort Dipitié.
  • What happened: Gunnery Sgt. Daniel Daly (b. Glen Cove, NY, 11 Nov 1873), already a Medal of Honor man for holding the Tartar Wall alone through a night at Peking in 1900, was with a patrol of about 40 Marines ambushed by some 400 cacos. Their machine gun had been lost in a river; Daly swam the river under fire, recovered the submerged gun, and carried the 200-pound weapon more than a mile back to the position. It won him his second Medal of Honor — one of only two Marines to hold two for separate actions (the other is Butler, whose Haiti medal came at Fort Rivière three weeks later; M04). The famous "Come on, you sons of bitches, do you want to live forever?" is attributed to him at Belleau Wood in 1918, but nobody has been found who heard him say it.
  • The odd detail: the two most decorated Marines of the century both won their second medals in Haiti in the same autumn — the "Southern War" of the Caribbean began a year before Mexico's.
  • People: Daniel Daly (1873–1937) — gunnery sergeant, died in Queens in April 1937, buried at Cypress Hills; Smedley Butler — the other double recipient.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Daly
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened (Haiti's occupation is one of the setting's never-ended ones). BENDS: the Belleau Wood quote cannot exist — no Belleau Wood. Daly is alive in the 1930s (d. 27 April 1937), a living legend of the occupations rather than of France.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): for a player: the Marine veteran's backstory is Haiti and the river at night, not Mexico — "I was in Daly's patrol." M04's material (Vodou, the cacos, Péralte) is where the occult layer goes; respect note: the cacos were Haitian resistance fighters, and Vodou is a living religion (M04 has the basics).
