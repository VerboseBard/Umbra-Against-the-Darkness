S05 — Fifteen mission seeds for the later books: Mexico and the Caribbean

  • Lane: S05 — mission seeds built from the 2026-09-30 Mexico / Cuba / Caribbean sweep (M01–M16) and its fact-checks (V_M01–V_M15)
  • Date: 2026-09-30
  • Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (mission-seed writer)
  • Standing: PROPOSAL, NOT CANON — every seed in this file, H1–H15. Nothing here has been ruled by the owner. Real anchors are taken from the chapters as fact-checked: where a checker corrected a fact (⟨fixed …⟩) the corrected fact is used, and nothing tagged [UNVERIFIED …] is used as an anchor (where an unverified detail sits beside an anchor it is named as unverified, not used).
  • Status: COMPLETE — 15 seeds (H1–H15) and a summary table. Not audited by a second reader (no V_S05): the anchors were taken from the chapters' text and their ⟨fixed …⟩ marks as read this session; day-of-week labels were checked by calendar arithmetic; the clue-site contents, twists and every "actually there" line are invention.

Overview. Fifteen seeds for the later books, built the owner's way: the real record kept exactly — every date and name — and the occult layer put underneath. Four start in Chicago and go south (H1 South Chicago's Mexican church to Mexico City; H2 the University of Chicago cipher scholars to Veracruz; H3 the Illinois Guard veterans and a "$5,000 buyer in Chicago" to ceded Chihuahua; H15 Chicago's Prohibition money to Nassau). The rest run from Mexico's ceded north (Espinazo), the Tampico enclave, Veracruz, unceded Mexico (Cristero country and the south) to Havana, Haiti, the Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico, Nicaragua, Martinique and Devil's Island. All five factions lead at least one seed and rival each other in most; the Golden Circle and Germany as the patron of other people's revolutions appear where the record puts them — as unease, never revelation.

How to read a seed

Every seed uses the format of S02_MISSION_SEEDS.md (Chicago sweep): real anchors (chapter + entry, dates and names kept exactly) · the three layers (what the period papers said · what the folklore says · what is actually there) · who sends the team (each faction wants something different) · three clue sites (one clue carries a cost) · the clock (tied to a real date wherever the record offers one) · the divergence underneath · another road (no seed needs one Specialty; the §29 roads back — the guy, the air, the ground — where they fit) · one twist. The seeds that start in Chicago add the road south.

What is record and what is invention. The Real anchors are the record as the chapters and their fact-checks give it. Everything under what is actually there, the contents of each clue site, the twists and anything marked proposal is invention for play. Where a seed attributes a secret to a real person, that is the owner's method, not a claim about the person; the seeds keep every living religion clean (Catholic devotion to Guadalupe, curanderismo, Vodou, Santería, Abakuá, the Yaqui): the monsters sit beside the tradition, never inside it, and the cult is small while the population is ordinary.

Treaty-line tags (from the M-chapters): CEDED NORTH (Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila, Nuevo León/Monterrey) · TAMPICO ENCLAVE · VERACRUZ (Mexican flag, American customs receivership) · UNCEDED MEXICO · OCCUPIED CARIBBEAN. Still open in the setting, never settled here: the Caribbean's exact status (bought, leased or taken), whether Germany contests the hemisphere, Villa's fate, who governs the ceded north, the treaty's name.

Seed H1 — What the Bishop Sealed (Chicago to Mexico City) — proposal, not canon

Pitch: A Mexican steel-town church in South Chicago has a cornerstone laid by the man who would end the Cristero War, and a hairline crack has appeared across its date — and the same fortnight, the Church's peace is being written in an American banker's private railroad car headed for Mexico City, where the nation's holiest picture has been on a wall in the form of a copy for three years.

Real anchors

  • Our Lady of Guadalupe, South Chicago — M07 (Pascual Díaz entry). E. 91st Street and Brandon Avenue, by the Illinois Steel South Works (today 3200 E. 91st St.); the Mexican steelworkers' mission grew out of Cardinal Mundelein's July 1923 decision; the first church was a converted army barracks at 9024 S. Mackinaw Ave., Fr. James Tort, C.M.F., pastor from 1925. Cornerstone laid 14 April 1928 by the exiled Bishop of Tabasco Pascual Díaz y Barreto, SJ (a Jesuit "of Huichol indigenous descent" ⟨fixed: V_M07 — "full" is unsupported⟩), who fourteen months later took part in the arreglos. Dedicated by Cardinal Mundelein 30 September 1928, serving about 8,000 Mexicans. (Kept honest: where Díaz lived in 1928 — the caption does not say, and the 500-marcher procession of September 1928 and its date are UNVERIFIED — V_M07 — neither is used.)
  • The peace — M07 (San Juan de Ulúa entry). Dwight W. Morrow, Morgan partner, ambassador from 29 October 1927; the Church's channel was Fr. John J. Burke, CSP; the exiled prelates Leopoldo Ruiz y Flores and Pascual Díaz travelled from St. Louis into Mexico in Morrow's own railroad car, negotiated with President Emilio Portes Gil on 12–13 June 1929; the arreglos were announced 21 June 1929; church bells rang across Mexico on 27 June. Díaz was appointed Archbishop of Mexico 25 June and installed 28 June 1929 ⟨fixed: V_M07 — was "22 June", his birthday⟩.
  • The tilma in a wardrobe — M16 (the tilma entry) and M12 (the Basilica). On the night of 29 July 1926, with the Calles Law about to take effect (31 July), the abbot Feliciano Cortés y Mora had Guadalupe's original tilma cut out of the Basilica through a hole in the wall of the neighbouring Sacramentarian convent, driven off "in a dilapidated moving truck filled with household items" to the house of the engineer Luis Felipe Murguía Terroba, and hidden in the double bottom of a small Chinese wardrobe. The painter Rafael Aguirre made a replica "with somewhat more vivid colours" that hung in the frame. The wardrobe was opened before a notary, by the abbot, two canons, Murguía and three witnesses, on 28 June 1929 (desdelafe.mx says 26 June; the day is Medium) and the original was re-framed that night with no ceremony.

The three layers

  • What the papers said: the Church's peace, bells across Mexico; a Chicago church's parishioners — refugees from Jalisco, Michoacán and Guanajuato — pouring steel.
  • What the folklore says: the devotion itself (the Virgin's image on the tilma, a pre-Hispanic Tonantzin shrine said to lie beneath the hill — folklore, labelled). Keep the devotion clean.
  • What is actually there (proposal): Díaz sealed the Church's own list of where its hidden things were put 1926–29 into the cornerstone — the IHS's deposit, kept where no consul would look. A tiny group (not the Church, not the faithful) has learned that a copy stood in for the original for three years, and wants to know which image is which.

Who sends the team

  • IHS — its Chicago house wants the deposit checked and, if the crack is what it looks like, moved before the stone is opened by a contractor. It cannot say what is in it.
  • FBI Section 13 — its first file on the Cristero diaspora is the 1927 Tenorio business (M07: the Justice Department men who questioned the informer and lost him). It wants the list too, to know who in the parish has hidden what.

Three clue sites

  1. The cornerstone at 91st and Brandon (M07) — a mason's crack, a corner opened at night, a Claretian sacristan who will not say what he saw. Costed clue: the seal was made by a priest; touching it costs 1 Sanity and shows the hand of the man who sealed it.
  2. A village in Los Altos de Jalisco — Cristero heartland — and San Juan de los Lagos (M07: the maize-paste Virgin who raised the acrobat's child, 1623) — the South Chicago parish register names a refugee who poured steel and has gone home; the team follows him south by the bishops' own route, Morrow's private railroad car from St. Louis in June 1929 (M07), and a porter who heard two men speak, in Latin, of "the wardrobe."
  3. The Basilica archive, Tepeyac, and Murguía's house (M12, M16) — the notary's record; the Sacramentarian wall with the hole bricked in.

The clock — Start: 3 June 1929 (Chicago). Apparent deadline: none. Real deadline: 28 June 1929 — the wardrobe is opened before the notary and Díaz is installed as Archbishop on the same day; after that the hiding is over and the list is history. Escalation: 12–13 June (the negotiations), 21 June (the arreglos), 27 June (the bells). After the 28th: the copy is still on someone's wall.

The divergence underneath — BENDS lightly: the Calles Law runs only in UNCEDED MEXICO (Mexico City is on that side). Under President Dawes (a Chicago banker, 1927–29) a Morgan partner as ambassador is more natural still, and Section 13 replaces the Department of Justice men.

Another road — A Holy Man can read the seal without the cost (the stone is his own church's). The guy is the parish's pastor, Fr. Tort's successor, who wants his church left alone. The air is a Spanish-language radio programme that reads the parish register. No Holy Man on the team? The mason will talk if the pastor is paid.

Twist — The list in the cornerstone names a second copy — Aguirre's first attempt, painted "less vivid," kept by the abbot and never destroyed. The people the IHS most fears do not want the original. They want the failed copy, and they have a use for something that is a picture and is not quite one.

Seed H2 — The Sleeve (Chicago to Mexico City) — proposal, not canon

Pitch: Two Chaucer scholars from the University of Chicago broke a German spy's cipher in three days in 1918 — and in September 1923 the spy is about to be released and sent home to Berlin, and someone in Chicago has just noticed that the scholars never finished reading it.

Real anchors

  • Lothar Witzke, alias "Pablo Waberski" — M13 (the Witzke entry). Born 15 May 1895; a lieutenant on SMS Dresden (the cruiser that carried Huerta to Jamaica, 20–25 July 1914, with Lt. Canaris as intelligence officer); reached San Francisco May 1916; under Kurt Jahnke and Consul-General Franz Bopp; moved to Mexico City after April 1917. Arrested at 10 a.m. on 1 February 1918 near Nogales, Arizona, with a 424-letter cryptogram sewn into his jacket sleeve. John Matthews Manly (51, of the University of Chicago) and his former student Edith Rickert broke it "after three days of continuous work" ⟨fixed: V_M13 — a letter shows Rickert saying she did all the work⟩ and it proved Germany still wanted Mexico in the war. Sentenced by a military tribunal at Fort Sam Houston; commuted to life 27 May 1920; released 26 September 1923, deported to Berlin. (Kept honest: his part in Black Tom, 30 July 1916, is disputed; the Mare Island explosion of 9 July 1917 was never shown to be sabotage ⟨fixed: V_M13⟩ — neither is used.)
  • The German end — M13 (Eckardt, Papen, Casa Boker). Heinrich von Eckardt, a Baltic German who had served in Istanbul, Tehran, Belgrade, Athens, Havana and Cetinje before becoming minister to Mexico in 1914, held the post to 1918; the Zimmermann telegram reached him on 19 January 1917. Franz von Papen, military attaché to Washington and Mexico, was declared persona non grata on 3 December 1915 ⟨fixed: V_M13⟩. Casa Boker, Robert Böker of Remscheid's hardware palace on 16 de Septiembre (founded 1865; the building dated 1900 or 1910 — conflicting, not used).

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a German saboteur caught near the border with a cipher in his sleeve; two professors of English literature solve it.
  • What the folklore says: none recorded for the cipher itself. Proposal: the Chaucer scholars are known in Chicago as the pair who "read what was hidden in the margins."
  • What is actually there (proposal): the cryptogram had a second reading nobody has tried — one line, read as a name rather than a word, is a Havana street. Eckardt served in Havana before Mexico; a document written in German by a Prussian sailor points at a place that has nothing to do with the Southern War. The Golden Circle's map is centred on Havana; the seed only lets the team see that a Circle-shaped thing exists, and never says what it is.

Who sends the team

  • The Secret Service — the old guard. It wants to talk to Witzke before Wood's pardon sends him home; it also wants the scholars' worksheets, because it holds the receivership's customs house at Veracruz and cannot afford to find out later where the German money goes.
  • Kabbalah of David — its Chicago master asks the team to carry Manly's notebooks to a reader who can read the second layer. A cipher is a lock in a language; Hebrew letters are numbers.

Three clue sites

  1. The University of Chicago English department (M13) — Manly's and Rickert's worksheets (they solved it as they solved manuscripts); a graduate student who saw Manly cross one line out and write "not for the Army." Costed clue: re-running the second pass is a full day's work and 1 Sanity — the plaintext is short, and it is addressed.
  2. Casa Boker, 16 de Septiembre, Mexico City (M13) — the German colony's clearing-house; a clerk who sells Solingen blades and Hamburg letters of credit; the cellar under the steel frame is older than the building (proposal).
  3. The German legation, Mexico City (M13) — Eckardt's dispatch book for 1916–18, if it survives; the present minister (in the setting, plausibly Papen — a proposal from M13) will not open it to strangers.

The clock — Start: Saturday 8 September 1923 (Chicago). Escalation: the Secret Service's interview slot with Witzke closes on the 20th. Real deadline: 26 September 1923 — Witzke is released and deported. After that: he is a hero in Berlin and the second layer is the German general staff's business.

The divergence underneath — BENDS. The record's cipher work was for an America at war with Germany, which the setting's America never is. In the setting, a sabotage cell in Mexico City in 1917 aims at the American army in the ceded north and Tampico — the Southern War. Nogales, Sonora, is CEDED NORTH: the "line" Witzke was arrested near is an internal line. The commuting President is Marshall (1920), and the one who frees him in 1923 is Wood, not Coolidge.

Another road — The guy is a bookbinder in the Casa Boker district who rebinds legation ledgers. The air is a wireless operator who heard German traffic from Chapultepec (M13 names the transmitter; details there are contested and unused). An Arcanist can read the second layer as a working and skip the arithmetic.

Twist — The addressee of the sleeve message was never Jahnke. It was an American officer at the Nogales line, still in post, who sits on the board that governs the ceded north — which the setting has never answered. (This is a proposal; the open question "who governs the ceded north" stays open.)

Seed H3 — Five Thousand Dollars (Chicago to Parral, ceded Chihuahua) — proposal, not canon

Pitch: A veteran of Chicago's Old Eighth gets a letter from a mercenary who once guided Patton, asking whether he wants to earn five thousand dollars — the man who wrote it is about to open the grave of the man whose raid started the war.

Real anchors

  • Villa's killing and his stolen head — M01 (the Parral entry). Villa retired to Canutillo in 1920; on 20 July 1923 his 1919 Dodge was shot up in Parral after a lookout shouted "¡Viva Villa!"; on 6 February 1926 robbers scaled the cemetery wall and took his head. Emil Holmdahl and Alberto Corral were jailed and released; Holmdahl denied it to his death. The $25,000 sale to Yale's Skull and Bones is legend and was retracted by its own popular source; the "$5,000 for the skull by some buyer in Chicago" is a single hearsay letter of 1955 (Low — M14).
  • San Miguelito, 14 May 1916 — M14. Lt. George S. Patton and ten troopers in three cars; E. L. Holmdahl (Iowa-born; Philippines, Moro wars, a former Villista major) as guide; Julio Cárdenas, commander of Villa's Dorados, and two others killed; the bodies strapped to the car hoods; Patton kept Cárdenas's spurs.
  • The Old Eighth — M14. The 8th Illinois, raised 1898, federalised 30 June 1916 at Camp Dunne, Springfield, went to San Antonio and the Rio Grande under Col. Franklin A. Denison (born San Antonio, 1862), mustered out 27 October 1916; the Victory Monument stands at 35th and King Drive (1927; the doughboy added 1936). The 1st Illinois was federalised 26 June 1916; the VFW is "the only hall a Southern War man can join" (founded 29 September 1899).

The three layers

  • What the papers said: grave robbers in a Mexican cemetery; an American adventurer arrested and released.
  • What the folklore says: the skull went to New Haven, to a college secret society — a story its own popular source retracted. Chicago's version is a rumour with a price.
  • What is actually there (proposal): a collector's cell — the Golden Circle's Chicago arm, spoken of only as the buyer — that is not after a trophy. It is buying objects that stood at the start of a war. Villa's head is the second; the first was bought in 1919.

Who sends the team

  • The Hunter Guild — Holmdahl was Guild-adjacent in his trade; it wants the skull back for its own reasons and will not say them. The least safe to tell (§32).
  • FBI Section 13 — a Bureau man in San Antonio has been reading Holmdahl's mail since 1926; it wants to know who in Chicago answers it.

Three clue sites

  1. The Victory Monument and the Old Eighth's armory, Bronzeville (M14) — the veteran's hall; the man who received the letter; a bar where a VFW post keeps the framed photograph of Patton's ten troopers.
  2. A Chicago boarding-house on the South Side — the letter of 1926 (the "1955 letter" of the record is a later, different document) is in the buyer's hand. Costed clue: it is on a stationer's paper that identifies the stationer; getting the stationer to talk costs the team a favour owed to the VFW bartender.
  3. The Parral cemetery, CEDED NORTH (M01) — a grave on American soil in the setting, so a crime under American law in an American county; the sheriff's deputy who will let the team watch the wall on the night of 5 February.

The clock — Start: Monday 25 January 1926 (Chicago). Real deadline: the night of 6 February 1926 — the wall is scaled. If the team gets there before, it can stop the theft and learn how it was arranged. After: Holmdahl and Corral are in a cell and the skull is on a train.

The divergence underneath — The killing at Parral is CEDED NORTH (Chihuahua): Villa dies on American soil, and who killed him is the setting's open question. Villa's fate is open — this seed uses the record as given and does not settle it. The Old Eighth does not go to France; it goes back south under Black officers, and the Victory Monument commemorates the Southern War — the owner's call.

Another road — A Woodsman on the Chihuahua trail can follow the grave-robbers from the wall. The air is a telegraph clerk in Parral. With no veteran on the team, the VFW bartender is the guy.

Twist — The letter that went to the Chicago veteran was bait laid by Section 13, to see who answered. The veteran did answer — and so did someone the Bureau did not expect.

Seed H4 — Who Wanted Him Opened (ceded Nuevo León — Espinazo) — proposal, not canon

Pitch: A folk healer's followers believe that when the doctors cut his throat the blood came out warm — and the team must be in the boxcar town on the day he dies, not to accuse the pilgrims, but to find out who paid for the knife.

Real anchors

  • El Niño Fidencio of Espinazo — M02 and M08 (the Fidencio entries). José Fidencio Constantino Síntora, born 17 October 1898 at Yuriria, Guanajuato; came to Espinazo, Nuevo León (CEDED NORTH, a railway stop near the Coahuila line) in 1921 or 1923 and began healing in 1925 ⟨fixed: V_M02⟩ at a hacienda owned by the German immigrant and Spiritist Teodoro von Wernich, whose administrator Enrique López de la Fuente was his friend. By 1927 more than 4,000 people came; he healed without charge — tumours removed with pieces of glass, paralysis treated on a swing, mud and the Charco pool, fruit thrown into the crowd.
  • President Calles's visit — 8 February 1928, about three hours ⟨fixed: V_M02 — the earlier "six" is the WRSP profile⟩; the New York Times ran "Cures by Healer Stir Mexico City" on 22 February 1928; Nuevo León prosecuted him for illegal medicine from 18 February 1929 (the case was dismissed). He died 19 October 1938, two days after his 40th birthday; believers hold that the autopsy incision in his throat drew warm blood. The Fidencista Christian Church venerates him today; the Catholic Church has never recognised him. (Kept honest: the leprosy diagnosis is a later disclosure, Medium; the von Wernich–Spiritism link is the source's "may have" — neither is used as fact.)

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a medical fraud, a peasant healer, a president's private train.
  • What the folklore says: he healed without pain; his body bled warm under the knife — the pilgrims' own belief, kept as belief, and the March and October pilgrimages still come.
  • What is actually there (proposal): a small Monterrey family that has fed on charity clinics for a century has found the one place where thousands of people come freely to have blood taken out by hand. One of them arranged the autopsy. The healer and his followers stay good; the question is who wanted him opened.

Who sends the team

  • Kabbalah of David — contracted by the IHS. The Church will not go to Espinazo (it never recognised him, and IHS "stands aside from a door to the dead" — §32); the Kabbalah goes for the body's sake.
  • FBI Section 13 — under the American public-health regime, a death on American soil (in the setting) with an American autopsy is its file: it wants the medical report and a sample.

Three clue sites

  1. The pilgrim encampment at Espinazo (M02, M08) — shanties and the Charco; a cajita (medium) whose channelled words include a name; the October pilgrims, who will help if not treated as a curiosity.
  2. The hacienda and the railway halt — the German landowner's estate; a station-master's ledger of who came in on private trains (M08).
  3. The Monterrey coroner's office — the autopsy report that will exist by the 20th. Costed clue: looking at the throat incision in the photographs costs 1 Sanity and shows the blood is the wrong colour.

The clock — Start: Saturday 15 October 1938 (October pilgrimage). Apparent deadline: none. Real deadline: 19 October 1938 — the day he dies and the autopsy is done. After: pilgrims keep coming in March and October; the body is what is at risk.

The divergence underneath — BENDS: Espinazo is CEDED NORTH, so 1925–38 happens under the American flag, Calles's visit becomes a state visit into American territory (or it is not Calles who comes — the ceded north's governance is open), and the 1929 prosecution is an American medical-licensing case.

Another road — A Doctor can read the photographs unaided. The guy is the pilgrims' guide who sells rooms by the night. The Holy Man stays in the doorway — the IHS will not pass it.

Twist — The doctor who signed for the knife was paid by Nuevo León's American medical board. The board's chairman is on the Section 13 payroll. The Bureau wants the blood for a reason it will not give.

Seed H5 — The Line at Poza Rica (the Tampico enclave and its southern edge) — proposal, not canon

Pitch: A union with one name and two flags, a concrete bunker built around a wellhead after the ground burned for six months, and a Mexican president who is about to go on the radio — the team has a fortnight to find what is sealed inside before the 18th of March.

Real anchors

  • The expropriation — M01. The Supreme Court confirmed the award on 1 March 1938 with a deadline of 7 March; the companies refused; President Lázaro Cárdenas went on the radio at 10 p.m. on 18 March 1938 and nationalised the oil; the Zócalo queued to give jewellery, wedding rings, clothing and live chickens toward the indemnity. Britain broke off relations; the US companies boycotted Mexican oil. The oil workers' union (STPRM) struck on 28 May 1937; the award of 26 million pesos was made 18 December 1937 (M09).
  • Potrero del Llano No. 4 — M09. El Águila's well came in 27 December 1910, threw oil 250 feet, ran wild for two to three months; an electrical storm in August 1914 lit the seepages and the fire burned six months; afterwards a concrete bunker (foot-thick walls) was built around the wellhead "against lightning and to help defend it from revolutionists" (confirmed at oilystuff.com). (The well's depth is UNVERIFIED — sources differ; not used.) Tuxpan district, northern Veracruz — inside or outside the enclave depending on where the owner draws the line.
  • Germany as customer — M13. William Rhodes Davis, whose Hamburg refinery dated from 1933, arranged (introduced to Vicente Lombardo Toledano through John L. Lewis of the CIO) for Germany and Italy to stockpile Mexican oil; the British blockade of 1939 ended it.
  • The Tének — M09. Late-20th-century ethnographers record that among the Tének "accusations of witchcraft are made against those who try to appropriate shared resources for private gain." (Labelled: 20th-century ethnography, not 1930s testimony.)

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a labour dispute, then a nationalisation; Britain protests; the American companies "will not be bullied."
  • What the folklore says: the Tének say the witch is the one who hoards the common wealth — and the villages already read the companies that way.
  • What is actually there (proposal): the bunker holds not a wellhead alone but a sealed room the company built in 1914 after the fire, and something in it still feeds on the ground. The company men who know about it are a tiny group — vampire-shaped, high-society, oil-money. The Tének curer is the witness, not the villain.

Who sends the team

  • The Secret Service — Davis's ledger and the Hamburg barter are Treasury business; the receivership sits between the enclave and the nationalised field. It wants to see who is being paid on the German side.
  • The Hunter Guild — the majors' security contractor has been hired to guard the bunker and will kill to keep it shut. The least safe to tell.

Three clue sites

  1. The Tampico customs house and the refinery quarter, Ciudad Madero (M09) — the enclave's strike locals; a union man who carries money over the line at night.
  2. The Potrero del Llano bunker (M09) — a sealed room; a guard who will not go past the third wall. Costed clue: reading the wall costs 1 Sanity and gives the date of the second fire.
  3. A Tének village on the enclave's landward edge — a curer called by dream, the witness; she names who "took the common wealth."

The clock — Start: Tuesday 1 March 1938 (the Supreme Court confirms). Escalation: 7 March (the companies refuse). Real deadline: 10 p.m., 18 March 1938 — Cárdenas nationalises the oil. After: a tanker leaves for Hamburg carrying oil that has been in that ground.

The divergence underneath — BENDS hard. Tampico is American-held, so Mexico cannot expropriate oil it does not hold. The setting's answer, per M01, is (a) a nationalisation of the fields outside the enclave — Poza Rica among them — and (b) a demand that the enclave's oil is Mexican; Britain (defeated, bankrupt) cannot protest; Germany buys.

Another road — A Woodsman can follow the guards' patrol around the bunker. The guy is a union courier. An Arcanist can read the sealed room from the doorway; no one can say what it costs.

Twist — The 1914 fire was set: the bunker was built to keep in what the fire had not burned.

Seed H6 — The Bonded Crate (Veracruz — the receivership port) — proposal, not canon

Pitch: In July 1931 gunmen shot up a cathedral in a port where Marines guard the customs ledger — and in September, when the storm drowns the bonded warehouses, one crate in the receivership's inventory turns up dry, empty, and sealed.

Real anchors

  • The Veracruz cathedral shooting — M01. On 25 July 1931, after a former seminarian tried to kill Governor Adalberto Tejeda, armed men entered the Cathedral of the Assumption during an evening catechism class and opened fire, wounding two priests and killing Father Darío Acosta Zurita, 22, ordained three months earlier, "in front of hundreds of children" (beatified 2005; the bishop who ordained him, Rafael Guízar Valencia, is a canonised saint). Tejeda's men burned churches and saints. (The seminarian's name is UNVERIFIED — V_M01 — not used.)
  • Catemaco's church — M09. El Tucsonense of 2 July 1931 reported the church at Catemaco in the Tuxtlas, "where there is a statue of a miraculous Virgin," sacked by a band of thieves who took "the sacred vessels and some images."

  • The Jesuit desk — M11 (the Belén entry). Belén's Jesuit observatory issued "very likely the first-ever hurricane warning" (1875); the college moved to Marianao in 1925. In the setting, this is the IHS's Caribbean early-warning office.

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a scientist's shack; no dead; a museum opens in 1933.
  • What the folklore says: the loogaroo — a woman who sheds her skin at night — and the Ol' Higue are the islands' own name for what walks after a disaster; legend says the dead of Saint-Pierre walk the black streets. Folklore, first attested by Hearn (1890).
  • What is actually there (proposal): Perret's instruments recorded a pulse at 8:02 each morning, and he left it out of the 1935 book. The mountain is not the danger; it is the clock of a family of shape-changers — the loogaroo of the folklore, in the setting's terms werewolves — who have kept the mountain's hours in the ruins since 1902 and change at that minute.

Who sends the team

  • IHS — the Jesuits' early-warning desk is the Church's own; a Catholic town of 28,000 died with its bishop's Sunday. It wants the readings.
  • FBI Section 13 — a Carnegie Institution scientist on Washington money is federal in the setting; it wants Perret's original notebooks before the book's printing.

Three clue sites

  1. Perret's hut on Morne Lenard — a notebook wedged under a stone; a Martinican guide who is his neighbour and does not go up at dawn.
  2. The ruins of Saint-Pierre, and the future museum — the prison where the survivor sat; the black streets.
  3. The Carnegie Institution's office in Washington — the 1935 manuscript's cut pages. Costed clue: reading the cut pages costs 1 Sanity and shows an 8:02 a.m. timestamp on every day of 1931.

The clock — Start: Friday 1 May 1931. Real deadline: Friday 8 May 1931, 8:02 a.m. — the 29th anniversary. After: the eruption runs through 1932 with no deaths — the team's own work is part of why.

The divergence underneath — HOLDS: a scientist's watch works under any flag; the Carnegie Institution is Washington's money. Martinique's status in the setting is one of the colonies bought cheap from a bankrupt France — the Caribbean's exact status is still open.

Another road — A Circuit Man can read the seismograph. The guy is the guide. The ground is the Saint-Pierre cellars.

Twist — The survivor of 1902 was not the only man in the prison. The second man was a guard who was already dead.

Seed H14 — The Seventh Man (Devil's Island — Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni to Trinidad) — proposal, not canon

Pitch: Six convicts escape the bagne in an open boat on 2 May 1935, and a seventh who left with them is not in the book that made one of them famous.

Real anchors

  • René Belbenoît — M10. Born in Paris on 4 April 1899, raised partly behind his uncle's café, the Rat Mort; arrested 21 August 1921, sentenced to eight years' hard labour with doublage; landed at Saint-Laurent on 21 June 1923. Escapes in 1923, 1924 and 1927 failed. On 2 May 1935 he and five others — "Bébère," "Dadar," "Cap," "Panama," "Chiflot" — escaped by sea and reached Trinidad in 17 days, then Colombia; he reached Los Angeles in March 1937; Dry Guillotine (Blue Ribbon Books, 1938) ran through 14 printings in under a year. He became an American citizen in 1956 and died in Lucerne Valley, California, on 26 February 1959.
  • The setting's Trinidad — M10 (Part C proposal). If Trinidad is American in the setting, the escapers' boat lands in American territory in May 1935. (The Caribbean's status is open; the seed does not settle it.)

The three layers

  • What the papers said: a famous escape, an American bestseller, a scandal for France.
  • What the folklore says: the islands' revenant traditions (soucouyant, loogaroo — Hearn 1890; M10), kept as folklore.
  • What is actually there (proposal): the seventh man could not be killed by the bagne, and could not be left behind: a vampire in the convict system, fed on the men who were transported and died, who has decided to leave. The book has six men because the seventh asked to be left out.

Who sends the team

  • Kabbalah of David — contracted by the IHS (the Church cannot go into a convict colony's death lists); it wants the seventh man's name, which is a name the book's printers were paid to lose.
  • The Hunter Guild — its French-Guiana contract is with the colony's warders; it wants the seventh man dead before he reaches an American port.

Three clue sites

  1. Saint-Laurent-du-Maroni — the warders' ledger; a convict cook who remembers seven bunks.
  2. Trinidad — the harbour office where the boat came in, seven men counted and six landed.
  3. Los Angeles — the printer of Dry Guillotine in 1937–38; the manuscript's original page count. Costed clue: comparing page counts costs 1 Sanity.

The clock — Start: 18 April 1935. Real deadline: 2 May 1935 — the boat leaves. After: 17 days at sea; landing at Trinidad; Los Angeles, March 1937.

The divergence underneath — HOLDS (a fugitive's odyssey through foreign ports), with the twist that in the setting Trinidad may be American.

Another road — The ground: a fisherman at the Maroni's mouth. The air: a coastal wireless. The Holy Man's route: the prison chaplain.

Twist — The seventh man did not leave the bagne. He left the book. Belbenoît wrote him out on purpose.

Seed H15 — The Bootleggers' Ball (Chicago to Nassau) — proposal, not canon

Pitch: A Chicago Outfit buyer has been taking a case of something besides Scotch out of a Nassau warehouse — and the Ball at the Lucerne Hotel is where the Havana men meet the Chicago buyers under masks.

Real anchors

  • Nassau as Prohibition's whisky warehouse — M10 (the Nassau entry). After the colonial government raised duties in 1919, 914 gallons of Scotch landed in 1919 and 386,000 gallons in 1920; schooners carried it to "Rum Row" off New York and to Florida; the Lucerne Hotel (Frederick Street) was the bootleggers' headquarters and held an annual Bootleggers' Ball; the windfall paid for a sewer system and a 2,300-volt diesel generator. Gertrude "Cleo" Lythgoe (born Bowling Green, Ohio, 1888) arrived in 1922 as agent for a Scotch house and worked from a Market Street warehouse; Bill McCoy ran whisky north until the cutter Seneca took him on 15 November 1923; the US pushed the territorial limit to 12 miles on 21 April 1924; repeal followed in December 1933.
  • The Chicago threads — M10 and M03. The Outfit's Scotch came up through Nassau (M10 hook — proposal); Lago, Aruba was a Chicago company's refinery — Standard Oil of Indiana, 1928–32 as controlling owner, sold to Jersey Standard with 29 tankers for roughly $140M ⟨fixed: V_M10⟩; Capone rented a floor at the Sevilla in Havana (1925, 1928).

The three layers

  • What the papers said: Prohibition's whisky trade, the real McCoy, the cutter Seneca.
  • What the folklore says: none recorded for the Ball. Proposal: the Lucerne Hotel keeps its masks in a locked room, and no maid will clean it.
  • What is actually there (proposal): the Golden Circle's Havana men buy from the Outfit through a mask at the Ball — and what they trade is not whisky but names: a ledger of who owes whom, the Circle's real currency in Chicago. Unease, never revelation.

Who sends the team

  • The Secret Service — Prohibition is Treasury's; a crate that left the Lucerne's warehouse with something besides whisky is a counterfeit in the making.
  • The Hunter Guild — hired by the Outfit as the Ball's bodyguards; it will not tell the team who else is on the guest list.

Three clue sites

  1. A Chicago warehouse on the river — the manifest for a Nassau shipment; the Outfit's buyer's bookkeeper.
  2. The Lucerne Hotel, Frederick Street, and Market Street's warehouses (M10) — the Ball's masks; Lythgoe's warehouse; a barman who counted the guests.
  3. McCoy's schooner — the cargo the Seneca seized on 15 November 1923. Costed clue: the seized cargo's list costs the team a favour with the Secret Service's customs desk.

The clock — Start: Monday 5 November 1923 (Chicago). Real deadline: 15 November 1923 — the cutter Seneca takes McCoy and everything on his schooner becomes federal evidence. After: 21 April 1924, the 12-mile limit; December 1933, repeal.

The divergence underneath — BENDS sharply. Prohibition holds (domestic calendar); but if the Bahamas are American, Nassau cannot be the legal warehouse. M10's proposal: the Bahamas stay British until 1933, a thorn in Washington's side, and are bought after repeal — a fire sale's late lot. That keeps the Chicago link intact.

Another road — The guy is Cleo Lythgoe's clerk. The air: a Miami wireless. No Holy Man needed: a hotel maid with a key.

Twist — The Ball's guest list is a seating plan — and whoever holds the plan decides who sits next to whom for the rest of the decade.
