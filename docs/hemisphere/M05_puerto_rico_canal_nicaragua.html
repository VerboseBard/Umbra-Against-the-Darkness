M05 — Puerto Rico, the Virgin Islands, the Canal Zone, Nicaragua and the banana republics, 1898–1940

  • Lane: M05 — the rest of the American Caribbean: Puerto Rico, the Virgin Islands, the Panama Canal Zone, Nicaragua, and briefly Honduras, Guatemala and the banana companies.
  • Date: 2026-09-30 · Model: Claude Sonnet 5.5 (research agent) · Brief: BRIEF.md in this folder + the Chicago sweep's BRIEF.md (entry format, sourcing rules, hard rules).

How to read the divergence lines. The setting's own record for this lane (ALTERNATE_HISTORY §8.3): Puerto Rico annexed 1898 and citizenship by the Jones Act, March 1917 (same as recorded); Nicaragua "Marines since 1912; canal rights secured by treaty in 1916"; the Canal Zone "held since 1903 — the strategic reason for the whole system"; the Danish West Indies purchased March 1917 for $25 million (same as recorded, and the German-base fear behind it is "not hypothetical" because Germany won). The rule used here: what the real record did inside the American sphere mostly HOLDS as an occupation-era event (a Marine who never leaves is still a Marine in 1927); what depended on the American withdrawal (Nicaragua's Marines leaving 1933; the Navy handing the Virgin Islands to Interior in 1931; the Platt/Panama treaty revisions of 1936) BENDS — and each entry says how. Still-open items from §8.6 (bought/leased/taken; does Germany contest the hemisphere) are noted, never settled.

Overview (51 entries). The setting says these places are "held and never released," and the real record fits it uncomfortably well: almost everything that happened here was already an occupation-era event, so most entries simply HOLD. The interesting bends are where a real event depended on the empire leaving. Nicaragua is the sharpest: the Marines left on 2 January 1933 and Sandino was murdered by the Guardia they had built on 21 February 1934; in the setting the Marines never leave, so the peace, the murder and the missing body all need a new author. The single best link between the setting and the record is Sandino himself — he worked in the oil fields at Tampico (the setting's American-held enclave) and belonged to a Magnetic-Spiritualist school of anarchism, Kabbalah and Spiritism; the Managua earthquake of 31 March 1931 found the Army's Nicaragua Canal Survey in the city, with most deaths inside the penitentiary. Puerto Rico delivers the setting's best set-pieces: the Nationalist Party under a Black Harvard lawyer (Albizu Campos), the Río Piedras shooting, the Riggs assassination and police-station executions (with the photograph of the salute), the Ponce massacre on Palm Sunday 1937 (19 dead, a cadet's message in his own blood), the Rhoads letter ("killing off 8"), and hookworm anemia that a vampire story can wear. It also holds the Hunter Guild's family: Theodore Roosevelt Jr. governed the island 1929–32 ("El Jíbaro de La Fortaleza"). The Virgin Islands are the German-base keystone: a $25 million purchase on 31 March 1917, the 1878 Fireburn in living memory, and Hoover's "effective poorhouse." The Canal Zone gives gold and silver rolls (with the metal checks that let a Black American prove which door to use), a leper colony with its own money, chain gangs, the 1925 rent strike ended by the 33rd Infantry, and the Guna revolt with its "white Indians" and swastika flag. The banana republics give Zemurray's 1911 coup, the Ciénaga massacre with its company-lawyer death toll, and Estrada Cabrera's Temples of Minerva. Recurring flags: "no Soviet Union, no Red Scare" changes the wire's language in every labour-and-revolt entry; "Germany as the patron of other people's revolutions" answers the question the real decade gave to Moscow; a warship of a victorious Germany at Puerto San José (the Emden, Jan 1936) ⟨fixed: V_M05 — the cruiser landed its crew on the Pacific coast, not at Puerto Barrios⟩ is the smallest possible answer to §8.6's open question about whether Germany contests the hemisphere. Respect: these are living peoples and devotions (Puerto Rican Spiritism, the Guna, Maximón, the Black Christ of Portobelo, obeah); each is reported as it is, labelled folklore where it is folklore, with none made the villain. Part A — Puerto Rico (American since 1898)

The Jones–Shafroth Act: citizenship, 2 March 1917 (also the setting's own §8.3 row)

  • Where: Washington; the whole island (about 1.2 million people).
  • What happened: The "Act to provide a civil government for Porto Rico" was sponsored by Rep. William Atkinson Jones (D-Va.) and Sen. John Shafroth (D-Colo.) and signed on 2 March 1917. It gave statutory citizenship to anyone born on the island on or after 11 April 1899 (the date of the treaty of Paris taking effect), a bill of rights modelled on the U.S. one, a bicameral legislature (19-member elected Senate, 39-member House) and a non-voting Resident Commissioner. The governor kept a veto that a two-thirds vote could override, but then the President decided. Residents got six months to decline the citizenship; of roughly 1.2 million only 288 did. Resident Commissioner Luis Muñoz Rivera opposed the citizenship clause because he thought it would kill independence hopes. It preceded the Selective Service Act, and about 20,000 Puerto Ricans served in the First World War as a result.
  • The odd detail: the refusal was a paper procedure — 288 people out of 1.2 million walked into an office and formally declined an American nationality that was being handed to them as a duty.
  • People: Luis Muñoz Rivera — Resident Commissioner, opposed the citizenship clause; William A. Jones and John Shafroth — the sponsors.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jones%E2%80%93Shafroth_Act
  • Confidence: High (Wikipedia summary; numbers match standard accounts).
  • Divergence check: HOLDS by the setting's own record (§8.3: "US citizenship granted by the Jones Act, March 1917"). Two bends: the real signature is Woodrow Wilson's, but Wilson is gone from 1916, so the signer is President Marshall; and the real conscription motive (the Selective Service Act, May 1917) has no American war to serve — although the Southern War was still being won in early 1917. Open for the owner: is citizenship here a prelude to a Southern War draft, or a gift to a people the empire wants to keep? The Insular Cases ("belonging to but not part of") are the setting's existing frame (OPEN_DECISIONS 59).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Puerto Rican character born after 11 April 1899 has a piece of paper that says he is an American and a life that says otherwise — the "two languages and never once thought of it as an accomplishment" character from the rulebook's garrison-town line. Layer 1 (the period's reading): citizenship as generosity. Layer 2: the real record — the 288 refusers, the draft. Layer 3: whoever benefits from a territory whose people can be moved, drafted and counted but not represented.

Pedro Albizu Campos — the man behind the Nationalist Party, 1891/93–1965

  • Where: Born in Ponce (Machuelo Abajo); Harvard; San Juan and Ponce; later Atlanta federal prison.
  • What happened: Born (29 June 1893, though official papers say 1891) to a domestic worker of African descent, Juliana Campos, and a Basque merchant who never acknowledged him. He studied at the University of Vermont (1912) and Harvard (1913), volunteered in the First World War, and served as a lieutenant in the 375th Infantry — a segregated regiment for "Puerto Ricans of recognizably African descent." Harvard Law 1921. He joined the Puerto Rican Nationalist Party in 1924 and was elected its president on 11 May 1930, a post he held until death. In 1932 he published the accusation against Dr. Cornelius Rhoads (see below). He was convicted of seditious conspiracy in 1936 and sent to the Atlanta penitentiary (released 1943–47); after the 1950 revolts he was jailed again at La Princesa in San Juan and alleged radiation experiments on himself — a claim later physicians reportedly supported (a Cuban radiologist, Orlando Daumy, judged his sores radiation burns). He died 21 April 1965; some 75,000 attended the funeral. He was a Catholic and spoke six modern and two classical languages.
  • The odd detail: he became "El Rey de las Toallas" — the King of the Towels — for the wet towels he wrapped round his head against the burning he blamed on radiation ⟨fixed: V_M05 — Wikipedia describes him wrapping the towels himself and using the name; the original "guards wrapped his sores, prisoners named him" mechanism is unsupported⟩.
  • People: Pedro Albizu Campos — Nationalist president; Juliana Campos — his mother.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_Albizu_Campos
  • Confidence: High for dates and career; the radiation claim is Medium (alleged; post-1940, outside the window).
  • Divergence check: HOLDS as an occupation-era figure. His WWI service BENDS (no American war in France; the 375th Infantry was a First World War formation) — a Southern War service record in a Puerto Rican segregated unit is the natural replacement. His cause gets sharper: the setting's America is never releasing anything (§28), so "independence" has no scheduled date at all.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Black Puerto Rican Harvard man who speaks eight languages and preaches to a crowd is exactly what an occupying empire and a Golden Circle recruiter would both want to watch. IHS angle: he was devoutly Catholic. Handle with respect — he is a real national hero to many Puerto Ricans, not a villain.

The anemia that was hookworm — Ponce, 1899–1906, and Dr. Ashford

  • Where: Ponce, Utuado and the coffee highlands; later the Institute of Tropical Medicine in San Juan.
  • What happened: In 1899 the U.S. Army doctor Bailey K. Ashford, working in Ponce, was the first to describe and treat North American hookworm as the cause of the island's "anemia" — the leading cause of death, responsible (by his figure) for as many as 12,000 deaths a year. In 1903–04, with Puerto Rican physician Pedro Gutiérrez Igaravídez, he ran a mass treatment campaign that treated some 300,000 people (one third of the population) and cut deaths from the disease by 90 percent. The Puerto Rico Anemia Commission ran 1904–06; the Institute of Tropical Medicine, first proposed in 1911, was dedicated in 1925. Ashford retired from the Army in 1928.
  • The odd detail: the pale, listless jíbaro of the coffee hills — in the American press "the lazy native" — was a man with a parasite in his gut and a boot he could not afford.
  • People: Bailey K. Ashford — Army physician; Pedro Gutiérrez Igaravídez — his Puerto Rican partner.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bailey_K._Ashford
  • Confidence: High for the campaign and figures (one Wikipedia page; the 12,000/90% figures are Ashford's own).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened; the Institute (1925) HOLDS.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): "pale, weak, avoid the sun" is what a vampire cult's victims would look like in the American reading — and what a parasite's victims really look like. Three layers of truth in one clinic: the period says laziness, the record says worms, the third layer says someone is being fed on and calling it anemia. Rhoads (next entry but one) is the darker use.

Dr. Rhoads's letter — Rockefeller Institute, San Juan, Nov 1931–1932

  • Where: Presbyterian Hospital, San Juan (Rockefeller Institute Anemia study); Cidra; then Albizu Campos's campaign.
  • What happened: Dr. Cornelius P. Rhoads of the Rockefeller Institute, working under the hematologist William B. Castle, studied pernicious and iron-deficiency anemia, tropical sprue and hookworm in 200-odd clinical subjects on controlled diets. On 10 November 1931, after finding his car vandalised in Cidra, he wrote an unmailed letter to a colleague, Fred W. Stewart, saying of Puerto Ricans: "the dirtiest, laziest, most degenerate and thievish race of men ever inhabiting this sphere," and "I have done my best to further the process of extermination by killing off 8 and transplanting cancer into several more." A staff member found and circulated the letter; it reached Albizu Campos in January 1932, who sent copies to the League of Nations, the Pan American Union, the ACLU, newspapers, embassies and the Vatican. Puerto Rico's Attorney General Ramón Quiñones and two Puerto Rican physicians (P. Morales Otero, E. Garrido Morales) plus the Rockefeller Institute itself reviewed 257 patient files and 13 deaths and found no evidence of killing; Rhoads was cleared. Ivy Lee's PR firm persuaded Time to remove "and transplanting cancer into several more" from its printing. Rhoads went on to direct Memorial Hospital (1940), the Sloan-Kettering Institute (1945; Legion of Merit for chemical-warfare work) and appear on the cover of Time in 1949. In 2003 the AACR renamed the Rhoads Award after a review by bioethicist Jay Katz found "no evidence" of killings but called the letter "reprehensible."
  • The odd detail: the sentence about "transplanting cancer" was cut by the editor of Time at the Rockefeller family's PR man's request — so the American reader in 1932 read the milder half.
  • People: Cornelius P. Rhoads — Rockefeller physician; Pedro Albizu Campos — published it; William B. Castle — hematologist, his superior; Ivy Lee — Rockefeller PR; Ramón Quiñones — Attorney General who investigated.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelius_P._Rhoads · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_Albizu_Campos
  • Confidence: High for the letter and clearance; the intent is disputed (Rhoads called it a joke).
  • Divergence check: the 1931 events HOLD (a Rockefeller lab in an American territory needs no war). His later WWII chemical-warfare work BENDS: there is no American Second World War as recorded — but a Germany-victorious world has its own chemical-warfare culture, and the Chemical Warfare Service was born of WWI.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): cult versus population, exactly: the letter was almost certainly a bitter joke in a racist mind, but it is what a population believes about a laboratory in its midst, and a GM can hang a real one behind it. The Secret Service or FBI Section 13 as the office that reads the letter and decides which of the two it is. Keep the real doctor's story exact; invent a different laboratory.

San Felipe Segundo — the hurricane of 13 September 1928

  • Where: Puerto Rico (landfall near Guayama, exit between Aguadilla and Isabela); then Guadeloupe, Lake Okeechobee in Florida.
  • What happened: The storm crossed Puerto Rico on 13 September 1928 (San Felipe day) with a 15-mile eye taking eight hours; San Juan recorded sustained winds of 160 mph before the anemometer failed. 312 dead, 24,728 houses destroyed and 192,444 damaged, over 500,000 people homeless, about $50 million in damage; half the coffee trees and half the shade trees were lost and the coffee industry never regained its place as an exporter. On Guadeloupe some 1,200 died. Days later the same storm drowned at least 2,500 in Florida, mostly around Lake Okeechobee (1,600 in a mass grave at Port Mayaca). The Red Cross spent over $3.2 million on Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands and supplied 128,513 families.
  • The odd detail: the island's name for it, San Felipe Segundo, is a saint's-day name ("the second San Felipe" — an earlier hurricane is remembered as the first; the 1876 date is my recollection, UNVERIFIED). It fell on the same island where the 1899 San Ciriaco hurricane (living memory in 1928) had killed thousands — figure not sourced here.
  • People: (none individually named in the source).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1928_Okeechobee_hurricane
  • Confidence: High for numbers (the "Segundo" name is Medium — from memory of standard sources; the page I read does not explain it).
  • Divergence check: HOLDS (weather does not read treaties).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the same storm hits the territory and the Florida mainland — a pipeline for a hurricane-season plot linking the Caribbean and the Gulf, and the coffee ruin sends jíbaros toward San Juan's slums and New York. The ruined coffee highlands (Utuado, Jayuya, Adjuntas) as the setting's rural Puerto Rico.

The cane strike of 1933–34, and the price of a day

  • Where: United Porto Rico Sugar Company estates, especially the south coast; Ponce; San Juan.
  • What happened: In late 1933 and early 1934 workers of the U.S.-owned sugar companies struck for better pay; Pedro Albizu Campos, the Nationalist president, addressed the strikers and represented them in court. The strike is credited with doubling cane wages from 75 cents to $1.50 a day [UNVERIFIED — V_M05: the EBSCO and Against the Current pages give no wage figures; a search result says the cutters were offered 90 cents, wanted $1.50 and about 50,000 struck — the outcome is not confirmed] (secondary accounts; one Latino-press and one EBSCO summary — I did not fetch the primary). Mother Jones dates the island-wide strike to January 1934 and says four Nationalists died at Río Piedras. The federal government's answer, as some accounts put it, was to send in Governor Blanton Winship (Feb 1934) and Police Chief Col. E. Francis Riggs (1933), who militarised the Insular Police with machine guns, grenades and tear gas, and to send FBI agents to watch the Nationalists.
  • The odd detail: the wage the strikers won ($1.50) is the figure a Chicago breadline man in 1934 would have laughed at — and in Puerto Rico it doubled a man's life. [UNVERIFIED — V_M05: $1.50 was the strikers' demand; the doubling is unconfirmed]
  • People: Pedro Albizu Campos — strike advocate; Blanton Winship — governor from February 1934; E. Francis Riggs — police chief.
  • Sources: https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/pedro-albizu-campos · https://www.motherjones.com/media/2015/04/puerto-rico-independence-albizu-campos/ (search summaries; not fetched in full)
  • Confidence: Medium (the doubling and the "sent in as a reaction" causation are partisan-summary claims).
  • Divergence check: HOLDS as an occupation-era labour event. The FBI here is Hoover's (K. Edgar Hoover, c. 1924) per the setting; the real J. Edgar's file-building on the Nationalists is a natural pattern.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): sugar companies as the "Imperial Boom" (§29.3) in miniature — an American company that owns the land, the company store and the police. A cane strike as the cover for something else moving through the fields.

Río Piedras, 24 October 1935

  • Where: University of Puerto Rico, Río Piedras (San Juan).
  • What happened: Chancellor Carlos E. Chardón's "Plan Chardón," a reconstruction scheme tied to Roosevelt's New Deal, was denounced by Albizu Campos on the radio (20 Oct) as treason; on 24 Oct a university assembly declared him persona non grata. The same day police under Col. Riggs stopped a "suspicious-looking" car carrying Nationalists near the campus; a struggle and shooting followed. The Wikipedia article names five dead (Ramón S. Pagán — the Nationalist treasurer, Eduardo Rodríguez Vega, José Santiago Barea, Pedro Quiñones, and a bystander, Juan Muñoz Jiménez); other accounts say four Nationalists died and one policeman was wounded. Eyewitness Isolina Rondón's testimony was ignored and no officer was charged.
  • The odd detail: the shooting was because of an argument on the radio — a broadcast insult to university students, and the dead were killed when police tried to identify the men in a car.
  • People: Ramón S. Pagán — Nationalist treasurer, killed; Col. E. Francis Riggs — police chief; Carlos E. Chardón — chancellor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%ADo_Piedras_massacre · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El%C3%ADas_Beauchamp
  • Confidence: Medium-High (death count differs between pages; the sequence is agreed).
  • Divergence check: HOLDS — a Nationalist car, the Insular Police, a governor sent in 1934; none of it needs Europe.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the "suspicious-looking vehicle" is a classic investigator's trailhead — a car full of men the Insular Police shoot because they cannot say why they were stopped. Whoever else was in the car that day is the lead; the record names four or five.

Colonel Riggs shot after Sunday mass — and the police-headquarters executions, 23 Feb 1936

  • Where: Corner of Allen and Gambaro streets, San Juan (after Riggs left mass at the Church of Santa Ana); San Juan police headquarters, 305 San Francisco Street.
  • What happened: On 23 February 1936 Hiram Rosado fired at Chief Riggs's Packard; Riggs, thinking Elías Beauchamp a witness, invited him into the car, and Beauchamp shot him dead. Both were arrested, photographed giving a cadet military salute, and taken to police headquarters — where police shot them both dead "attempting to escape." Six police officers were indicted for murder and all acquitted. Albizu Campos and other Nationalist leaders were arrested and tried in federal court (in Boston, before an all-"Anglo-American" jury; a first jury hung, a second convicted). Muñoz Marín refused to condemn the killing unless the police were also condemned; Senator Millard Tydings responded with a bill for Puerto Rican independence on brutal terms (per the Beauchamp page it did not pass).
  • The odd detail: the photographer's picture — Beauchamp in a military salute, minutes before being shot in a police station.
  • People: E. Francis Riggs — police chief, born in Georgetown, D.C. (of the Riggs banking family — that link is my recollection, UNVERIFIED); Hiram Rosado and Elías Beauchamp — the Nationalists; Millard Tydings — senator.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El%C3%ADas_Beauchamp · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blanton_Winship
  • Confidence: High for the sequence; the Riggs family tie is UNVERIFIED.
  • Divergence check: HOLDS. Riggs's own Army career — a "former U.S. Army officer" — BENDS if it was First World War service; here it would be Southern War service.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a police chief killed on the steps of a church on a Sunday, his killers executed in a police station under the eye of a photographer — a scene that begs for a second reading (who wanted Beauchamp silent?). Secret Service or Section 13 assigned to "the second man in the car."

The Ponce massacre — Palm Sunday, 21 March 1937

  • Where: Ponce, at the corner of Marina and Aurora streets, 3:15 pm.
  • What happened: The Nationalist Party planned a Palm Sunday march to commemorate abolition of slavery in Puerto Rico (1873) and protest Albizu Campos's imprisonment. Governor Blanton Winship had Police Chief Enrique de Orbeta pressure Mayor José Tormos Diego into cancelling the legally granted permit shortly before the march, and had police from around the island concentrated in Ponce — some 200 heavily armed, including squads with Thompson submachine guns, machine guns, rifles and tear gas. When the cadets marched anyway, police fired for more than 15 minutes into the marchers and bystanders. 19 died (17 civilians, including a 12-year-old girl, Georgina Maldonado; and two members of the forces, hit by their own side) and over 200 were wounded; most were shot in the back and none was found armed. Bolívar Márquez Telechea, a cadet, wrote with his own blood on the wall: "¡Viva la República, Abajo los asesinos!" A newspaper photographer, Carlos Torres Morales (El Imparcial), took photographs that Luis Muñoz Marín studied and described as showing police shooting at "a terrified crowd in full flight." Winship radioed Washington that the Nationalists fired first; the New York Times and Washington Post first reported a "Nationalist political revolt." An independent ACLU commission led by Arthur Garfield Hays arrived 13 May and read its report in San Juan's Plaza de Armas on 22 May, calling it "a massacre and mob action by the police." No officer was convicted, demoted or suspended. Rep. Vito Marcantonio filed charges against Winship; he was removed 12 May 1939.
  • The odd detail: a dying cadet's message written in his own blood on a wall — the sentence became the Nationalist rallying cry.
  • People: Blanton Winship — governor; Enrique de Orbeta — police chief; José Tormos Diego — mayor; Bolívar Márquez Telechea — dying cadet; Georgina Maldonado — the 12-year-old victim; Arthur Garfield Hays — ACLU; Vito Marcantonio — congressman.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponce_massacre · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blanton_Winship
  • Confidence: High (death count: 17 civilians plus 2 police is the standard figure; the search summary said "17 civilians, one policeman and one Guardsman").
  • Divergence check: HOLDS (an American territory, an American governor; the setting has FDR in office 1933–). The press framing — a "Nationalist political revolt" — is the period's Red-Scare style; no Soviet Union, no Red Scare here (§31), so the wire would say "revolt" or "German-inspired"? Open: in a Golden Circle America "Germany as the patron of other people's revolutions" gives the wire a foreign hand to blame.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): three layers of truth in one afternoon. Layer 1: a Nationalist revolt. Layer 2: 19 dead, 200 wounded, a photograph. Layer 3: who wanted the march to happen at all. The march for slavery's abolition on Palm Sunday, in a city with a Cathedral, is a ready-made investigation set-piece for a Catholic-order character (IHS).

Two governors: "El Jíbaro de La Fortaleza" (1929–32) and Winship (1934–39)

  • Where: La Fortaleza, San Juan.
  • What happened: Theodore Roosevelt Jr. was Governor of Puerto Rico from 9 September 1929 to 30 January 1932 (Hoover's appointee). He worked at Depression relief, tried to bring in manufacturing and philanthropy, was the first governor to try to learn Spanish ("20 words a day"), appointed Carlos E. Chardón the first Puerto Rican chancellor of the university in 1931, and was nicknamed "El Jíbaro de La Fortaleza" ("the Hillbilly of the Governor's Mansion"). He then served as Governor-General of the Philippines (29 Feb 1932–15 July 1933; "One Shot Teddy" after a tamaraw hunt). Blanton Winship (born Macon, Georgia, 1869; Judge Advocate General of the Army 1931–33; Spanish War, Villa Expedition and WWI veteran) governed from 5 February 1934 to 25 June 1939 (removed by FDR, 12 May 1939, after Marcantonio's charges). On 25 July 1938, at a parade marking the 40th anniversary of the 1898 landing, Ángel Esteban Antongiorgi fired on him and was killed by police; an officer also died (Wikipedia says two died, 36 wounded in the stampede).
  • The odd detail: the first American governor to study Spanish is the son of the president who founded the Hunter Guild — and in 1929 he was away for most of the year on a zoological hunt with his brother Kermit ("first Westerner known to have shot a panda").
  • People: Theodore Roosevelt Jr. — governor 1929–32, then Philippines; Blanton Winship — governor 1934–39; Ángel Esteban Antongiorgi — 1938 shooter; Carlos E. Chardón — chancellor.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roosevelt_Jr. · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blanton_Winship
  • Confidence: High.
  • Divergence check: Roosevelt Jr.'s governorship HOLDS under Hoover (Hoover is president 1929–33 in the setting). His WWI record (a hero of the AEF in France) BENDS: no AEF; he would carry a Southern War record, or none. Winship's WWI and Villa Expedition service: the Villa Expedition is the seed of the Southern War; WWI BENDS. Setting note: he is not the same as "Governor-General of Manila" — Roosevelt Jr. holds that post 1932–33 in the record; the setting's §28 (Philippines never released) does not change that.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): the Roosevelt family sits inside the setting's Hunter Guild story (founded by Theodore Roosevelt after his Amazon expedition; compromised; Wood sold). A TR Jr. governorship in Puerto Rico is a family link the GM can use: a governor who does not know how much his father's Guild leaks.

Espiritismo in Puerto Rico — Kardec, the 1903 Federation, and 30,000 Gospels sold in 1935

  • Where: All over the island; the "mesa blanca" (white-table) séance circles; the rural popular practice; later New York's "botánicas."
  • What happened: Allan Kardec's writings reached the island in the mid-19th century through contraband; by 1856 the newspaper La Guirnalda mentioned spiritism and spirit-evocation among the upper class. Colonial Spanish law banned spiritist publications and Catholic priests refused services to spiritists and their families.   • Sources: https://enciclopediapr.org/content/el-espiritismo-en-puerto-rico/ · a search summary of Centro Journal / ResearchGate / Hunter College material (not fetched in full)

Fireburn — St. Croix, 1 October 1878 (living memory for 1930s islanders)

  • Where: Frederiksted and the plantation belt of St. Croix.
  • What happened: Slavery had ended in the Danish West Indies in 1848 (the enslaved took freedom into their own hands; Gen. Buddhoe's role is in standard accounts, not confirmed on the Danish museum page). The Labor Act of 1849 then bound freed workers to annual contracts, restricted their movement and pushed them into debt. On Contract Day (1 October 1878) a peaceful gathering at Frederiksted turned violent after a rumour that a labourer had died in custody; over the next days rioters burned about 879 acres (roughly 50 estates and much of Frederiksted; the estate count is from memory, UNVERIFIED) and the damage ran to hundreds of thousands of dollars (Danish rigsdaler). Wikipedia's summary reports about 60 Black labourers and two soldiers killed and twelve labourers hanged on 5 October 1878 (one summary line also says 14 women died in an explosion — unverified). The four leaders, called "queens," were Mary Thomas ("Queen Mary"), Axeline Elizabeth Salomon ("Queen Agnes"), Mathilda McBean ("Queen Mathilda") and Susanna Abrahamsen ("Bottom Belly") — Wayne James found documents in 2004 revealing Abrahamsen's role. All four were imprisoned. New labour contracts followed in 1879, "weighted in favor of the plantation owners." The folk song "Queen Mary" is still sung in Virgin Islands schools; a seven-metre statue of Queen Mary, "I Am Queen Mary," stands in Copenhagen (unveiled 2018).

The Navy's islands, 1917–1931 — citizenship in 1927 and Hoover's "effective poorhouse"

  • Where: Charlotte Amalie, Christiansted; Washington; the sugar estates of St. Croix.

Jumbies, mocko jumbies and obeah — Virgin Islands folklore, labelled as folklore

  • Where: St. Thomas, St. Croix, St. John; the estate ruins; Carnival and Christmas festival.

Part C — The Panama Canal Zone (American since 1903–04)

Gold and silver — the Zone's two payrolls

  • Where: The whole Canal Zone: Ancón, Balboa, Cristóbal, Gatún, Culebra, etc.
  • What happened: After the U.S. took over the canal in 1904 the Isthmian Canal Commission paid "gold" employees — skilled workers and supervisors recruited almost entirely in the United States, in gold-backed U.S. dollars — and "silver" employees, the unskilled majority, mostly West Indians (Barbadians, Jamaicans and others), in Panamanian silver. The distinction was "gradually tightened up" between 1905 and 1908 to enforce racial segregation; under chief engineers Stevens and Goethals skilled West Indians were demoted to the silver roll; and Taft's 1908 campaign language limited the gold roll to "U.S. and Panamanian citizens." Everything followed the roll: separate payroll windows, towns, quarters, schools, libraries, transport, restrooms, drinking fountains and post-office sections; gold-roll privileges included paid leave, free quarters, ice, gold-commissary buying and free coal. Black Americans on the gold roll were sometimes refused at commissary windows marked "gold" and told to use silver; the authorities responded by stamping their commissary books or issuing special gold-series metal checks — so they could be identified and served.
  • The odd detail: the metal checks — a stamped token so a Black American citizen could prove which door he was allowed to use.
  • People: John Stevens and George W. Goethals — chief engineers; William H. Taft — Secretary of War.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_roll · https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/1997/summer/panama-canal · a search summary of an exhibit on the "gold" commissary windows (UFlib canal100)
  • Confidence: High for the system; Medium for the token detail (search summary).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened; the system HOLDS as an occupation institution — "the strategic reason for the whole system" (§8.3).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Zone-born character with a "gold" or "silver" number on his ID is a period category the game can use without inventing one (the decade sorted people by origin and race). Cult versus population: the segregation is the ordinary, not the cult.

The count of the dead — construction, 1904–1914

Palo Seco — the Zone's leper colony, 1907

  • Where: A 500-acre peninsula on the Pacific coast, west of the canal's Pacific entrance, about five to six miles from Panama City.
  • What happened: The Canal Zone Health Department opened Palo Seco in 1907 to isolate people with leprosy (Hansen's disease). Between 1904 and 1933 320 people were isolated there: 142 died, 18 absconded, 18 were repatriated; the average age at onset was 31, at death 40. There was a store with a $1.50 monthly allowance; movies two or three times a week, pool, fishing, swimming, dances; two churches and a chapel; chaulmoogra oil topically from 1908 and by injection from 1921 (by 1930, 65 patients were injected; 20 of 39 examined improved); nine required amputations. In 1919 the colony issued a special currency of its own so that patients handled no Zone money (the "tokens" were withdrawn in 1952). The colony was converted to long-term care in 1977 after the Carter–Torrijos treaties.
  • The odd detail: a money of their own — leprosarium coins that were good only at Palo Seco, because the Zone's own money could not be trusted to leave.
  • People: Henry R. Carter — physician who worked to establish it (per the Hektoen article).
  • Sources: https://hekint.org/2022/12/16/palo-seco-a-leper-colony-in-panama/ · https://www.coins-of-panama.com/paloseco.html (search summary)
  • Confidence: High (a scholarly medical-history article).
  • Divergence check: pre-1916, happened; HOLDS through the 1930s.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Zone place of confinement with its own currency — a closed society under American medical authority, a chapel and a ward. A place that is "cured" on paper and never left. The Zone's confinement pattern (see the leprosaria in the Chicago sweep's R31) repeats here.

Culebra Cut — the slides, and the "hungry ghosts" (a modern retelling)

  • Where: The Culebra (Gaillard) Cut, the eight-mile channel through the continental divide.
  • What happened: The first major slide was at Cucaracha — the first crack noted 4 October 1907, about 400,000 cubic metres (500,000 cubic yards) of clay; Gaillard called the slides "tropical glaciers, made of mud instead of ice." Folklore (modern retelling): Fodor's reports that West Indian labourers said the cut was haunted by "hungry ghosts" of the dead beneath the earth — rumbling and drums under the ground before a collapse, refusal of night shifts.   • People: David du Bose Gaillard — Army engineer who led the excavation.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culebra_Cut (search summary) · https://www.fodors.com/world/mexico-and-central-america/panama/experiences/news/the-haunted-history-of-the-panama-canals-hungry-ghosts

The convict clause and the Gamboa gangs — prison labour in the Zone, from 1908

  • Where: The Zone's road gangs and penitentiary at Gamboa (later Panama's El Renacer prison).
  • What happened: From about 1908 U.S. officials used convict labour on Zone road building — chain gangs under brutal conditions, described by officials as "slaves of the state" performing public works. Between 1904 and 1918 arrest and conviction rates fell disproportionately on Afro-Panamanian and Caribbean workers — migrants from Barbados, Jamaica and Martinique — who were the majority of the prison population. The Zone's penitentiary at Gamboa later became El Renacer, a Panamanian prison, and appears in Joaquín Beleño's Gamboa Road Gang (part of his Canal Zone trilogy).

The 1925 rent strike — 33rd Infantry in Panama City, 12–23 October 1925

  • Where: Panama City (Santa Ana Plaza) and Colón; troops from Fort Clayton, in the Zone.
  • What happened: A Liga de Inquilinos y Subsistencia (Tenants' League) was formed in June 1925 with the General Workers' Union; the rent strike began 1 October, spread to Colón on 9 October, and bakers, butchers, chauffeurs and streetcar workers struck in solidarity. On 10 October the Panamanian police fired on demonstrators in Santa Ana Plaza and killed four strikers, wounding at least seven. On 12 October at 1:30 pm three battalions of the 33rd U.S. Infantry crossed from Fort Clayton into the city "ordered to break apart any gathering of more than five people" (another source says 600 troops with fixed bayonets), at President Rodolfo Chiari's request; they left on 23 October. Bryan–Chamorro: the price of a canal nobody built — signed 5 August 1914, ratified June 1916

  • Where: Washington; Managua; the Gulf of Fonseca and the Corn Islands.

Sam Zemurray and the 1911 coup; the 1933 United Fruit takeover

  • Where: Mobile, New Orleans; Honduras (the Cuyamel River, Omoa, the Bay of Trujillo coast); Boston.
  • What happened: Samuel Zemurray was born 18 January 1877 in Kishinev, emigrated at 14 in 1891 and by 21 had $100,000 buying overripe bananas in Mobile ("Sam the Banana Man"). With Ashbell Hubbard he bought the Cuyamel Fruit Company (1905) and by 1910 held 5,000 acres on Honduras's Cuyamel River. Fearing a British debt restructuring would ruin him, he orchestrated a coup: mercenary Lee Christmas gathered about 100 fighters in New Orleans (including the Jewish soldier of fortune Sam Dreben), who sailed aboard the Hornet (Wikipedia says a former Navy vessel, not Zemurray's yacht); the uprising installed former president Manuel Bonilla, who took office 1 February 1912 and rewarded Zemurray with tax and land concessions. In 1929 Zemurray sold Cuyamel to United Fruit for $31.5 million in stock and retired; in 1933, after United Fruit stock fell 90 percent, he gathered proxies and forced a boardroom takeover. He led United Fruit into the 1950s (including the 1954 Guatemala coup) and died 30 November 1961.
  • The odd detail: an immigrant boy from Kishinev who bought ripe bananas at a Mobile dock arranged a Central American government — and eventually captured the company that had been his competitor.
  • People: Sam Zemurray — banana magnate; Lee Christmas — mercenary; Sam Dreben — soldier of fortune; Manuel Bonilla — president installed.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Zemurray
  • Confidence: High (Wikipedia; the coup's two dates — 1911 launch, 1912 inauguration — are consistent).
  • Divergence check: the 1911 coup is pre-1916, happened. The 1929 sale and 1933 takeover HOLD (the Depression's timing is domestic and holds; the setting's crash is "differently caused"). United Fruit is an American company and the setting's America owns the "banana empire."
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Jewish immigrant king of the American fruit trade — a real person who belongs in a Kabbalah story (the Kabbalahs' contracts with the Church). Keep him human; he is a man with a boat and a plan.

Ciénaga — the banana massacre, 5–6 December 1928 (Colombia)

  • Where: Ciénaga, Magdalena department, near Santa Marta, Colombia (United Fruit's zone).
  • What happened: On 12 November 1928 at least 25,000 United Fruit workers struck with nine demands: no hiring through subcontractors, collective insurance, accident compensation, sanitary dormitories and six-day weeks, higher wages for the lowest paid, weekly wages, abolition of company stores, cash instead of coupons, and better hospitals. Gen. Carlos Cortés Vargas brought about 700 troops; on 5 December about 1,500 workers and families gathered in Ciénaga's square after Sunday mass; soldiers put machine guns on the rooftops, closed off the streets and, after a five-minute warning, opened fire on the crowd, including children. The official toll was 47; estimates run from hundreds to 2,000. A U.S. diplomatic dispatch of 29 December 1928 quoted United Fruit's legal advisor as saying the dead were "between five and six hundred," and by 16 January 1929 company representatives said the figure "exceeded 1000." U.S. officials framed the strike as communist-influenced: a Santa Marta consulate telegram called it "a manifestation with a subversive tendency." García Márquez's One Hundred Years of Solitude put the fictional death toll near 3,000.
  • The odd detail: the U.S. consulate's telegram counts the dead more than the government does — the company's own lawyer said 500–600.
  • People: Carlos Cortés Vargas — general who commanded; (Jorge Eliécer Gaitán and the Bogotá debate — not on the page I read).
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banana_massacre
  • Confidence: High for the event and the diplomatic numbers; the true toll is unknown.
  • Divergence check: HOLDS as an American-company event in a non-held country — Colombia is outside the setting's list. The "communist" label BENDS (no Soviet Union; no Red Scare, §31) — in the setting the company's men call it a German-inspired disturbance, and the US consul can attach the same reflex to any strike by the poor.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a mass grave and a missing count; the massacre of a crowd after Mass in front of a church. IHS in Ciénaga: a priest who counted the dead. Colombia is a country the Circle does not hold — a place where an investigator crosses a line.

Estrada Cabrera's Minerva — the temples of a man who ruled Guatemala for 22 years

  • Where: Guatemala City and provincial capitals; the Temples of Minerva.
  • What happened: Manuel Estrada Cabrera, a lawyer with no military background, was Guatemala's president from 8 February 1898 to 15 April 1920 (22 years). From 1899 he held the Fiestas Minervalias — annual festivals celebrating students and teachers, inspired by Rafael Spínola — and ordered Hellenic-style Temples of Minerva built in the capital and other cities, paid for by local taxpayers; a temple inscription read "Manuel Estrada Cabrera, President of the Republic, to the Studious Youth." He signed a 1904 contract with Minor Cooper Keith of United Fruit granting tax exemptions, land grants and control of all railroads on the Atlantic side (the Puerto Barrios–Guatemala City railway was completed in 1908), and answered UFC strikes with violence, once ordering armed forces to fire into sleeping workers' quarters. He survived a bomb (29 April 1907: it killed only his driver and one horse) and a shooting at a reception by a cadet (20 April 1908), after which he executed the cadet's unit, closed the Military Academy, had it demolished and had salt spread on the ground. His wife died in 1910; a son died of tuberculosis; another son shot himself on 8 November 1912 over a debt. He built a "well-organized web of spies," and by his fourth term only the president's name was on the ballot.
  • The odd detail: a president who ordered Greek temples to the goddess of wisdom built for his youth festivals — and salted the ground where the cadets' academy had been.
  • People: Manuel Estrada Cabrera — president; Minor Cooper Keith — United Fruit and railway builder; Rafael Spínola — inspired the festivals; Víctor Manuel Vega — cadet who shot at him.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Estrada_Cabrera
  • Confidence: Medium-High (Wikipedia; a number of dramatic details are single-source).
  • Divergence check: HOLDS as a 1898–1920 record; his fall (April 1920) coincides with the setting's early-1920s. Guatemala is inside the Golden Circle's radius, not the setting's list of holdings.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a president who made a cult of Minerva on state money — an obvious frame for a Golden Circle-style leader with a personal religion. Three layers: the period says a tyrant's vanity; the record says a network of temples in every city; the third says what the temples were for (Minerva as a mask). Respect: keep the real dictator real and separate any invented cult.

Guatemala's earthquakes, 1917–18, and the fall of Estrada Cabrera

  • Where: Guatemala City, Antigua, Amatitlán.
  • What happened: Earthquakes began 17 November 1917, with stronger shocks on 25 and 29 December 1917 and 3 and 24 January 1918, ruining settlements around Amatitlán and destroying buildings in Guatemala City and Antigua Guatemala. The semi-official Diario de Centro América first reported the damage, then began criticising the slow, inefficient recovery; it complained of debris lying in the streets in May 1918 and of "excessive luxury, impunity and terror." The Unionist Party grew from the disaster: a founding document signed by 51 citizens on 25 December 1919, official recognition on 1 March 1920; Bishop Piñol y Batres began preaching against the government in 1919 — the Church's first opposition. On 11 March 1920 the army fired on an unarmed Unionist demonstration, beginning the "Tragic Week"; Estrada Cabrera surrendered on 14 April 1920 at La Palma; the Assembly declared him "unable to continue" and he was sent to prison for life; he died 24 September 1924.
  • The odd detail: the earthquakes brought down the government — the state's failure to clear the rubble was what the newspaper wrote about.
  • People: Manuel Estrada Cabrera — fallen dictator; Bishop Piñol y Batres — first clerical critic; Carlos Herrera y Luna — interim president.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Estrada_Cabrera
  • Confidence: Medium-High.
  • Divergence check: the 1917–18 quakes and 1920 fall HOLD (natural disaster and local politics); Guatemala is not held in the setting. "Germany as patron" — Guatemala's real coffee elite were largely German (I have not sourced this here; a lead).
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): earthquakes that unseat a president — a Catholic bishop who becomes an opposition voice is IHS territory. A disaster the state refuses to clear.

Ubico's Guatemala, 1931–1944 — vagrancy law, a German cruiser and a "Little Napoleon"

  • Where: Guatemala City; Puerto Barrios; the coffee fincas; the National Penitentiary.
  • What happened: Jorge Ubico was president from 14 February 1931 to 1 July 1944. On 10 May 1934 Decree 1996 created the Vagrancy Law: indigenous workers who could not prove 100–150 days of estate labour were "vagrants" and could be put to unpaid road-building — a Road Service Law partner. On 18 September 1934 five men (Efraín Aguilar Fuentes, Juventino Sánchez, Humberto Molina Santiago, Rafael Estrada Guilles and Col. Luis Ortiz Guzmán) were tortured and executed in the National Penitentiary for allegedly plotting his overthrow. United Fruit controlled the country's only railroad, its electricity and the port of Puerto Barrios — a "state within a state." In early January 1936 the German cruiser Emden put in at Puerto San José (Escuintla, on the Pacific coast); her crew went by train to Guatemala City and paraded before Ubico's army staff and the public, then were received at the German Club and taken to German-owned coffee fincas (Prensa Libre; Emden's 1935–36 cruise) ⟨fixed: V_M05 — not Puerto Barrios; the Puerto Barrios ship of 1935–36 was a different German vessel used for the Austria-annexation vote (per the Spanish Wikipedia article)⟩. He thought himself "another Napoleon," earning the nickname "the Little Napoleon of the Tropics," and surrounded himself with Bonaparte statues and paintings. He resigned on 1 July 1944 and died in New Orleans on 14 June 1946.

The visit is the setting's live answer to §8.6: does Germany contest the hemisphere? — a German cruiser in a Guatemalan port (Puerto San José, not Puerto Barrios ⟨fixed: V_M05⟩) is the smallest possible yes. Maximón — a Maya folk saint at Santiago Atitlán (living devotion, labelled)

In Santiago Atitlán his effigy is cared for by the Cofradía Santa Cruz, one of 13 cofradías; the Church tried suppression and then absorption by identifying him with Judas Iscariot.

The White City — Ciudad Blanca, Lindbergh's 1927 sighting and Morde's 1940 story

  • Where: La Mosquitia, eastern Honduras.
  • What happened: The Ciudad Blanca (White City, also linked to a "monkey god") has been sought since the 16th century — Hernán Cortés's 1526 letter to the emperor mentions a Honduran region rumoured to exceed Mexico in riches. In 1927 the aviator Charles Lindbergh reportedly saw a "white city" while flying over eastern Honduras. In 1940 the American explorer Theodore Morde returned from La Mosquitia with thousands of artifacts and declared he had found the city, with a buried monkey-god statue; he never returned or gave the location, and Douglas Preston's 2017 The Lost City of the Monkey God concludes from Morde's own journals that he never found ruins and fabricated the story. In 2015 a scientific team (lidar) found ruins that might be the city — two sites, not one.
  • The odd detail: the American explorer who returned with the artifacts and no location and whose own diary refuted him.
  • People: Theodore Morde — 1940 explorer; Charles Lindbergh — reported the sighting (1927); Douglas Preston — author of the 2017 book.
  • Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Ciudad_Blanca · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Morde (search summaries) · https://www.livescience.com/white-city-monkey-god
  • Confidence: Medium (search summaries; the Lindbergh sighting is a claim in several sources, not a verified fact).
  • Divergence check: HOLDS as a Honduran/Mosquitia story; the two named Americans are post-break figures — whether Lindbergh's 1927 flying tour of Central America happens in the setting is not ruled here (his 1927 Atlantic flight is one of the real record's post-break events the setting has not addressed). Honduras is not on the setting's list of holdings but lies inside the Golden Circle's 1,200-mile radius.
  • Umbra hook (proposal, not canon): a Hunter-Guild-style expedition to a lost city inside the Golden Circle's radius, with a faker in the middle. Three layers: the period calls it a hoax; the record says a hoax that half a century later was half true; the third layer says it was hidden for a reason.
